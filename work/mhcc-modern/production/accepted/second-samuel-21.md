# 2 Samuel 21 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The Gibeonites avenged. | 1–9 |

| Battles with the Philistines. | 15–22 |

## The Gibeonites' wrong is answered (verses 1–9)

All suffering arises from the entrance of sin into the world and should lead us to humility and repentance.

Some disasters especially call earlier sins to remembrance.

God's judgments may concern wrongdoing committed a long time before.

We should not object as though we understood all the reasons behind his governance.

The people's suffering for Saul's wrongdoing may have reflected their participation in it.

Likewise, a later generation may suffer consequences arising from an earlier generation's sins.

The passage does not disclose every reason, and God has not promised to explain every aspect of his providence.

Delay does not remove guilt.

Nor can sinners assume that because judgment has been postponed, they will escape it.

A financial payment could not satisfy the demand for justice after bloodshed.

Henry understands Saul's descendants as possibly having followed his violent example, since the family is described as a **house of bloodshed**.

He argues that the Gibeonites' demand was not driven by personal vengeance but by concern for public justice.

The sentence was carried out at the beginning of harvest.

Henry views it as an extraordinary judicial action under Israel's covenant order, intended to remove the judgment that had withheld productive harvests for years.

He argues that public penalties directed toward the common welfare should not automatically be condemned as cruelty.

This is his interpretation of an ancient historical judgment, not a contemporary authorization for collective punishment or private violence.

## Rizpah honors the dead (verses 10–14)

When a guilty land has enjoyed many years of prosperity, its people should be thankful.

When abundance is misused, it is not surprising if scarcity follows.

Yet people often spend far more effort identifying physical causes of hardship than asking whether sin has contributed to their troubles.

The Lord defends the cause of those who cannot or will not avenge themselves.

The prayers of poor people are powerful.

When God sent rain upon the earth, the bodies were buried.

The rain showed that the Lord had heard prayer and granted mercy to the land.

Henry sees in these events a picture of Christ.

He was hung upon a tree and **became a curse for us**, removing our guilt though he himself was innocent.

Through him, God is reconciled to us.

## David fights the Philistines (verses 15–22)

These events appear to have occurred near the end of David's reign.

David grew exhausted but did not flee.

The Lord provided help when he needed it.

Even strong believers sometimes grow weary in spiritual conflict.

Satan may attack them most fiercely at such moments.

But those who stand firm, resist him and trust the Lord will receive help.

At last they will become more than conquerors.

**Death**, the Christian's last enemy, is like a mighty descendant of Anak.

But through the One who has already triumphed on our behalf, believers will finally overcome even death itself.
