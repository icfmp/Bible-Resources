# Luke 14 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Christ heals a man on the sabbath. | 1–6 |
| He teaches humility. | 7–14 |
| Parable of the great supper. | 15–24 |
| The necessity of consideration and self-denial. | 25–35 |

## Jesus heals on the Sabbath (verses 1–6)

The Pharisee who hosted Jesus seems to have intended harm by inviting him, as others had done.

Yet Jesus did not refuse to heal the sick man simply because opponents would object to his actions on the Sabbath.

We must carefully distinguish **the demands of piety and charity**, the work of genuine necessity and habits of selfish indulgence.

True wisdom from above teaches us to continue patiently in doing good.

## Jesus teaches humility and generosity (verses 7–14)

Jesus notices our behavior even in ordinary daily activities.

He watches us not only during worship but also around our tables.

Human pride often brings a person low, while humility prepares the way for lasting honor.

Christ teaches us that **practical charity is better than a show of importance**.

He did not mean that proud people who give generously without faith can demand a reward from God.

Rather, he calls us to help **poor and afflicted people from love for him**.

## The great banquet (verses 15–24)

This parable displays the free grace and mercy of God through Christ's gospel.

To people who recognize their spiritual poverty and misery, the gospel provides food and a feast for the soul.

The invited guests all found some excuse for staying away.

Henry applies the initial rejection to **the Jewish nation's neglect of Christ's gracious invitation**.

It also exposes the reluctance present in the human heart to accept the gospel.

People who reject the offer are ungrateful, treating the God of heaven with contempt and provoking his righteous anger.

When the invitation was refused, the apostles were sent to the **Gentiles**, and the church was filled with people from the nations.

God has not prepared the gospel's rich provision in vain.

If some refuse it, others will thankfully accept.

**The poor and socially insignificant are as welcome to Christ as the wealthy and powerful**.

Indeed, the gospel often prospers especially among those suffering poverty, disability or other worldly disadvantages.

At last Christ's house will be full, when the number of his elect people is complete.

## Count the cost of following Jesus (verses 25–35)

Not every disciple of Christ will be crucified, but **every disciple must bear a cross** while performing the duties of faith.

Jesus commands us to expect this and carefully consider it.

The illustration of **building a tower** teaches us to count the expense of our discipleship.

The illustration of **a king considering battle** teaches us to weigh its dangers.

The cost includes putting sin to death, even the sinful desires we love most.

Even the boldest sinner cannot withstand God when he judges.

Who can measure the power of his anger?

Our true interest is to **seek peace with him**.

We need not send to discover his terms of peace; he has already offered them, and they are wholly for our good.

Every believer's faith will be tested in some form.

May we be genuine disciples, not careless in our profession or frightened away by the cross.

Then we will be **good salt in the earth**, spreading something of Christ's goodness to those around us.
