## Worship, words and wandering thoughts (verses 1–3)

When approaching God in worship, take time to prepare your mind.

Guard your thoughts against wandering, and keep your affections from becoming absorbed in wrong things.

We should avoid **meaningless repetition**.

The passage does not condemn long or detailed prayers in themselves.

It condemns prayers made up of words that carry no sincere meaning.

Our thoughts wander so frequently that attendance at God's appointed worship can become little better than **the sacrifice of fools**.

Hasty and thoughtless words in prayer reveal a foolish heart, a low view of God and carelessness concerning our souls.

## Vows, fear of people and injustice (verses 4–8)

When people make promises rashly, their own words lead them into sin.

Henry describes a person coming before the priest and trying to excuse a vow by claiming it was rash or wrong to fulfill.

Such mockery of God could bring his displeasure and destroy the gain the person hoped to retain improperly.

Do not be controlled by **fear of other people**.

Keep God before your eyes.

When you see the poor oppressed, do not use that wrongdoing to accuse divine Providence of injustice.

Nor should you despise the institution of civil government merely because people corrupt its purposes.

Likewise, do not reject religion because faithful people sometimes suffer mistreatment.

Even if oppressors seem secure for a long time, **God will call everyone to account**.

## The vanity of great riches (verses 9–17)

The kindness of God's Providence is distributed more widely than a careless observer may imagine.

A king needs the same basic necessities of life as other people.

Poor people may enjoy simple food more than a king enjoys a luxurious feast.

Certain bodily needs cannot be satisfied by silver, and worldly abundance **cannot satisfy the soul's spiritual desires**.

More wealth requires a bigger household, more servants, more guests and more people depending upon it.

A laborer often sleeps well, not only because of physical tiredness, but because **there are few anxious responsibilities to interrupt the night**.

The believer who has worked diligently in God's service can also rest sweetly in him, even in **the long sleep of death**.

By contrast, people who possess almost everything may still be unable to obtain a good night's sleep.

Their abundance itself becomes the source of anxiety.

Riches can harm their owners, leading the heart away from God and duty.

Rich people may also use wealth to indulge their own sinful desires and oppress others.

At death they discover that their work was **labor for the wind**.

They cannot tell where the benefits have gone.

When worldly people lose what they coveted, they often respond not with godly repentance but by anger toward **God's Providence and everyone around them**, multiplying their misery.

## Enjoy God's gifts with grateful obedience (verses 18–20)

Life is God's gift.

We should not regard our vocation as mere drudgery but seek appropriate joy in the work God has assigned us.

A cheerful spirit is a great gift.

It makes duties easier and suffering more bearable.

Those who have used wealth rightly may remember earlier days with thankfulness and comfort.

Solomon repeatedly describes God as **the Giver of both life and its enjoyment**.

We must therefore receive his gifts and use them according to **his will and for his glory**.

Let this passage lead us to Christ's gracious command:

Do not labor only for food that perishes, but for **food enduring to eternal life**.

**Christ is the Bread of Life**, the soul's only true food.

Everyone is invited to receive this heavenly provision.
