## Gideon calms the Ephraimites (verses 1–3)

People unwilling to risk anything in God's cause may be the first to criticize those who serve with greater zeal and courage.

Those most reluctant to undertake difficult work can also be the most offended when they do not receive credit for it.

Gideon gave a remarkable example of self-denial.

He showed that **humility is the best answer to envy**.

The Ephraimites expressed their anger in excessive and improper words.

Their bitter language showed how weak their complaint really was: **when scolding grows louder, reasoning often grows weaker**.

## Gideon's exhausted men continue the pursuit (verses 4–12)

Gideon's men were **exhausted but still pursuing**.

They were weary from what they had done, yet eager to continue the campaign.

This is often the experience of a true Christian.

A believer may feel faint and yet keep striving.

The world understands little of the long struggle faithful believers maintain against the sin in their own hearts.

The strength that enabled them to begin came from God.

They must continue trusting that same divine strength, because only through him can the conflict end in victory.

## Succoth and Penuel are punished (verses 13–17)

Active servants of the Lord can meet more dangerous opposition from people who merely profess faith than from open enemies.

They must not let the conduct of **Israelites in name but Midianites in heart** discourage them.

Even when ready to faint from inward struggles and outward hardship, they should pursue the enemies of their souls and of God's work.

The Lord will give them strength to persevere.

The less help people offer—and the more they try to hinder—the more God's assistance may become evident.

The towns had disregarded Gideon's warning.

The resulting punishment was just.

Many people learn through the **thorns of affliction** because they would not learn in any gentler way.

## Gideon confronts the kings of Midian (verses 18–21)

The kings of Midian had to answer for the killing of Gideon's brothers.

They acknowledged that they had committed murder.

Gideon acted as the **avenger of blood**, being the nearest relative of those who were killed.

The kings had not expected to be confronted about these crimes so long afterward.

Yet murder seldom goes entirely unpunished, even in this life.

Sins people have forgotten must still be accounted for before God.

What poor comfort it is to hope that death may involve less pain or disgrace than someone else's death!

Many people worry more about those things than about the coming judgment and what follows it.

## Gideon refuses kingship but makes an ephod (verses 22–28)

Gideon refused the position of ruler offered by the people.

No good person should be pleased by receiving honor that belongs to God alone.

Gideon wanted the great victory to be remembered.

He therefore made an **ephod** from the finest spoils.

Henry thinks it probable that this ephod was associated with a **teraphim** and that Gideon intended it to be consulted as an oracle.

A single wrong decision by a good person can lead many others into false practices.

The ephod became a trap even to Gideon himself and eventually helped bring ruin upon his family.

How quickly possessions and ornaments that flatter desire, stir pride and encourage bodily indulgence can bring shame upon those attached to them!

## Gideon dies, and Israel forgets him (verses 29–35)

While Gideon lived, he restrained the people from abandoning the worship of Israel's God.

After he died, they felt free to follow the Baals.

They also showed no kindness to Gideon's family.

It is hardly surprising that those who forget God should also forget their friends.

We know our own failures of gratitude toward the Lord and see ingratitude all around us.

We should therefore learn to bear unkind treatment patiently, even when it follows good service.

Following God's example, let us resolve not to be overcome by evil but to **overcome evil with good**.
