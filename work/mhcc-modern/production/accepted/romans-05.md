# Romans 5 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The happy effects of justification through faith in the righteousness of Christ. | 1–5 |
| That we are reconciled by his blood. | 6–11 |
| The fall of Adam brought all mankind into sin and death. | 12–14 |
| The grace of God, through the righteousness of Christ, has more power to bring salvation, than Adam's sin had to bring misery, | 15–19 |
| as grace did superabound. | 20, 21 |

## The blessings flowing from justification (verses 1–5)

A profound change occurs in a sinner's position before God when that person becomes a true believer, whatever their past may have been.

**Justified by faith, they have peace with God**.

A holy and righteous God cannot be at peace with sinners while they remain under sin's guilt.

Justification removes that guilt and establishes peace **through our Lord Jesus Christ**, the great Peacemaker and Mediator between God and humanity.

The happy condition of believers is **a state of grace**.

We have been brought **into** this grace, showing that we were not born in this condition.

Nor could we enter it by our own abilities.

We are brought into it as **pardoned offenders**.

And we **stand** in grace: a posture indicating firmness and perseverance.

We remain secure, sustained by divine power.

The hope of sharing God's coming glory gives believers reason to rejoice even now.

Suffering produces perseverance **not by its own power**, but through **God's grace working in and through it**.

People who endure patiently often receive great comfort from God, increasing alongside their sufferings.

Trials also teach them necessary lessons about themselves.

Christian hope will not disappoint because it is sealed by **the Holy Spirit as the Spirit of love**.

The Spirit graciously pours God's love into believers' hearts.

A true knowledge of God's love enables us to remain unashamed of both **our hope and our suffering for Christ**.

## Christ's blood brings reconciliation (verses 6–11)

Christ died for sinners who were not merely useless or weak.

They were **guilty and hateful through sin**, deserving a judgment that would have displayed God's perfect justice.

Christ died **not to save us in our sins, but to save us from them**.

He died for us **while we were still sinners**.

The sinful mind is not merely at odds with God but is **hostility itself** toward him (**Romans 8:7; Colossians 1:21**).

God intended not only to rescue sinners from punishment but also to **transform them**.

While people remain in sinful rebellion, Henry describes mutual loathing between them and God (**Zechariah 11:8**).

That Christ should die for such people is an incomparable mystery of love, worthy of eternal worship and wonder.

We may imagine one person risking death for a righteous person, although Paul treats this as no more than a possibility.

But what purpose would that sacrifice serve?

It would **release the intended beneficiary from the suffering or penalty**.

Christ's death does not exempt believers from ordinary **bodily death**, which they still experience.

Therefore the evil he died to remove is more terrible than death itself: **sin and the righteous wrath due to sin**.

By grace believers are brought to repent and trust Christ, and are justified **by the price of his shed blood and by faith in that atonement**.

How much more will the living, risen Lord preserve them from final surrender to sin and Satan and from permanent departure from him!

**The risen Christ will complete the saving purpose of his dying love**.

Those trusting him not only rejoice in hope of heaven and even amid suffering, but also **rejoice in God himself** as their unchanging Friend and all-sufficient portion, through Christ alone.

## Adam's sin brought death to humanity (verses 12–14)

Paul now compares the evils resulting from the fall of **Adam, humanity's first father**, with the blessings purchased by Christ.

The purpose is to show that Christ's benefits **do more than merely remove Adam's evils**.

When Adam sinned, his nature became guilty and corrupt, and that fallen condition passed to his descendants.

**In Adam, all have sinned**.

Death entered through sin, because death is sin's wages.

The consequence included **physical, spiritual and eternal death**.

Had Adam not sinned, death would not have come upon him.

But death's sentence was passed upon him as upon a criminal, and spread through all humanity like a disease no one escapes.

The fact that sin prevailed in the world **before the Mosaic law** demonstrates humanity's solidarity with Adam's transgression.

Death ruled not only over adults who personally chose sin but also over **infants**.

Henry interprets this as evidence that Adam's descendants share the condemnation flowing from his first sin.

Adam was **a type of the One to come**, who would serve as **Surety of a new covenant** for those united to him.

## Christ's righteousness brings a greater blessing (verses 15–19)

Through one man's offense, the whole human race became exposed to condemnation.

But God's free grace, mercy, righteousness and salvation come through **Jesus Christ**, who was truly human and yet **the Lord from heaven**.

Through Christ, the multitude of believers enters **a safer and more exalted condition** than the state lost in Adam.

The free gift does not simply place them back in a new probationary trial.

Instead, it establishes them in **a justified condition**, as Adam would have enjoyed had he remained faithful.

Despite the differences, the comparison is striking.

Through **one person's offense**, sin and death prevailed to bring condemnation.

Through **one person's righteousness**, grace prevails to justify all who are united to Christ through faith.

God's gracious gift abounds through Christ to many.

Yet great numbers prefer to remain under sin and death rather than seek the blessings of grace's reign.

**Christ will never cast out anyone willing to come to him**.

## Grace exceeds the increase of sin (verses 20 and 21)

Through Christ and his righteousness, we receive **greater privileges than those lost in Adam**.

The moral law revealed the sinfulness of many thoughts, attitudes, words and actions.

In this sense transgressions multiplied.

The law did **not create more sin**; rather, it **revealed the sin already there**.

Just as bringing brighter light into a room exposes dust and filth previously hidden, God's law exposes our corruption.

The entrance of the law made Adam's offense and its consequences in our hearts appear more clearly.

The law's warnings make **the comfort of the gospel** sweeter.

Through Paul, the Holy Spirit teaches a deeply consoling truth suited to sinners everywhere.

Whatever one person's advantages compared with another's, **every human being has sinned, stands condemned by the law and needs forgiveness**.

A righteousness capable of justifying cannot be made from **a mixture of sin and holiness**.

No one has a claim to eternal reward without pure and spotless righteousness.

Let us therefore seek **the righteousness of Christ**.

- **6–11:** Christ died for **hostile sinners**, salvation **from sin and wrath rather than bodily death**, **blood and faith in atonement**, risen Lord's ongoing preservation, rejoicing **in God** — retained.
- **12–14:** **Adam's guilt/corruption**, physical/spiritual/eternal death, death before law and **infants** as Henry's support, Adam **type of Christ the Surety** — retained.
