# Genesis 10 — Modern English

## The descendants of Noah (verses 1–7)

This chapter shows how the whole earth was populated through Noah's three sons.

Henry observes that, apart from the Jewish people, no nation can know with certainty which one of these seventy ancestors it descended from.

The Jewish people's records of fathers and sons were preserved for the sake of the Messiah.

Learned writers have nevertheless made reasonably plausible arguments about which nations came from the different sons of Noah.

The islands of the Gentiles fell to the descendants of Japheth—probably including Britain.

In biblical language, places beyond the sea from Judea are called islands (Jeremiah 25:22).

The promise that the islands will wait for the Lord's law (Isaiah 42:4) points to the conversion of the Gentiles to faith in Christ.

## Nimrod, a mighty hunter, ruler, and builder (verses 8–14)

Nimrod became a powerful man in his day.

Before him, people had generally been content to live on equal terms with their neighbors. Each man ruled his own household, but no one claimed authority over other households.

Nimrod wanted to dominate his neighbors.

The spirit of the giants before the flood—those mighty and famous men (Genesis 6:4)—came to life again in him.

Nimrod was a mighty hunter.

Hunting was then a way to prevent wild animals from multiplying until they became dangerous.

It demanded courage and skill. As a result, Nimrod gained opportunities to command others and gradually gathered many people under his leadership.

It was probably through this activity that he first acquired power and began forcing others to submit to him.

He violated his neighbors' rights and property, persecuted innocent people, and sought to make everything his own through force and violence.

He continued his oppression in defiance of God himself.

Nimrod was also a powerful ruler.

Through some combination of strategy and military force, he established a monarchy that terrified the powerful and seemed likely to dominate the whole world.

He was a great builder too.

Notice what his life teaches us about ambition.

**Ambition has no limits.** The more it has, the more it wants. It is always crying, “Give me more!”

**Ambition cannot rest.** Once Nimrod ruled four cities, he was not content until he had four more.

**Ambition is costly.** Nimrod would rather bear the enormous expense of building cities than miss the honor of ruling them.

**Ambition is reckless.** It will stop at nothing.

Nimrod's name signifies rebellion. Tyrants over other people are rebels against God.

A day is coming when conquerors will no longer receive the praise they are given in the partial histories written by human beings.

In the impartial record of Scripture, they will instead be remembered with disgrace.

## Canaan's descendants and the line of Shem (verses 15–32)

Canaan's descendants were numerous and prosperous. Their land was rich and pleasantly situated.

Yet Canaan was under God's curse—and that curse was not without a cause.

Those under God's curse may prosper for a time in this world.

We cannot determine whether a person lives under God's love or his displeasure merely by looking at outward circumstances.

The decisive question concerns what is happening within.

God's curse always acts, and its consequences are terrible.

It may work secretly, affecting the soul without anything visible to others.

Or it may work slowly, without immediate consequences. But it reserves unrepentant sinners for a day of wrath.

Canaan had a more desirable land than either Shem or Japheth.

Yet Shem and Japheth had a better inheritance: the blessing of God.

Abram and his descendants, God's covenant people, came through Eber. From Eber they received the name *Hebrews*.

How much better it is to become like Eber, the father of a family of faithful and upright people, than to become the father of a family driven by the pursuit of power, worldly riches, or empty pleasures!

Goodness is true greatness.
