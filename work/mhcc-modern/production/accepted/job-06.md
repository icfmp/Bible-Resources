# Job 6 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Job justifies his complaints. | 1–7 |
| He wishes for death. | 8–13 |
| Job reproves his friends as unkind. | 14–30 |

## Job defends his complaints (verses 1–7)

Job continued defending his expressions of distress.

In addition to his outward suffering, an inward sense of divine wrath destroyed his courage and resolve.

**Feeling that God is angry with us is harder to bear than outward affliction**.

How much more must the Savior have endured in the garden and upon the cross when he bore our sins and offered his soul to divine justice for us!

Whatever suffering God permits in our health or circumstances, we can submit more readily while our judgment remains clear and our conscience at peace.

But when either sound reasoning or peace of conscience is disturbed, the sufferer's condition calls for deep sympathy.

Job rebuked his friends' accusations.

Their words brought no relief, but seemed tasteless, repulsive and burdensome.

## Job asks for death again (verses 8–13)

Job had wished for death to end his suffering.

Eliphaz rebuked that wish, but Job repeated it more forcefully.

It was rash to speak as though God should destroy him.

Who could endure even an hour of the Almighty's wrath if God unleashed its full force?

Let us rather pray with David that God would spare us a little longer.

Job took some comfort from his conscience, knowing he had in some measure served God's glory.

People who possess grace, know something of its presence and exercise it also have spiritual wisdom to help them in their darkest times.

## Job rebukes his friends' lack of compassion (verses 14–30)

During prosperity, Job expected much from his friends.

Now he was disappointed.

He compared them to **streams that dry up in summer**.

Anyone who makes created things the foundation of hope will find them fail just when help is most needed.

But those who trust God find grace in time of need (**Hebrews 4:16**).

People who make gold their hope will eventually be ashamed of that confidence.

We are wise to stop relying ultimately upon human beings.

Trust **the Rock of Ages**, not broken reeds; **the Fountain of Life**, not broken cisterns.

Job's words carried a sharp accusation: **“Now you are nothing.”**

If only we could keep the same recognition of created things' frailty that we sometimes have at a sickbed, at death's approach or under the weight of guilt!

Job reproached his friends for treating him harshly.

He asked them for neither money nor rescue, but only **a kind look and a kind word**.

We often receive less than we expect from people, even when we expect little.

From God we receive more than we expect, even when we expect much.

Job was willing to admit error if his friends could demonstrate it.

Even if he had been wrong, they should not have treated him so severely.

He held firmly to his integrity.

He knew he had not committed the extraordinary wickedness they imagined.

Yet it is best to entrust our reputations to the Lord who preserves our souls.

On the final day, every upright believer will receive God's approval.
