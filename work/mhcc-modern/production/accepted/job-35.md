# Job 35 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Elihu speaks of man's conduct. | 1–8 |
| Why those who cry out under afflictions are not regarded. | 9–13 |
| Elihu reproves Job's impatience. | 14–16 |

## Our conduct cannot enrich God (verses 1–8)

Elihu reproved Job for caring more about his own justification than God's honor.

He directed Job's attention to the heavens.

They stand far above us, and God is infinitely greater still.

Neither human sins nor our faithful service can diminish or enrich him as though he depended on us.

We have no reason to complain when God does not give everything we hoped to receive.

Instead, we should be thankful that he has already given **more good than we deserve**.

## Why some cries during suffering go unanswered (verses 9–13)

Job had wondered why God did not intervene when oppressed people cried out against their oppressors.

He found this difficult to reconcile with God's justice.

Elihu answered that people often ignore the mercies they receive **during their suffering**.

Instead of recognizing and thanking the Lord for these blessings, they focus entirely on their trouble.

God gives **songs in the night**.

When our circumstances are dark and sorrowful, his providence and promises offer enough comfort to strengthen us and even make us rejoice amid trials.

If we refuse the consolations God offers and attend only to what we have lost, we have no right to demand that he answer every prayer as we wish.

Events that kill the **body** cannot destroy the **soul**.

When God has not removed an affliction, it does not follow that his arm lacks power or his ear cannot hear.

Elihu taught that the sufferer may still need to be humbled.

This is Elihu's explanation in the present dispute, **not a license to assume that every unanswered plea for relief proves a particular sin in another sufferer**.

## Elihu corrects Job's impatience (verses 14–16)

During prosperity we may imagine our mountain will never be moved.

During suffering we may imagine the valley will never be filled.

But expecting tomorrow to resemble today is as foolish as thinking that fair or stormy weather will last forever.

Job had no reason to despair when looking to God.

A day of final judgment is coming.

Then what appears wrong will be seen in the light of divine justice, and what is dark or difficult will be understood.

Elihu saw Job's fretful complaints and distrust of Providence as sinful.

His task was to humble Job, because Job had spoken rashly about things beyond his knowledge.

When we suffer, we should not speak only of **how great our pain is**.

We should also consider and praise **how great God's mercy is**.
