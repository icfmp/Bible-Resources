# Genesis 8 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God remembers Noah and dries up the waters. | 1–3 |
| The ark rests on Ararat; Noah sends out a raven and a dove. | 4–12 |
| Noah leaves the ark at God's command. | 13–19 |
| Noah offers a sacrifice; God promises not to curse the earth again. | 20–22 |

## God remembers Noah and dries up the waters (verses 1–3)

The entire human race, apart from Noah and his family, had died. When God remembered Noah, his mercy returned to humanity. He would not bring the human race to a complete end.

Divine justice had been satisfied through the destruction of sinners.

God sent a wind to dry the earth and close up the sources of the floodwaters.

The same hand that brings devastation must also bring deliverance. Therefore we should always look to that hand.

When afflictions have accomplished the purpose for which God sent them—whether to destroy or to heal—they will be taken away.

The earth was not flooded in a single day, and it was not dried in a single day.

God ordinarily brings deliverance to his people gradually. That teaches us not to despise the day of small beginnings or despair as we wait for greater things.

## The ark comes to rest; the raven and the dove (verses 4–12)

The ark came to rest on a mountain. God's wise and gracious providence had directed it there, so that it could settle sooner.

God has appointed times and places of rest for his people after they have been tossed about.

Often he provides a timely and comfortable place for them to settle, without any planning of their own and far beyond what they could foresee.

God had told Noah when the flood would begin, but he did not reveal when or by what stages it would end.

Noah needed to know the first in order to prepare the ark. Knowing the second would merely have satisfied his curiosity.

By withholding that information, God exercised Noah's faith and patience.

Noah first sent out a raven. It flew about and fed on the carcasses floating on the waters.

Then he sent out a dove. She returned the first time without any good news.

On her second flight, however, she brought back a freshly plucked olive leaf in her beak. This plainly showed that trees, including fruit trees, were beginning to emerge from the water.

Noah sent the dove out a second time seven days after the first, and the third time another seven days later.

Perhaps these flights took place on the Sabbath. After worshipping with his little church, Noah may have looked for special blessings from heaven and sought news of them.

The dove is a picture of a soul touched by grace. Unable to find lasting peace or satisfaction in this flooded and defiling world, it returns to Christ as its ark, its Noah, and its rest.

A worldly heart, by contrast, is like the raven. It is content with the world and feeds on the carrion it finds there.

But let my soul say, “Return to your rest”—to your Noah, as the expression suggests (Psalm 116:7).

And just as Noah reached out, took the dove, and brought her into the ark, so Christ saves, helps, and welcomes those who flee to him for rest.

## Noah leaves the ark at God's command (verses 13–19)

God considers what will benefit us, not merely what we want.

He knows better than we do what is good for us, how long our restraints should continue, and when the blessings we desire should come.

We would gladly leave the ark before the ground was dry.

If the door remained shut, we might even be tempted to remove the covering and climb out another way.

But God's time for showing mercy is always best.

Just as Noah waited for God's command before entering the ark, he waited for God's command before leaving it, however tedious his confinement had become.

In everything we do, we should acknowledge God and keep him before us whenever we move or make a change.

Only those who follow God's directions and submit to him can claim to be under his protection.

## Noah worships; God promises the seasons will continue (verses 20–22)

Noah entered a devastated world.

We might have expected him to build a house for himself first. Instead, he began by building an altar to God.

Those who begin with God begin well.

Noah had only a few animals, and preserving them had required great care and labor. Yet he did not begrudge using some of them in God's service.

Serving God with the little we have is the way to receive more. Whatever honors God is never wasted.

The very first action in the new world was worship.

Today we express our thanks not through burnt offerings, but through praise, godly devotion, and godly conduct.

God was pleased with Noah's offering.

But the burning flesh itself could please him no more than the blood of bulls and goats could. Its value lay in pointing to Christ's sacrifice and expressing Noah's humble faith and devotion to God.

The flood had swept away a wicked generation, but it had not removed sin from human nature.

Since people are conceived and born in sin, they think, plan, and love evil from their youth. That was as true after the flood as it had been before.

Yet God graciously declared that he would never again drown the world.

As long as the earth remains and human beings live upon it, summer and winter will continue.

Clearly, this earth will not remain forever.

The earth and everything in it will one day be consumed by fire. We look for new heavens and a new earth when these present things have passed away.

But while this earth remains, God's providence will keep the seasons moving in their appointed order.

We can depend upon his promise.

We see that God keeps the promises he has made to his creatures. From that, we may confidently expect him to keep every promise he has made to believers.

## Chapter-level editorial audit

Compared in a separate reading pass with [CCEL's original Concise text for Genesis 8](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc/mhcc.ii.viii.html) and the CrossWire reading witness for the same chapter. The official correction manifest for `MHCC 0.1.1` does not identify an editorial-text change in Genesis 8.

- **1–3:** Noah as preservation of humanity, divine justice and mercy, God sending wind and closing the waters, both judgment and rescue under the same divine hand, removal of afflictions after their purpose and gradual deliverance—retained.
- **4–12:** providential resting-place, God's choice not to reveal the timetable, raven feeding on carrion, dove/olive leaf, repeated seven-day intervals, the *possible* Sabbath timing, dove/gracious soul and raven/worldly soul imagery, Psalm 116:7 and Christ receiving those who flee to him—all retained.
- **13–19:** God's wisdom versus impatient desires, temptation to leave before the appointed time, Noah waiting for both entrance and exit commands and obedient reliance on divine protection—retained.
- **20–22:** first altar before first home, costly worship, no waste in honoring God, New Covenant worship expressed in praise/devotion/conduct, animal sacrifices only acceptable as figures of Christ and expressions of faith, enduring sinful nature, no further universal flood, eventual fiery destruction and new heavens/earth, dependable seasonal promises—all retained.
