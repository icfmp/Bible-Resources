# Luke 19 — Modern English

| Original outline | Verses |

## Zacchaeus is converted (verses 1–10)

Anyone who sincerely wishes to see Christ should imitate **Zacchaeus**, overcoming obstacles and making the necessary effort to reach him.

Jesus invited himself to Zacchaeus's home.

Wherever Christ comes, he opens the heart and makes it willing to receive him.

A person who sincerely wants to know Christ will be **known by Christ**.

Those whom Jesus calls must humble themselves and come down.

We may welcome him with joy because **he brings every true good gift**.

Zacchaeus publicly showed that he had genuinely been converted.

He did **not** expect to be justified by his good works.

Rather, by his actions he showed, **through God's grace, the sincerity of his faith and repentance**.

Now that Zacchaeus had turned from sin to God, he was truly blessed.

He had been saved from both **sin's guilt and its power** and shared in all the benefits of salvation.

Christ had entered his home, and where he comes he brings salvation.

Jesus came into this lost world **to seek and save the lost**, when no other salvation was available.

He seeks even those who had not begun to seek or ask for him.

## The nobleman and his servants (verses 11–27)

This parable resembles **the parable of the talents in Matthew 25**.

When Christ calls people, he equips them with gifts needed for their responsibilities.

Anyone entrusted with power or resources must use them in service.

The Spirit's gifts are given for **the common good** (**1 Corinthians 12:7**).

Everyone who has received a gift must use it in serving others (**1 Peter 4:10**).

Christ calls his servants to account for the opportunities entrusted to them.

The parable also warns of the punishment awaiting those openly hostile to him and those who profess loyalty without genuine service.

Henry notes a distinction between the two parables:

The **single mina given to each servant** suggests the **one gospel offered equally to all who hear it**.

The **different numbers of talents** suggest the varied abilities and advantages through which people may make use of that same gospel.

## Jesus enters Jerusalem (verses 28–40)

Christ rules all creatures and can use them as he pleases.

He knows every human heart and holds it within his sovereign power.

Jesus' triumphal entry and his disciples' joyful praise angered the proud Pharisees, who opposed him and his kingdom.

He refuses to be discouraged by the contempt of proud people but **welcomes the praises of the humble**.

The Pharisees wished to silence those praising him.

Yet they could not prevent God's purpose.

The Lord who can raise up children of Abraham from stones and turn stone-like hearts toward himself can bring praise even from the mouths of children.

How will people feel when the same Lord returns in glory to judge the world?

## Jesus weeps over Jerusalem (verses 41–48)

Jesus looked ahead to the suffering his enemies would bring upon themselves and **wept over Jerusalem**, where his own precious blood would soon be shed.

His tears teach us that reflecting God's character means showing **compassion and goodwill toward sinners**.

No one can hold Christian truth rightly while becoming hardhearted toward people who need mercy.

Yet Jesus' compassion did not mean divine judgment was unreal.

He wept over Jerusalem, and terrible judgment later fell upon it.

The Lord does not delight in a sinner's death.

But he will fulfill his warnings against those who continue rejecting his salvation.

The Son of God did not weep for himself or over something trivial.

He knew the value of souls and the heavy weight of human guilt.

May he cleanse our hearts from every corruption through his Spirit.

May people everywhere listen carefully to the message of truth and salvation.
