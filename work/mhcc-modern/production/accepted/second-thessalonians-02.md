# 2 Thessalonians 2 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Cautions against the error that the time of Christ's coming was just at hand. There would first be a general apostasy from the faith, and a revealing of the antichristian man of sin. | 1–4 |
| His destruction, and that of those who obey him. | 5–12 |
| The security of the Thessalonians from apostacy; an exhortation to stedfastness, and prayer for them. | 13–17 |

## Do not be deceived about the Lord's return (verses 1–4)

When errors arise among Christians, we should **correct them**.

Faithful servants should be especially careful to remedy mistakes that have grown from **misunderstanding their own words or actions**.

Our cunning adversary looks for opportunities to harm us.

Satan may encourage false teaching **even through the misuse of Scripture's words**.

We may be unsure about the time of Christ's return, but we have every reason to be sure **that he will return**.

Believers in every generation of the church, and the faithful before Christ's first coming, have trusted this promise.

All God's people will be **gathered to Christ** to live with him in happiness forever.

But wrongly assuming the time is already at hand creates a danger.

When the predicted date passes, the disappointed person may begin to doubt **the event's certainty itself**.

False doctrines resemble wind that makes water churn and ripple.

Our minds can be just as unstable.

It is enough to know that Jesus **will return and gather his people**.

Paul gave a reason the Thessalonians should not yet expect the final day: a great **falling away** would take place first, bringing the revelation of **the man of sin, Antichrist**.

People have debated at length **who or what** this figure is.

The man of sin not only commits wicked acts but **promotes and commands others to sin**.

He is called a son of destruction because **his own ruin is certain**, while he becomes the instrument through which many others are destroyed in body and soul.

The imagery of God's temple points to a usurper who claims **God's authority within the Christian church** and demands honors **belonging to God alone**.

## Corrupt religion and false miracles (verses 5–12)

Something was restraining the full appearance of the man of sin.

**Henry supposes that this restraint was the power of the Roman Empire**, which Paul did not name explicitly at the time.

Corruption in doctrine and worship, followed by the usurpation of authority, entered **gradually**.

Thus the **mystery of lawlessness** advanced step by step.

Superstition and idolatry were promoted under the appearance of devotion.

Bigotry and persecution were justified through claims of zeal for God's glory.

The mystery of lawlessness had already begun during the apostles' lives, when some professed zeal for Christ but actually opposed him.

Paul announced the destruction of this antichristian condition.

**God's pure word joined with God's Spirit** exposes its deceit.

In the Lord's appointed time, it will be destroyed through **the brightness of Christ's coming**.

False signs, visions and miracles can be used to support deceptive doctrine.

Some may be fraudulent displays or claimed miracles intended **to mislead people**.

Those willingly deceived **did not love the truth** and therefore refused to believe it.

They found pleasure in false notions.

God leaves such people to themselves, and they consequently enter deeper sin, experience spiritual judgment here and face **eternal punishment afterward**.

Henry concludes that many of these prophecies had already been fulfilled and confirmed Scripture's truth.

**As a seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Protestant commentator, he specifically identifies the papal system and the Roman Catholic Church with this antichristian pattern**.

That identification is preserved **as Henry's historical interpretation**, not as an independently established fact or as a replacement of the biblical text.

He also insists that **the Lord has not yet completely destroyed this evil through his appearing**, and that **further prophecies remain to be fulfilled before the end**.

## The elect persevere through faith and the Spirit's holiness (verses 13–15)

When many turn away from the faith, we can rejoice that God preserves **a remnant chosen by grace**.

We should also desire evidence that we belong to that remnant.

The perseverance of God's children rests upon **his everlasting love**, present from before the world's foundation.

Yet the promised end must never be separated from the **appointed means**.

**Faith and holiness belong together**, just as holiness and eternal happiness belong together.

God's outward call comes **through the gospel**.

That call becomes effective through **the inward work of the Holy Spirit**.

Believing God's truth brings the sinner to **trust Christ**, love him and obey his commands.

The Holy Spirit seals the gospel upon the heart.

Henry says we have **no certain evidence of any apostolic teachings delivered outside the contents of Holy Scripture**.

Therefore we should hold firmly to the apostolic doctrines preserved **in Scripture** and reject unsupported additions and vain traditions.

## Christ himself gives enduring comfort (verses 16 and 17)

We may and should pray not only **to God the Father through Jesus**, but also **directly to the Lord Jesus Christ**, our Savior.

We pray in Christ's name to his Father, who has also become **our Father through him**.

The love of God in Jesus Christ is the fountain of **everything good we now possess or hope to receive**.

Believers have strong reason to receive **lasting consolation**, because grace gives them **a secure hope**.

The free grace and mercy of God—not personal worth or merit—are the foundation of that hope.

The more joy we take in God's **word, deeds and ways**, the more likely we are to persevere in them.

When we waver in faith, hesitate in our duties or continue in uncertainty and distrust, it is no surprise that we fail to experience **the joys of Christian faith**.
