# Romans 9 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The apostle's concern that his countrymen were strangers to the gospel. | 1–5 |
| The promises are made good to the spiritual seed of Abraham. | 6–13 |

## Paul's grief for his people (verses 1–5)

As Paul prepared to speak about the rejection of unbelieving Jews and the calling of the Gentiles, he expressed extraordinary love for his own people.

He called Christ to witness and appealed to **a conscience enlightened and guided by the Holy Spirit**.

Henry interprets Paul's willingness to be **accursed** as a readiness to endure disgrace, crucifixion and even a period of extreme horror and distress if that could save his nation from the destruction approaching through unbelief.

Indifference toward the eternal condition of other human beings contradicts both **the law's command to love** and **the gospel's mercy**.

The Israelites had long professed worship of Jehovah.

They possessed the law and its national covenant.

Their temple worship pictured salvation through the Messiah and provided means of fellowship with God.

The promises about Christ and his salvation were entrusted to them.

**Christ rules over all as Mediator, and he is also God blessed forever**.

## God's promise reaches Abraham's spiritual descendants (verses 6–13)

The rejection of unbelieving Jews under the gospel did not break God's promises to the patriarchs.

His promises and warnings will be fulfilled.

**Grace is not inherited through family bloodlines**.

Outward membership and advantages in the visible church do not always bring salvation.

Some descendants of Abraham were chosen and others were not.

God acted according to **the counsel of his own will**.

God knew both Esau and Jacob as sinful from birth, naturally children of wrath like others.

If left to themselves, both would have continued in sin.

But for **wise and holy reasons not revealed to us**, God intended to change Jacob's heart while leaving Esau to his own perversity.

Henry draws from this history a lesson about God's dealings with fallen humanity.

Scripture distinguishes **people who outwardly profess faith** from **those who genuinely believe**.

Many enjoy religious privileges without becoming God's children.

Nevertheless, **everyone is encouraged to use diligently the means of grace God has provided**.

## God's sovereign mercy is just (verses 14–24)

Everything God does is just.

The difference between those made holy and others comes **from God's grace alone**.

His grace comes before and effectively changes those he saves.

It is **distinguishing grace**, freely given by a Benefactor who owes it to no one.

No sinner deserves it.

Therefore, those who are saved should **thank God alone**, while those who perish have only themselves to blame (**Hosea 13:9**).

God is bound only by what he has freely promised in his covenant, his revealed will.

He has promised to **receive rather than reject anyone who comes to Christ**.

But the grace that first **draws a sinner to come** is a special gift granted according to his will.

“Why does he still find fault?” is not an objection the creature is entitled to press against the Creator.

The truth as revealed in Jesus humbles human pride and exalts the sovereign Lord.

We are too weak and ignorant to pronounce judgment on God's hidden purposes.

As a potter decides what to make from clay, God possesses sovereign authority over his creation.

He does no injustice, however difficult his purposes may appear to us.

He will demonstrate his hatred of sin.

But he also prepares **vessels of mercy**.

**Sanctification prepares the soul for glory**, and this is God's work.

Sinners prepare themselves for judgment through their sins; **God prepares saints for heaven**.

Everyone he intends to receive in glory is made fit for heaven through grace in this life.

Who are these vessels of mercy?

**Those whom God has called, from Jews and Gentiles alike**.

No injustice exists in God's patience toward guilty sinners, even when he delays their final judgment.

The guilt belongs to hardened sinners themselves.

Though such truths exceed our ability to understand fully, those who love the Lord should adore **his pardoning mercy and recreating grace**, and diligently make their calling and election sure.

## Jewish and Gentile believers share God's mercy (verses 25–29)

The Old Testament foretold both the rejection of unbelieving Jews and the inclusion of Gentiles.

Seeing Scripture fulfilled confirms the truth of God's actions.

That **anyone** should be saved is a wonder of divine power and mercy.

Even the remnant preserved would have perished like the rest if God had treated them according to their sins.

Henry warns that even among the many people who profess Christianity, it is to be feared **only a remnant may finally be saved**.

## Faith, not legal works, brings righteousness (verses 30–33)

The Gentiles had not understood their guilt and misery or urgently sought a remedy.

Nevertheless, many received **righteousness by faith**.

They did not need to become Jewish proselytes or submit to the ceremonial law.

They embraced **Christ**, believed in him and accepted the gospel.

Many Jews spoke of justification and holiness and desired to be counted God's special people.

But they sought it **in the wrong way**.

Instead of humbling themselves and receiving Christ through faith, they tried to obtain righteousness by obeying the requirements and ceremonies of the Mosaic law.

The unbelieving Jews were offered righteousness, life and salvation on **the gospel's terms**.

But they did not want or accept those terms.

Have we personally sought to know how to be justified before God, receiving that blessing through **faith in Christ, the Lord our Righteousness**?

Those who do will not be ashamed at the final judgment.

Then false refuges will disappear under God's righteous judgment, and **no hiding place will remain except the refuge God has provided in his Son**.
