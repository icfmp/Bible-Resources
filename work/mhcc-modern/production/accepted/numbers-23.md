# Numbers 23 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Balak's sacrifice, Balaam pronounces a blessing instead of a curse. | 1–10 |
| Balak's disappointment, and second sacrifice, Balaam again blesses Israel. | 11–30 |

## Balaam blesses instead of cursing (verses 1–10)

With the camp of Israel clearly visible, Balaam ordered seven altars to be built, with a bull and a ram offered upon each.

What foolish superstition it is to imagine that human beings can command God to do what they want!

Through God's controlling power and love for Israel, the curse Balaam intended was turned into a blessing.

God intended to accomplish his own glory through Balaam, and therefore met him.

If the Lord could place words in the mouth of a man who was hostile to both God and Israel, he can certainly provide words for people who sincerely want to glorify him and build up his people.

The God who made Balaam's donkey speak now made the wicked prophet say what his own heart did not want to say.

The miracle was as great in one instance as in the other.

Balaam declared that Israel was safe.

He admitted that he could do only what God allowed.

He declared Israel blessed for being distinct from the nations, numerous enough to have dignity and strength, and secure in its future.

He also wished to enjoy the end of a righteous person's life.

Death comes to everyone, including the righteous.

We do well to consider our own deaths.

The righteous are blessed not only while living but also when dying.

Their death can therefore be more desirable than life itself.

But many people want the death of a righteous person without being willing to live a righteous life.

They want to be saints in heaven but not on earth.

Balaam expressed a wish, not a prayer rooted in genuine commitment.

He wanted the good end without caring about the way to reach it.

Others likewise quiet their consciences with promises of future change or false hopes, while neglecting the one path by which sinners can be justified before God.

## Balaam blesses Israel again (verses 11–30)

Balak became angry with Balaam.

A wicked prophet was forced to acknowledge God's governing power, frustrating a wicked king.

For a second time, an intended curse became a blessing—this time even stronger and more extensive than before.

Human beings change their minds and break their promises.

God does not change his mind or withdraw his promises.

When Scripture speaks of God **repenting**, Henry explains that this means a change in the way he deals with people, not a change within God's own mind.

There was sin among Jacob's descendants, and the Lord saw it.

Yet it was not the kind of settled rebellion for which he was now abandoning them to destruction.

When people trust God's mercy, accept his salvation, refuse to indulge cherished sinful desires, and seek to honor him, they may be assured of acceptance in Christ and the forgiveness of their sins.

Think of the wonders of Providence and grace: redeeming love, forgiving mercy and the Spirit making people new!

Balak could no longer expect to destroy Israel.

Balaam now warned that Balak had more reason to fear destruction by Israel.

Because Balaam would not pronounce the curse Balak wanted, the king told him to say nothing.

Yet God's purposes prevail over every human scheme.

Balak and Balaam resolved to make another attempt, despite possessing no divine promise of success.

We, by contrast, have God's sure promises.

Let us persevere earnestly in prayer (Luke 18:1).
