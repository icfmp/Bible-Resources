# Revelation 12 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| A description of the church of Christ and of Satan, under the figures of a woman and of a great red dragon. | 1–6 |
| Michael and his angels fight against the devil and his angels, who are defeated. | 7–12 |
| The dragon persecutes the church. | 13, 14 |
| His vain endeavours to destroy her, He renews his war against her seed. | 14–17 |

## The woman and the dragon (verses 1–6)

John saw the church pictured as **a woman in heaven**, the mother of believers.

She was clothed with the sun, representing the way believers are **justified, sanctified and made radiant through union with Christ**, the Sun of Righteousness.

The moon beneath her feet showed her greater light compared with the reflected and weaker revelation received through Moses.

Her crown of **twelve stars** represented gospel teaching proclaimed by the **twelve apostles**, a glory belonging to all genuine believers.

She labored to bring forth a holy family, desiring that sinners' conviction would lead to their conversion.

The **red dragon** represents Satan and his chief agents—those who govern in his interests on earth.

Henry identifies this particular historical embodiment with **the pagan Roman Empire**, the city of seven hills.

He interprets the **ten horns** as ten kingdoms and the **seven crowns** as seven forms of government.

Those are his historical and symbolic interpretations, not settled facts supplied by the passage itself.

The dragon's tail sweeping away a third of the stars pictures the persecution and seduction of **ministers and teachers**.

The enemy sought to crush Christianity.

Nevertheless, the church brought forth a faithful and vigorous people in whom **Christ was formed anew**.

The Son of God will govern the nations, and his people share the honor he wins for them.

God protected this blessed offspring.

## The dragon's defeat and the church's weapons (verses 7–11)

The dragon's attack upon the church ultimately damaged **his own cause**.

Henry understands the conflict in heaven as being fought in **Christ's church, the kingdom of heaven present on earth**.

He identifies the opposing sides with **Christ, the great Angel of the covenant, and his faithful people**, and Satan with his instruments.

The church's strength rests in having **Jesus Christ as Captain of its salvation**.

Henry connects the dragon's defeat with **pagan idolatry being driven out of the Roman Empire through Christianity's spread**.

The church owes its deliverance and strength entirely to its King and Head.

The devil hates God's presence, yet is willing to appear before him in order to accuse believers.

We must take care not to provide grounds for such accusations.

When we do sin, we should confess before God, condemn our own wrongdoing and **entrust our case to Jesus Christ as Advocate**.

God's people overcome Satan through three means.

First, **the blood of the Lamb** is the only foundation of their hope.

Second, **their testimony to the gospel** is powerful through God to bring down spiritual strongholds.

Third, they show **courage and patience in suffering**, loving Christ more than their own lives and being willing to die rather than abandon his cause.

Henry sees these as the instruments through which Christianity overcame pagan idolatry.

He argues that if Christians had continued to rely upon **these spiritual weapons**, instead of other means, their victories would have been greater, more lasting and more glorious.

We too must trust the Lamb's blood **alone**, without adding anything else as a basis for salvation.

## The dragon persecutes the remaining faithful (verses 12–17)

Christ's church and its friends rightly praise God for deliverance from pagan persecution, although further trials await.

The **wilderness** is desolate and dangerous, inhabited by serpents and scorpions and lacking ordinary comforts.

Yet it can also be **a place of refuge and safety**.

Even withdrawal into such a refuge did not completely protect the woman from the dragon.

Henry records **two proposed interpretations** of the flood that the dragon sent against her.

Some understood it as **the barbarian invasions that overwhelmed the Western Roman Empire**, with pagan opponents hoping that the destruction would also end Christianity.

But people acting from worldly interests nevertheless protected the church through those upheavals, so the end of the empire did not secure a victory for idolatry.

Alternatively, the flood might mean **a torrent of error** threatening to sweep away God's people.

Neither identification is presented as certain.

Defeated in his attempts against the church as a whole, Satan turned his anger against particular believers and communities.

Faithfulness to God and Christ in **teaching, worship and conduct** exposes Christians to spiritual opposition.

That conflict continues until the final enemy is destroyed.
