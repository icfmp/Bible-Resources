# Genesis 46 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God's promises to Jacob. | 1–4 |
| Jacob and his family go to Egypt. | 5–27 |
| Joseph meets his father and his brethren. | 28–34 |

## God encourages Jacob (verses 1–4)

Even when an undertaking looks especially joyful, we should seek the Lord's counsel, help, and blessing.

As we take part in the worship he has appointed and receive assurances of his covenant love, we look for his presence and the peace that comes with it.

Every move from one home to another should remind us that one day we must leave this world.

Only Christ's presence can enable us to pass through the valley of the shadow of death without fear.

## Jacob's family travels to Egypt (verses 5–27)

We are given a detailed account of Jacob's family.

God's promises are certain to come true, yet their fulfillment is often slow.

Henry notes that 215 years had passed since God promised Abraham that he would become a great nation (Genesis 12:2).

Yet the branch of Abraham's descendants in which the promise would surely be fulfilled had grown to only seventy people.

This careful record was kept to show God's power in making those seventy grow into an enormous multitude.

## Joseph welcomes his family (verses 28–34)

It was only right that Pharaoh should know that a family had come to settle in his territory.

When others trust us, we must never repay that trust by deceiving them.

How would Joseph arrange things for his brothers?

Once they had plotted to get rid of him. Now he was planning how to settle them where they would prosper.

This is what it means to repay evil with good.

Joseph wanted them to live together in Goshen, near Canaan.

The Egyptians despised shepherds. Yet Joseph did not want his brothers to be ashamed to tell Pharaoh that shepherding was their occupation.

He could perhaps have obtained positions for them at court or in the army.

But such advancement might have aroused Egyptian jealousy and tempted them to forget Canaan and God's promise to their fathers.

There is nothing disgraceful about honest work.

We should be ashamed of being idle or having no useful occupation, not of doing humble work.

Usually it is best to remain in the occupation we know and have been trained to do.

Whatever work or position God has given us through his providence, we should adapt to it, be content with it, and not set our hearts on high status.

It is better to bring honor to a humble post than disgrace to an exalted one.

If we wanted to ruin our own souls or those of our children, we might chase great things for ourselves and for them.

But if we do not want that, we should be content when we have food and clothing.
