# 1 Kings 11 — Modern English

## Solomon turns to idolatry (verses 1–8)

Few passages in Scripture give a more sorrowful or astonishing example of human corruption than this one.

Solomon became a public worshiper of detestable idols.

He probably yielded little by little to pride and luxury and lost his taste for true wisdom.

Nothing in ourselves can make us safe from the deceitfulness and corruption of the human heart.

Nor does old age remove our sinful inclinations.

Unless sinful desires are crucified and put to death through God's grace, they will never simply disappear. They can remain even when the opportunity to satisfy them is gone.

Anyone who thinks he stands securely must be careful not to fall.

Without divine grace we are weak.

Let us therefore depend continually upon that grace, stay alert and keep sober judgment.

We are fighting a dangerous battle in enemy territory, while our worst enemies are the traitors within our own hearts.

## God announces judgment on Solomon (verses 9–13)

The Lord told Solomon, probably through a prophet, what his apostasy would bring upon him.

We have reason to hope Solomon repented and found mercy.

**But the Holy Spirit did not expressly record his repentance.**

Scripture leaves the matter uncertain, as a warning against deliberate sin.

Guilt may be forgiven while its disgrace remains.

We cannot determine before the day of judgment **whether Solomon was finally left under God's everlasting displeasure**.

## God raises opponents against Solomon (verses 14–25)

As long as Solomon walked closely with God and kept his duty, enemies did not disturb him.

Now two adversaries rose against him.

If God is for us, even powerful opponents cannot prevail.

But if he is against us, even a small trouble can frighten us; a mere grasshopper may become a burden.

These adversaries acted from ambition or revenge.

Nevertheless, God used their actions to correct Solomon.

## Jeroboam is promised the kingdom (verses 26–40)

When Ahijah explained why the kingdom would be torn from Solomon's house, he warned Jeroboam not to lose his own advancement through sin.

Yet David's house would continue, because **the Messiah** was to come from it.

Solomon tried to kill the man who would succeed him.

Had he not taught other people that however many plans human beings make, the Lord's counsel will stand?

Yet now Solomon himself imagined he could defeat that counsel.

Jeroboam took refuge in Egypt.

He was content for a time to live in obscurity and exile because he had the promise of a kingdom.

Should we not be equally content, when we have an even better kingdom promised to us?

## Solomon dies (verses 41–43)

Solomon's reign lasted as long as his father's, but his life was shorter.

His sin shortened his days.

If earthly possessions and opportunities could satisfy the soul and bring true joy, Solomon would have found it.

Instead, they disappointed him.

He left the record of his experience to warn us: **vanity and vexation of spirit**.

The New Testament announces that one greater than Solomon has come to rule over us and inherit the throne of his father David.

Solomon's greatness faintly represents something of Christ's far greater excellence.
