# Matthew 9 — Modern English

## Jesus forgives and heals a paralysed man (verses 1–8)

The friends who brought the paralysed man to Jesus showed **strong, humble and active faith**.

They believed he both **could** and **would** heal him. Faith that earnestly seeks Christ does not allow obstacles to prevent it.

Their humility brought the patient to Jesus rather than trying to claim an entitlement to healing.

Sin may be pardoned while illness remains. Equally, illness may disappear while sin remains unforgiven.

But when recovery from sickness accompanies the comfort of **peace with God**, healing becomes a special mercy indeed.

This is no excuse to continue in sin.

We may come to Christ with our sins **as an illness and misery from which we want deliverance**.

It is a terrible deception to approach him while **treasuring those sins and intending to keep them**.

The great purpose of his saving work is to **separate our hearts from sin**.

Jesus knows perfectly what people say within themselves. Evil thoughts are themselves sinful and offensive to him.

He demonstrated that his mission was to save his people from their sins.

Instead of continuing the argument with the scribes, he commanded the man to rise.

The man not only no longer needed to be carried on his bed; he was strong enough to **carry the bed himself**.

Every power and ability God gives for doing good should lead us to glorify him.

## Matthew is called (verse 9)

Matthew was at work in his occupation when Christ called him, as other disciples had been.

Satan often approaches idle people with temptation. Christ frequently calls people **while they are working**.

By nature we resist the Lord. We therefore need him to call us by his powerful word and draw us to himself.

If he speaks by **the word of his Spirit** to our hearts, the world's attraction cannot hold us and Satan cannot block the path.

We rise and follow.

This saving change has **Christ as its author** and **his word as the means**.

Neither Matthew's office nor its income could detain him once Christ called.

The disciples who had been fishermen later returned to fishing on occasion. Matthew, however, was never again found pursuing his former sinful gains.

## Matthew welcomes his former associates (verses 10–13)

After being called, Matthew invited former companions to hear Jesus.

He knew from personal experience what Christ's grace could accomplish and did not despair of their conversion.

People truly brought to Christ naturally long for **others to be brought to him as well**.

Those who imagine that their souls are perfectly healthy have no desire to receive help from the spiritual Physician.

The Pharisees despised Christ because they regarded themselves as whole.

Tax collectors and other sinners recognized their need for instruction and change.

It is easy—and sadly common—to put the worst interpretation on even good words and actions.

Those who resent other people receiving God's grace give reason to question whether they themselves possess it.

Jesus' fellowship with sinners was an act of **mercy**, because helping bring souls to conversion is a supreme work of mercy.

The gospel summons us to **repentance**, a change of both mind and conduct.

If human beings had not sinned, there would have been no need for Christ to come among them.

Have we recognized our own sickness and begun following the directions of **our great Physician**?

## John's disciples ask about fasting (verses 14–17)

John the Baptist was then in prison.

His circumstances, strict way of life and special message led the disciples particularly devoted to him to fast frequently.

Jesus reminded them of John's own testimony concerning him (**John 3:29**).

Though Jesus and his disciples lived sparingly, fasting during the joy of the Bridegroom's personal presence would not be appropriate.

When Christ is present with his people, all is well.

As the presence of the sun creates day and its absence brings night, so his presence brings joy.

Jesus then used familiar illustrations of **good judgment and timing**.

A patch of rough, unprepared cloth sewn onto a soft, worn garment can pull away and make its tear worse.

New wine placed in weak, decaying leather containers can ferment and burst them.

But fresh wine kept in strong new skins preserves both.

These comparisons teach that we must use **great care and wisdom with new believers**.

Do not unnecessarily give them a gloomy, discouraging picture of Christ's service.

Teach and urge duties in ways they are ready and able to bear.

## A woman's bleeding stops and Jairus's daughter lives (verses 18–26)

When someone we love dies, our loss should drive us toward **Christ, who is our life**.

Even the most powerful rulers are honored—not diminished—when they bow before Jesus.

Whoever seeks mercy from him must honor him.

Christ worked miracles in different ways, perhaps because he knew perfectly the varying dispositions of those who came to him.

On the way to the ruler's house, he received an appeal from a suffering woman and healed her.

If by living faith we reach out even to the **hem of Christ's garment**, our deepest spiritual evils will be healed.

There is no other true cure.

Nor should we fear that Christ knows the burdens and shame we hesitate to tell even our closest earthly friends.

When Jesus reached the house, he told the mourners to make room.

Sometimes **worldly sorrow** fills the heart so completely that people shut out Christ's comfort.

The girl was genuinely dead, though not beyond Christ's power.

The death of the righteous can particularly be regarded as **sleep**, awaiting their awakening.

We may not immediately understand Christ's words or actions, but they should never be despised on that account.

He sent away the mockers. Those who laugh at what they do not understand are not fitting witnesses of his works.

Spiritually dead people likewise do not rise to new life unless **Christ takes them by the hand** in the day of his power.

If raising one recently deceased child increased Jesus' fame, what will his glory be when **everyone in the graves hears his voice** and comes out—some to the resurrection of life and others to the resurrection of judgment?

## Two blind men receive sight (verses 27–31)

The Jews expected the Messiah. These two blind men knew and declared publicly that **Jesus was the promised Son of David**.

People who lose bodily sight through Providence may nevertheless receive the full illumination of their spiritual understanding through grace.

Whatever burdens or needs we carry, **the mercy of Jesus Christ is sufficient**.

The men followed and cried out to him.

Christ delayed the answer to **test their faith**, teaching us to pray persistently without losing heart.

The central question was not merely whether they wanted sight but **whether they believed**.

Natural need can produce urgent cries, but only grace creates saving faith.

Jesus touched their eyes. He opens the eyes of spiritually blind people through his word accompanied by the power of grace.

He made the healing depend upon their faith.

Those who approach Jesus are treated not according to imagined expectations or outward profession, but **according to faith**.

Jesus sometimes instructed healed people not to spread news of the miracle.

Henry connects this to his unwillingness to encourage the popular misconception that the Messiah would simply be an earthly political ruler, provoking unrest or rebellion.

## Jesus casts out a spirit that prevented speech (verses 32–34)

Henry contrasts the demon that made a person unable to speak with **blaspheming opponents** and says the former is the less terrible case.

Christ's cures reach the **root of a problem**: by breaking Satan's power, he removes its effects and opens the lips.

Pride can make people refuse even the clearest evidence.

They will accept false and absurd claims rather than Scripture because their hearts are hostile to a holy God.

## Jesus sees a great harvest in need of workers (verses 35–38)

Jesus visited not only large, prosperous cities but also poor and obscure villages.

He **preached and healed** in those places.

The souls of the least significant people in society are as precious to Christ as those of the most prominent.

They should be equally precious to us.

Although the land had priests, Levites and scribes, Henry regarded many as **worthless shepherds** (**Zechariah 11:17**).

Jesus therefore had compassion on the people, scattered like sheep without a shepherd and perishing through lack of spiritual knowledge.

Many people remain in that condition today.

We should pity them and do everything we can to help.

The crowds eager for spiritual teaching constituted **a plentiful harvest**, yet truly active and faithful workers were few.

**Christ is the Lord of the harvest**.

We should pray that he will raise up and send many laborers who work to bring souls to him.

When God stirs people to pray earnestly for a needed mercy, this can be a sign that he intends to grant it.

Those commissioned in answer to prayer have particular reason to hope that their work will bear fruit.
