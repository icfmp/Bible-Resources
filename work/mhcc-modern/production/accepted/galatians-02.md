# Galatians 2 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The apostle declares his being owned as an apostle of the Gentiles. | 1–10 |
| He had publicly opposed Peter for judaizing. | 11–14 |
| And from thence he enters upon the doctrine of justification by faith in Christ, without the works of the law. | 15–21 |

## The other apostles recognize Paul's calling (verses 1–10)

Paul faithfully explained the gospel he preached among the Gentiles and intended to continue preaching.

It was **Christianity free from any requirement to become Jewish**.

He understood that many people disliked this message, but he was not ashamed to acknowledge it.

He wanted to ensure that neither his earlier work nor his future ministry would be damaged by confusion or opposition.

Although we must trust God for the fruit of our work, we should still take reasonable precautions to prevent misunderstandings.

Some practices may be lawful in themselves.

But if accepting them in particular circumstances would **betray gospel truth**, we must refuse.

Nothing should be done that undermines the gospel.

Paul met the other apostles but received no addition to **his knowledge or authority** from them.

Recognizing the grace God had given him, they welcomed Paul and Barnabas as fellow workers.

They acknowledged Paul's **equal apostolic calling**, agreeing that Paul and Barnabas should go principally to the Gentiles while the others continued working among the Jews.

This division of labor agreed with Christ's purpose.

The gospel belongs **to God, not to us**. Ministers are entrusted with preserving and declaring it, never owning or altering it.

Paul also showed sincere charity toward Jewish believers, even though some of them were unwilling to show equal generosity toward Gentile converts.

Differences of opinion did not make him unwilling to help them.

Christians should extend such goodwill to **all genuine disciples of Jesus**.

## Paul publicly corrects Peter's public inconsistency (verses 11–14)

Despite Peter's important calling and reputation, Paul rebuked him when his behavior harmed **the truth of the gospel and the church's peace**.

Peter and those who followed him acted inconsistently with the principle they professed.

Through Christ's death, the dividing wall between Jews and Gentiles had been removed, and believers were no longer bound to follow **Moses' ceremonial law** as a condition of acceptance.

Because Peter's error was **public**, Paul reproved him **publicly**.

Henry carefully distinguishes two actions.

Paul could wisely tolerate and temporarily observe Jewish ceremonies when they were **not themselves sinful**.

Peter, however, withdrew from Gentile believers in a way that encouraged people to think those ceremonies were **necessary for salvation**.

That was a failure of courage and a betrayal of gospel freedom.

## We are justified through Christ, not the law (verses 15–19)

Having shown that he was not inferior to Peter or any other apostle, Paul turned to the gospel's foundational doctrine.

Why had they believed in Christ?

Was it not to be **justified by faith in him**?

Then how foolish to return to the law and expect justification by **moral efforts, sacrifices or ceremonial observances**!

Although the immediate controversy involved ceremonial law, the argument against dependence on **moral works for justification** is just as strong.

Seeking justification through Jesus does not make Christ **a servant of sin**.

Paul understood from the law itself that the law could not justify sinners by their works.

Its sacrifices and cleansings were no longer required because **Christ had fulfilled them by offering himself for us**.

As far as justification by the law was concerned, Paul was as dead to it as a dead person is to the threats of old enemies.

Yet this did not mean he could live lawlessly or carelessly.

Instead, he was freed **to live for God**, devoted to him through the motives and grace of the gospel.

The claim that justification **by faith alone** encourages sin is an old but entirely unjust accusation.

Using grace as permission for sin would make Christ the **promoter of sin**, a thought that should horrify every Christian heart.

## Crucified with Christ, living by faith (verses 20 and 21)

Paul described the Christian's hidden spiritual life through his own experience.

**The old person is crucified** (**Romans 6:6**), yet the renewed person lives.

Sin is put to death while grace grows.

Believers enjoy the comforts and triumphs of grace, but none of that grace originates in themselves.

They live in complete dependence upon **Jesus Christ**.

Though still physically alive, they no longer live according to the sinful nature.

Genuine faith fixes upon Christ's personal sacrifice:

**He loved me and gave himself for me.**

Paul had been moving farther and farther from God.

The corruption of his desires and understanding was so great that no lesser price could have redeemed him.

Consider what Christ paid!

Some claim to believe the Christian doctrines but have only **the outward form of godliness without its power**.

True faith in Christ crucified means more than accepting the historical fact that he died.

It includes being **crucified with him**, leaving the old way of life behind.

Grace can never be combined with human merit as its basis.

It would cease to be grace if it were not entirely free.

The more fully we depend on Christ for everything, the more willingly we obey his commandments.

He lives and rules within the believer.

We live by faith in the Son of God—a faith that **works through love**, produces obedience and transforms us toward his holy likeness.

Such a believer neither misuses God's grace nor treats it as worthless.
