# Revelation 8 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The seventh seal is opened and seven angels appear with seven trumpets, ready to proclaim the purposes of God. | 1, 2 |
| Another angel casts fire on the earth, which produces terrible storms of vengeance. | 3–5 |
| The seven angels prepare to sound their trumpets. | 6 |
| Four sound them. | 7–12 |
| Another angel denounces greater woes to come. | 13 |

## The seventh seal, silence and the prayers of the saints (verses 1–6)

When the **seventh seal** was opened, profound silence followed in heaven.

Henry suggests two possible meanings.

The church may have been quiet, since its cries under oppression reach heaven.

Or the silence may have expressed **solemn expectation**.

Seven angels received trumpets with which to announce God's purposes.

Jesus Christ, **the great High Priest of the church**, holds a golden censer and abundant incense.

This signifies the **fullness of merit in his glorious person**, through which he intercedes.

How much more eagerly we should study the fullness of Christ, learn his excellence and recognize the compassion with which he carries out his work as our Intercessor!

Prayers recommended by Christ in this way are received and heard in heaven.

Their acceptance leads to great changes on earth.

Christian worship and the Christian faith are pure and heavenly in their origin.

Yet when they enter the world and encounter sinful human passions and worldly plans, **great disturbances** may follow.

Henry connects these prophetic images with Jesus' words in **Luke 12:49**.

## The first four trumpets and the announcement of coming woes (verses 7–13)

At the **first trumpet**, hail and fire mingled with blood appeared.

Henry suggests this may picture **a storm of heresies and destructive errors falling upon the church**, or a tempest of physical destruction.

At the **second trumpet**, a great mountain burning with fire fell into the sea and a third of the sea became blood.

Some interpreters take the mountain to represent **leaders of persecution**.

Others associate it with **Rome's devastation by the Goths and Vandals**, accompanied by much cruelty and bloodshed.

These are interpretations Henry reports, not identifications established by the text.

At the **third trumpet**, a star fell from heaven.

Some interpret it as **a prominent ruler**.

Others take it to signify **a powerful individual who corrupted Christ's churches**.

The gospel's doctrines are springs that nourish spiritual life, comfort and strength.

When those springs become mixed with dangerous errors, people encounter bitterness and spiritual ruin where they had hoped to find refreshment.

At the **fourth trumpet**, the heavenly lights—the sun, moon and stars—were darkened.

Henry applies this to guides and governors, who occupy high positions and should provide **light and beneficial influence** to others.

When a people receive the gospel without allowing it to transform their hearts and lives, terrible judgments may follow.

God warns people through **his written word, ministers, their consciences and the signs of the times**.

If people are surprised by judgment after such warnings, they bear responsibility.

God's anger can make every comfort bitter and even life itself burdensome.

Yet he sets boundaries around the worst judgments in this world.

Corruption in a church's **doctrine and worship** is itself a great judgment, and frequently both the cause and a warning of further calamity.

Before the remaining three trumpets sounded, a solemn warning announced that still worse troubles were approaching.

If lesser judgments fail to bring repentance, the church and world must expect greater ones.

Let sinners seek rescue from coming wrath.

Let believers prize and thank God for their privileges and **patiently continue in doing good**.
