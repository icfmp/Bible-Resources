# James 2 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| All professions of faith are vain, if not producing love and justice to others. | 1–13 |
| The necessity of good works to prove the sincerity of faith, which otherwise will be of no more advantage than the faith of devils. | 14–26 |

## Faith must not lead us to favor the wealthy (verses 1–13)

Those professing faith in **Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory**, must not favor people according to wealth, clothing or social appearance. Such judgments contradict discipleship to **the lowly Jesus**.

This instruction does not justify rude or disorderly behavior. Ordinary civil respect remains appropriate.

But preference must never distort church appointments, the church's handling of offenses or any other matter of religion.

Careful self-examination helps us recognize these inconsistencies.

Places of worship require money to build and maintain. Henry allows that people who contribute may in some circumstances receive suitable accommodation. Nevertheless, **if Christians were more spiritually minded, poor worshipers would be treated with much greater attention**.

Lowly circumstances can favor **inward peace and growth in holiness**. God could provide every believer with worldly wealth if it would truly be good for them. Yet he has chosen people who are **rich in faith** and heirs of his kingdom.

Consider how wealth and worldly power can bring vice and disgrace upon religion.

God's **royal law** requires us to love our neighbor as ourselves. It comes from the King of kings. Anyone acting unjustly is condemned by it.

No number of good deeds can pay for an earlier sin. Under **the covenant of works**, breaking even one divine command brings condemnation that no past, present or future obedience can remove.

Those who are **in Christ** enjoy a different standing. They can serve God without slavish fear: God's commandments are not bondage, but their own sinful desires are.

Final judgment will be without mercy for those who remain unrepentant. Yet God delights to pardon and bless people who deserve condemnation. Everyone receiving his mercy should **show mercy in their conduct**.

## Living faith is proved through good works (verses 14–26)

It is wrong to mistake **mental agreement with the gospel** for the whole of Christianity.

True faith alone grants us a share in **Christ's righteousness, atonement and grace**, and **saves the soul**. But it produces holy fruit and reveals its reality in good works.

Merely agreeing that doctrines are true is very different from **saving faith**.

Outward profession can win the esteem of religious people or even worldly advantages. But what value is that if a person gains the world and loses the soul?

An opinion about the gospel, without obedience, is not living faith.

The believer must not simply **assent to what the Bible says** but **consent from the heart to receive Christ**. Genuine believing involves the whole person, not only the understanding.

**Abraham and Rahab** illustrate that justifying faith does not remain alone.

Abraham believed God, and this was **credited to him as righteousness**. His faith then produced obedience even when obedience tested his reason, affections and interests.

That is the meaning of justification through works in James: **not empty profession or faithless observance, but faith made visible through obedience**.

Rahab's actions showed how faith could change a sinner. Her deed demonstrated faith alive in the heart, not merely agreement in the mind.

The finest outward works without faith are dead because they lack the right root and purpose. But a claimed root of faith without fruit is also effectively dead.

**Faith is the root; good works are the fruit.** Both must be present.

The only alternatives are living as **God's friend** or remaining his enemy.

Faith that justifies and saves leads us to live **for God and toward God**, refusing what opposes him.
