# 1 Samuel 15 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Saul sent to destroy Amalek. | 1–9 |
| Saul excuses and commends himself. | 10–23 |
| Saul's imperfect humiliation. | 24–31 |
| Agag put to death, Samuel and Saul part. | 32–35 |

## Saul receives judgment against Amalek (verses 1–9)

God had announced judgment on the Amalekites long before (Exodus 17:14; Deuteronomy 25:19).

That judgment was delayed until their sins had reached their full measure.

Henry insists that the righteous Lord does no injustice.

The Kenites' ancestors had shown kindness to Israel.

God remembered that kindness and spared their descendants, even while judging Amalek for its ancestors' cruelty.

Henry sees this contrast as evidence of divine justice in this exceptional historical judgment.

It is dangerous to remain in the company of people under God's judgment.

We should separate from their wrongdoing rather than share in their sins and plagues (Revelation 18:4).

God's command to Saul was explicit and tested his obedience.

Saul's failure revealed a proud and rebellious heart.

He destroyed only the animals and goods that were least valuable.

He kept the better things for himself.

The things destroyed were understood in the narrative as given over to divine judgment.

The ancient command is described here as a historical act of judgment, **not a license for private violence or a modern religious campaign**.

## Samuel confronts Saul's self-justification (verses 10–23)

When Scripture speaks of **God repenting**, Henry explains that this does not mean God changes his mind as people do.

It means a change in the way God deals with people.

The change was in Saul: he had turned away from following the Lord.

Through his rebellion, he made God his enemy.

Samuel prayed for Saul through an entire night.

The rejection of sinners grieves believers.

God does not delight in their death, and neither should we.

Saul boasted that he had obeyed.

Sinners often try to escape God's judgment by defending themselves.

But the sound of the animals Saul had spared spoke against him, like rust testifying against a hoarder (James 5:3).

People often claim to obey God while their indulgence of sinful desires, love of the world, angry and unkind attitudes, and neglect of holy responsibilities testify otherwise.

Consider how much harm arises from greed!

The deepest evil of sin is **disobedience to God**.

Saul's hearts—and ours when ruled by fleshly desires—try to excuse disobedience by presenting it as something pleasing or useful.

It is difficult to persuade the disobedient that they are wrong.

But God values **humble, sincere, conscientious obedience** more than any burnt offering or sacrifice.

Obedience honors God and requires us to deny ourselves more than a visible ceremony does.

It is much easier to bring an animal to the altar than to bring every proud thought and our whole will into submission to God.

People unwilling to submit to God's rule are unworthy to rule other human beings.

## Saul's confession is insincere (verses 24–31)

Henry identifies **three signs of hypocrisy** in Saul's repentance.

**First**, Saul sought Samuel's approval and forgiveness, appearing more anxious about Samuel's opinion and favor than God's.

**Second**, he continued making excuses even while admitting wrongdoing.

That is not the response of genuine repentance.

**Third**, he cared chiefly about preserving his reputation and influence among the people.

Human beings are changeable and weak.

They alter their plans, fail to accomplish what they intend, and meet events they could not foresee.

But God is not like that.

The **Strength of Israel** does not lie or fail.

## Samuel judges Agag and mourns Saul (verses 32–35)

Many people imagine that their greatest danger is past when it is actually near.

They put the thought of death and judgment far away.

Samuel called Agag to account for his own evil acts.

Agag had followed the cruelty of his ancestors, and Henry understands the judgment upon him as reckoning with that history of bloodshed.

Saul seemed unconcerned that God had rejected him.

But Samuel grieved for Saul continually.

In a similar way, Jerusalem remained comfortably careless while Christ wept over it.

Do we truly desire to do **all** God's will?

Let us turn to him, not merely with outward forms and appearances, but sincerely.
