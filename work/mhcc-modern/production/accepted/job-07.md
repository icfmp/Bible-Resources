# Job 7 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Job's troubles. | 1–6 |
| Job expostulates with God. | 7–16 |
| He begs release. | 17–21 |

## Job describes life's exhausting labor (verses 1–6)

Job tried to excuse a wish he could not truly justify: the desire for death.

Remember where human beings live: **on earth**, not yet in hell.

Our time here has an appointed limit, determined by the One who created us and sent us.

Life during that time is a struggle.

We are like day laborers assigned work to be finished before night, when we must give an account.

Job thought he had as much reason to long for death as a tired laborer has to long for evening and sleep.

Rest is sweet to someone exhausted by work.

Even a wealthy person may not find as much enjoyment in riches as a laborer finds in earned wages.

Job complained that his days had become useless and had remained so for a long time.

Yet when we are no longer able to work actively for God, **quietly waiting for him** can still be acceptable service.

His nights were restless.

When something painful has been appointed for us, it helps to believe that it has some holy purpose.

When we enjoy comfortable nights, we should recognize them as gifts and give thanks.

Job's body had become repulsive to him.

What fragile bodies we inhabit!

Life was moving rapidly toward its end.

Each day leaves a thread behind, like a **weaver's shuttle**.

Many weave only a spider's web, which cannot endure (**Job 8:14**).

But if we live for God in faithful service and loving work, we will eventually reap what we have sown and wear what we have woven.

## Job reasons with God about mortality (verses 7–16)

Thinking personally about life's brevity, uncertainty and certain ending can make these truths useful to us.

We die only once.

Therefore we need to prepare well, because mistakes made at death cannot be corrected afterward.

Other clouds rise after one disappears, but the identical cloud never returns.

New generations arise, while earlier generations vanish from the earth.

Glorified believers never return to the grief and responsibilities of their old earthly homes.

Condemned sinners never return to their old entertainments and pleasures.

We should prepare for a **better dwelling place when we die**.

Job might have used these truths to reach a better conclusion than simply, **“I will complain.”**

With only a few breaths remaining, we ought to devote them to the holy breathings of faith and prayer, not words that express sinful rebellion.

We should ask the Keeper of Israel, who neither slumbers nor sleeps, to watch over us while **we** sleep.

Job wanted rest in the grave.

Henry judges that to have been weakness.

A godly person may choose death rather than sin, but should remain willing to live as long as God appoints, since earthly life gives opportunities to glorify him and prepare for heaven.

## Job seeks forgiveness (verses 17–21)

Job challenged God's treatment of human beings.

Yet amid the questioning his thoughts seem to have risen toward God with some faith and hope.

Notice how seriously Job regarded his sins.

Even the best believers have sins to confess, and the holier they become, the more aware they are of that sin.

God preserves human life and saves everyone who believes.

In this passage Henry considers it probable Job meant **the Observer of human beings**, whose eyes see everyone's conduct and heart.

Nothing can be hidden from him.

Let us plead guilty before his throne of grace, so that we do not stand condemned before his judgment seat.

Job denied his friends' charge that he was a hypocrite or wicked man.

Yet he admitted **to God** that he was a sinner.

Even the best people must do that.

Job wanted to know how to find peace with God and prayed urgently for forgiveness.

His desire was greater than relief from outward pain.

He wanted **God's favor to return**.

When God removes sin's guilt, he also breaks sin's power.

Job appealed to his approaching death as a reason to seek forgiveness now.

If his sins remained unforgiven when he died, he would be lost.

How miserable it is to live in sin without knowing the Savior!
