## Judas despairs after betraying an innocent man (verses 1–10)

People committing wicked acts rarely recognize all their consequences at the time.

But they must eventually answer for them.

Judas confessed openly to the chief priests that he had sinned by betraying **an innocent person**.

His words were a powerful testimony to Christ's character.

Yet the rulers remained hardened.

Judas threw down the payment and hanged himself, overcome by fear of divine judgment and despair.

Henry considers it very likely Judas died **before Jesus was crucified**.

The rulers who had hired Judas had themselves eagerly sought Christ's innocent blood, yet behaved as though his sin were no concern of theirs.

How readily people mock serious wrongdoing!

And how easily we reduce our sense of guilt by concentrating on **another person's sin** rather than our own.

But God's judgment follows truth.

Some interpreters understand the purchase of the burial field with Judas' returned money as a picture of the benefits Christ's blood would bring to strangers and Gentile sinners.

Henry mentions this as **an interpretation**, not an established fact about the land's original purpose.

The event is connected with prophecy (**Zechariah 11:12**).

Judas went a long way toward repentance, but not toward **saving repentance**.

He confessed sin, yet **not to God**.

He did not return to the heavenly Father pleading for mercy.

We should not mistake distress, confession and inward conviction for actual conversion while pride, hostility and rebellion still rule the heart.

## Jesus stands before Pilate (verses 11–25)

Pilate bore no personal malice toward Jesus and hoped that he might be acquitted.

His wife's message was another warning against condemning him.

God provides many restraints against sin through Providence, friends and conscience.

It is a mercy to hear the warning **“Do not do the evil thing that God hates”** before we act.

Under the religious leaders' influence the crowd demanded **Barabbas**.

They preferred a criminal to Christ.

People who prefer this world as their ruler and ultimate portion rather than God similarly choose what will ruin them.

Pilate saw the danger of resisting the crowd, but his hesitation also showed the power of conscience even in flawed rulers.

God arranged the events to make clear that **Jesus suffered not for wrongdoing of his own, but for the sins of his people**.

Pilate could not free himself from guilt merely by washing his hands.

As governor, he was responsible for protecting an innocent person.

Henry interprets the crowd's self-imprecation as connected with later national suffering.

That is his historical-theological claim about the narrative, not an assignment of guilt to Jews collectively in the present.

No ordinary person can bear the guilt of another.

Only **Christ, without any sin of his own, could answer for others' sins**.

All of us are involved in the moral lesson.

Whenever sinners reject Christ so they may keep beloved sins, they effectively choose **Barabbas instead of Jesus**.

Those sins rob God of his honor and destroy souls.

Christ's blood, which the crowd rejected, is made through mercy a source of blessing and refuge to all who trust him.

Let us flee to it.

## Barabbas is released and Jesus is mocked (verses 26–30)

The death by crucifixion described here was a terrible and degrading Roman punishment.

The victim was fastened to a cross that was then raised upright, leaving the person's weight hanging under severe pain.

Henry draws attention to this as the fulfillment of the figure of **the bronze serpent lifted on a pole**.

Jesus endured the humiliation and agony so that believers might receive **eternal life, joy and glory**.

## Jesus is led to the cross (verses 31–34)

Christ was taken to his death **like a lamb brought to slaughter and a sacrifice brought to the altar**.

Even the apparent mercies of the wicked can conceal cruelty.

The soldiers compelled **Simon** to carry the cross.

May the Lord make us willing to carry the cross appointed for us, taking it up daily with joy as we follow Jesus.

Has anyone experienced sorrow like the Savior's sorrow?

When we consider the manner of his death, let us understand the extraordinary **love** with which he loved us.

As though crucifixion itself were not painful enough, the soldiers added fresh bitterness and humiliation to it.

## Jesus is crucified and ridiculed (verses 35–44)

A notice placed over a criminal's head was meant to announce the crime and increase the shame of punishment.

The inscription over Jesus was intended as an insult.

Yet **God turned the accusation into an acknowledgment of Christ's honor**.

Two criminals were crucified beside him.

He was **numbered with transgressors** at his death, so that redeemed people might be numbered among the saints at their death.

His enemies mocked him.

People hostile to Christ often try to convince others of accusations against religion that they themselves know are false.

The religious leaders ridiculed his title **King of Israel**.

Many would accept a king who came down from the cross, offering a kingdom without the troubles through which believers must enter it.

But **without the cross there is no Christ and no crown**.

Those who expect to reign with Christ must be willing to suffer with him.

Jesus undertook to satisfy God's justice and submitted to the death of the worst criminals.

Details of his suffering fulfilled predictions in the **Prophets and Psalms**.

## Jesus dies (verses 45–50)

For **three hours** darkness covered the scene.

Henry understands that time as the most intense agony in which Jesus confronted the powers of darkness and experienced **the Father's displeasure against human sin**, bearing his soul as an offering.

There had never been such hours since humanity was created.

They were central to **the redemption and salvation of sinners**.

Jesus cried out using the words of **Psalm 22:1**.

He thereby teaches us to let Scripture guide our prayers, including in the deepest suffering.

Believers may have tasted drops of bitterness, but they can hardly comprehend the suffering Christ bore.

From it they learn something of his love and of sin's terrible evil.

They see their debt to the One who rescues them from coming judgment.

His enemies mocked the cry and misunderstood it.

Many insults against God's people and word arise from similarly gross misunderstandings.

Just before death Jesus cried with **full strength**.

His life was **not finally seized from him against his will**.

He gave it freely into the Father's hands.

He offered himself through the eternal Spirit as **both Priest and Sacrifice**.

Then he truly died.

His soul was separated from his body; his body was really dead.

His death was necessary because he had undertaken to become **an offering for sin**.

He completed that sacrifice by voluntarily surrendering his life.

## The veil tears and witnesses respond (verses 51–56)

The temple veil tearing signified that **Christ's death opened the way to God**.

Through him we may approach the throne of mercy now and later the throne of glory.

When we consider the crucifixion, our hearts should be **torn in repentance**, rather than merely our clothes.

A heart unmoved by the clearly proclaimed death of Jesus is harder than rock.

Graves opened and bodies of holy people who had died arose.

Scripture does **not** tell us exactly whom they visited, how they appeared or what later became of them.

We must not claim knowledge beyond what is written.

Extraordinary displays of divine Providence can awaken sinners and produce fear of God.

The centurion and soldiers were deeply affected.

The testimony about Jesus encourages us.

If we live for God without needlessly offending others, we may entrust our good names to him.

Let faith contemplate the crucified Christ and respond to his great love.

His faithful friends could only watch from a distance, unable to save him.

No event has displayed **the evil of sin** so powerfully as the beloved Son suffering on the cross, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God.

Let us willingly give ourselves to his service.

## Jesus is buried (verses 57–61)

Jesus' burial had no grand ceremony.

He had no house of his own in life and no grave of his own in death.

Having no personal sin, he nonetheless possessed no burial place.

His enemies intended that he be buried with criminals.

But God overruled their intentions, and Christ was laid in **a rich man's tomb**, fulfilling **Isaiah 53:9**.

Funerals can awaken fear of death.

Yet Christ's burial has transformed what the grave means for believers.

Remembering that change should give them hope.

We should also imitate his burial spiritually by continually putting **our own sins to death**.

## The tomb is sealed and guarded (verses 62–66)

On the Sabbath, when the chief priests and Pharisees ought to have been occupied with worship, they instead sought Pilate's help to secure Jesus' tomb.

Their actions were permitted to provide clearer evidence of Christ's resurrection.

Pilate allowed them to guard the burial place as carefully as they wished.

They sealed the stone and stationed guards.

They believed they had made the tomb secure.

Yet guarding it against the weak and frightened disciples was needless.

Trying to guard it against **God's power** was hopeless.

Their apparent wisdom became folly.

God catches the wise in their own schemes.

The furious plans of Christ's opponents ultimately serve **his glory**.
