# Judges 16 — Modern English

## Samson escapes from Gaza (verses 1–3)

Samson's character has appeared remarkable, even glorious in some respects.

But here he behaves so wickedly that some people question whether he was truly godly.

The apostle settles that question by including him among those commended for faith (Hebrews 11:32).

We can learn from this account by attending to Scripture's teaching about Satan's tricks, the heart's deceitfulness and God's ways of dealing with his people.

Some circumstances in Samson's time were exceptional.

Conduct connected with his calling would be gravely criminal in our time without a specific command from heaven.

Samson may also have shown genuine piety on occasions that Scripture does not record.

Yet his danger is plain.

People who indulge in drunkenness or sexual sin should recognize how they expose themselves to spiritual enemies seeking their ruin.

The more securely they sleep in their sins, the more dangerous their position becomes.

We may hope that when Samson awoke and saw his danger he resolved not to return to his wrongdoing.

It was bad to lie down without such fear of sin, but would have been worse to remain there without repentance.

## Delilah leads Samson into a trap (verses 4–17)

Samson had repeatedly suffered trouble through improper relationships with women.

Yet he ignored the warnings and fell into the same trap again.

This third occasion brought disaster.

Sexual immorality is one of the sins that steals the heart.

Many people fall into this deep pit.

Few escape, except through an extraordinary mercy that often costs nearly everything: reputation, usefulness and outward well-being, even where the soul is saved.

The pain that follows such sin may be **ten thousand times greater** than the brief pleasure it provided.

## Samson is blinded and imprisoned (verses 18–21)

False security has deadly consequences.

Satan flatters people into believing they are safe.

They stop watching and fearing.

Then he takes away their strength and honor and leads them captive.

While we sleep spiritually, our enemies remain awake.

Samson's eyes had first drawn him into sin.

Now his punishment fell upon those eyes: the Philistines blinded him.

With his physical sight gone, he had time to remember how earlier sinful desires had already blinded his judgment.

The surest way to preserve our eyes is to turn them away from worthless sights.

Let Samson's fall warn us to watch diligently against sinful physical desires.

When our consecration to God is profaned, our spiritual protection departs.

## Samson's strength begins to return (verses 22–24)

Samson's suffering became the occasion for deep repentance.

Losing physical sight opened the eyes of his understanding.

After losing bodily strength, he received renewed spiritual strength from the Lord.

God sometimes permits people to wander far into sin, yet finally brings them back.

Through severe earthly consequences, he shows his displeasure at sin while keeping them from eternal ruin.

Hypocrites may misuse such stories as excuses to sin.

Unbelievers may mock them.

But faithful Christians should become more humble, cautious, watchful and dependent upon God.

They should pray more earnestly for grace to avoid falling and thank God for preserving them.

If they do fall, they should not despair of his mercy.

## Samson's final prayer and death (verses 25–31)

Few actions increase a person's guilt more quickly than mocking and mistreating God's servants, even when those servants have brought disgrace upon themselves through their own folly.

Henry understands Samson as a **publicly appointed judge** acting under God's direction to vindicate the Lord's cause, Israel's cause and his own.

The strength Samson lost through sin was restored through prayer.

Henry argues that his action arose from holy zeal rather than merely private revenge because God accepted and answered the prayer.

The building collapsed through **God's almighty power**, not Samson's natural strength alone.

In this exceptional historical situation, Henry does not regard Samson as guilty of taking his own life.

He sought Israel's deliverance and the defeat of its enemies, rather than his own death.

Samson died in captivity among the Philistines—a severe correction for his sins.

Yet he died repentant.

Henry sees his death as a picture of Christ, who willingly laid down his life among transgressors, overthrew Satan's kingdom and secured deliverance for his people.

Samson's sin was great, and he deserved the judgments he brought upon himself.

Nevertheless, he found mercy at the end.

Everyone who repents and flees to Jesus Christ—the Savior whose blood cleanses from every sin—may also obtain mercy.

**But nothing in Samson's story gives anyone permission to continue sinning on the assumption that repentance and salvation can be postponed until the end.**
