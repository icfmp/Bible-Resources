# Isaiah 7 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Ahaz threatened by Israel and Syria; and is assured their attack would be in vain. | 1–9 |
| God gives a sure sign by the promise of the long-expected Messiah. | 10–16 |
| The folly and sin of seeking relief from Assyria are reproved. | 17–25 |

## God promises that the attack will fail (verses 1–9)

Ungodly people are often punished through others who are just as wicked.

The people of Judah were terrified by the armies of Israel and Syria.

Having made God their enemy, they did not know how to find his favor again.

Isaiah was called to teach them **confidence in God's protection**, rather than fear of their enemies.

Ahaz regarded the attacking kings as powerful. The prophet dismissed them as **the smoking ends of two burned-out torches**.

The kingdoms of Syria and Israel were already nearing their end.

Worldly rulers may for a time accomplish the purposes God permits, bringing destruction to others. But when their work is complete, he can extinguish their power.

Their attack on Judah would fail because they had devised an evil plan that offended God.

**Human beings make plans; God decides their outcome.**

How foolish for rulers already close to ruin to plan the destruction of their neighbors!

Isaiah therefore urged the people to believe the Lord's promise. **Faith is necessary to quiet the heart during severe trouble.**

## The sign of the promised Messiah (verses 10–16)

People who inwardly distrust God sometimes disguise that unbelief as reverence.

They refuse to trust his word yet claim they are unwilling to **test** him.

Isaiah rebuked Ahaz and his court for treating divine revelation so lightly.

Distrust greatly dishonors God. But human unbelief cannot cancel his promise.

The Lord himself would give a sign.

The Messiah was to be born from this people. **Judah could not be completely destroyed before that blessing was fulfilled.**

The promise points to a glorious birth and gives comfort through **Christ, our relationship to him and our hope in him**.

He would grow as other children did, eating ordinary food in his country. Yet unlike fallen children, he would **always reject evil and choose good**.

His birth would be accomplished by the **Holy Spirit's power**, but he would not need extraordinary food from angels.

Then Isaiah speaks of the nearer, swift defeat of Judah's two enemies.

Henry offers a **possible reading** of the immediate sign: before **Shear-jashub**, the prophet's young son present with him (**Isaiah 7:3**), grew three or four years older, the two hostile kings would no longer rule their forces.

This is presented as **Henry's interpretation**, not an identification of Shear-jashub with the promised Messiah.

Because God supplied the sign even after Ahaz had refused to ask, the prophecy could raise hope far beyond the immediate political crisis.

For believers awaiting the Messiah, that hope was a lasting comfort.

How thankful we should be that **the Word became flesh**! May we trust him, love him and follow his example.

## The cost of relying on Assyria (verses 17–25)

People unwilling to believe God's promises should expect his warnings to become frighteningly real.

No one can escape his judgments.

The Lord can sweep away a nation's supports and use whatever instruments he chooses, repaying or dismissing them as he wills.

Judah's pleasant land would be transformed. **Agriculture would cease**, and former fields would become desolate.

What sorrow can sin not bring upon a people?

Those who neglect the great salvation place themselves in danger of every kind of misery.

If we continually remain unfruitful despite enjoying the means of grace, we should tremble at the warning that fruit may never grow from us again.
