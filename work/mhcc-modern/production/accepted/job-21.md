# Job 21 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Job entreats attention. | 1–6 |
| The prosperity of the wicked. | 7–16 |
| The dealings of God's providence. | 17–26 |
| The judgement of the wicked is in the world to come. | 27–34 |

## Job asks his friends to listen (verses 1–6)

Job came directly to the central issue.

Does outward prosperity prove that someone belongs to God's true people, so that the loss of prosperity proves hypocrisy?

His friends answered **yes**. Job answered **no**.

Anyone looking at Job could see enough suffering to deserve sympathy.

Instead of speaking with such certainty about God's mysterious purposes, his friends should have responded with reverent silence and wonder.

## Wicked people may prosper (verses 7–16)

Job observed that notorious sinners sometimes experience extraordinary judgments, but **not always**.

Why does God allow some wicked people to prosper?

This is a time of divine patience.

God can use even the prosperity of ungodly people to carry out his plans, while their unrepentance moves them toward destruction.

Another reason is to show clearly that **there is a world beyond this one**.

Some wealthy sinners disregard God and religion, imagining that because they have everything they want in this world, they have no need to consider the next.

Yet true religion is not empty.

If it brings us no benefit, we may have only embraced **its outward appearance** without its living reality.

Job exposed the folly of such confidence.

## Providence deals differently with different sinners (verses 17–26)

Job contrasted wicked people's prosperity with his friends' assertion that they must always be visibly ruined in this life.

This did not contradict God's holiness or justice.

Even when wicked people prosper, their condition remains spiritually empty.

Their power and splendor can disappear in a moment.

Why one evil person enjoys a longer period of prosperity while another suffers early belongs to **God's wisdom**.

He is the Judge of the whole earth, and he will act justly.

Time is so brief compared with eternity that differences in earthly circumstances become small before final judgment.

Whether one unrepentant sinner dies in a palace and another in a prison, neither can escape the eternal consequence of continuing in sin.

We should therefore avoid becoming consumed with questions about why earthly fortunes differ.

## Judgment awaits beyond the grave (verses 27–34)

Job rejected the idea that all wicked people must suffer a visible and dramatic fall in this life, and that only wicked people suffer such a fall.

This was the false assumption his friends used to condemn him.

The full punishment of unrepentant sinners belongs chiefly **to the world to come**, not necessarily to present life (**Jude 1:14–15**).

A wicked person may retain power until death and receive a magnificent funeral.

But what is there to boast about in that?

A splendid tomb and a beautiful burial place with water to keep its grass green were considered honorable in the ancient East.

They are still empty earthly distinctions.

Death brings worldly prosperity to an end.

The fact that others died before us is little comfort when we ourselves approach death.

True courage at that hour comes from remembering that **Jesus Christ died and was buried not only before us but for us**.

He went ahead of us, died for us and now lives for us.

That is genuine comfort in the hour of death.
