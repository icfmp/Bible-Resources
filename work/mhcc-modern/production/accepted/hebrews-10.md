# Hebrews 10 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The insufficiency of sacrifices for taking away sin, The necessity and power of the sacrifice of Christ for that purpose. | 1–18 |
| An argument for holy boldness in the believer's access to God through Jesus Christ, And for steadfastness in faith, and mutual love and duty. | 19–25 |
| The danger of apostacy. | 26–31 |
| The sufferings of believers, and encouragement to maintain their holy profession. | 32–39 |

## Repeated offerings cannot remove sin (verses 1–10)

The tabernacle and rituals of the **Sinai covenant** were signs and pictures of the gospel.

The sacrifices repeatedly offered by Israel's high priests could not make worshipers perfect through **the pardon of sins and cleansing of conscience**.

But **God manifested in the flesh** became the sacrifice.

Christ's voluntary death upon the cursed tree served as the ransom.

Because the sufferer possessed **infinite worth**, his willing suffering had an infinite saving value.

A true atoning substitute had to be capable of **consenting freely to stand in sinners' place**; Jesus did so.

Everything Christ accomplished comes from **God's sovereign will and grace**.

The righteousness he brought and the sacrifice he offered once have **lasting, eternal power**.

Through his blood those coming to God receive inner comfort, forgiveness and strength and motivation for obedience.

## One sacrifice secures full and final pardon (verses 11–18)

The gospel covenant brings **complete and final forgiveness**, unlike the former order.

Under the law, offerings had to be repeated. They provided at most the forms of pardon belonging to that earthly dispensation.

But **one sacrifice of Jesus Christ** secures spiritual forgiveness for people from **every age and nation**, rescuing them from judgment beyond this life.

No human ceremony or invention can replace his sacrifice.

We must therefore trust this one Savior and seek assurance of his work through **the Spirit's sanctification producing obedience**.

As God's law is written on our hearts, we may know we are justified and that he will **remember our sins no more**.

## Draw near and encourage one another (verses 19–25)

Having explained Christ's sacrifice, the writer applies it to Christian living.

Since believers have an open way into God's presence, they **must make use of it**.

We come through **Jesus' blood and its atoning merit**.

The harmony of God's absolute holiness and forgiving mercy became clear through the suffering of **his incarnate Son**.

Heaven's way is through **the crucified Savior**; his death becomes life for believers.

To stay needlessly far away would show disrespect for Christ's welcome.

The call for bodies washed in pure water recalls the law's purifications; **baptism's water reminds Christians to live in holiness**.

Receiving comfort and grace from our reconciled Father, we should adorn his teaching in every respect.

Think actively about how to serve one another.

**Stir one another up toward love and good works**; fellowship among believers is a powerful aid to perseverance.

As trials and death approach, become more diligent, not less.

## The danger of deliberate apostasy (verses 26–31)

The warning concerns **a complete, final rejection of the Savior**.

Henry describes people who **knowingly and decisively despise Christ**, the only Savior; **resist the Spirit**, the only Sanctifier; and **renounce the gospel**, the only saving way.

Some hardened sinners taste terrible forebodings of judgment even during earthly life.

Dying after refusing divine mercy is more dreadful than suffering ordinary punishment.

Astonishingly, not only God's justice but **his despised grace and mercy** testify against such rejection.

Yet none of this means that a **sinner grieved over sin** must be excluded from mercy.

No one willing to accept Christ's sacrifice will be refused its benefit.

**Jesus never casts out those who come to him.**

## Persevere through suffering until the promised reward (verses 32–39)

Early Christians endured many kinds of suffering and difficult struggles.

Christian love is not selfish: it makes us **pity, visit, help and intercede for fellow sufferers**.

Earthly possessions are only shadows; heavenly happiness lasts forever and cannot be taken away by enemies.

It will richly repay every loss endured here.

For now, much of the believer's happiness remains **in promise**.

Even after our work seems finished, patience may require us to live on and wait until God's time for our reward.

Christ will come for believers at death, end their troubles and give the crown of life.

The struggle may be fierce, but it is brief.

God is not pleased with an outward religious profession that does **not persevere**.

Those sustained by grace through earlier trials can hope for grace to continue **living and dying by faith**, until they receive the salvation of their souls.
