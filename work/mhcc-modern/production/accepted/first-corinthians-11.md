# 1 Corinthians 11 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The apostle, after an exhortation to follow him, | 1 |
| corrects some abuses. | 2–16 |
| Also contentions, divisions, and disorderly celebrations of the Lord's supper. | 17–22 |
| He reminds them of the nature and design of its institution. | 23–26 |
| And directs how to attend upon it in a due manner. | 27–34 |

## Imitate Paul only as he imitates Christ (verse 1)

Henry believes this opening verse **properly belongs with the previous chapter's conclusion**.

Paul preached teachings the Corinthians ought to believe, but also lived in a way they ought to follow.

Nevertheless, **Christ is our perfect example**.

We should imitate the actions of human beings described in Scripture **only insofar as those actions resemble Christ's**.

## Order and appropriate dress in worship (verses 2–16)

Paul now begins considering abuses in **public Christian gatherings**, a subject continued in chapter 14.

Corinthian believers had received many spiritual gifts but had fallen into serious mistakes in using them.

As Christ did the Father's will and sought God's glory, Christians must openly acknowledge their submission to Christ and seek his honor.

Even our **clothing and outward conduct** should avoid bringing dishonor upon him.

Henry teaches that the woman was created **to assist and comfort the man**, and therefore should not conduct herself in public worship as if claiming independence from the order God appointed.

He interprets **“power” on the woman's head** as **a veil**, worn because of **the angels** who are present as witnesses of worship.

Their presence should restrain believers from misconduct in the assembly.

Yet Henry immediately adds that **man and woman were created for one another**.

They ought to bring **mutual comfort and blessing**, not live **as a slave and a tyrant**.

God's ordering of leadership and submission, both in Providence and in grace, aims at **mutual assistance and benefit**.

In the churches of that time, women commonly attended and participated in public worship **wearing veils**, and Henry considers it right that they did so.

Christianity generally permits a nation's social customs when they do not contradict **truth and holiness**.

It does not endorse deliberate eccentricity merely for the sake of appearing different.

## Divisions and selfishness at the Lord's table (verses 17–22)

Paul rebuked abuses of the Lord's Supper.

When ordinances do not help people grow spiritually, they may instead harden them.

Corinthian gatherings were marked by **divisions and disputes**.

People can worship in separate congregations while still loving one another.

They can also remain together in the same congregation while behaving without love.

Henry judges that **this lack of charity is the more serious kind of schism**.

An unworthy and careless way of receiving the Supper adds guilt.

Some wealthy Corinthians treated poorer Christians shamefully during the meal or accompanying **love feast**.

They consumed the food they had brought before poorer members could share it.

Some remained hungry while others had more than enough.

What ought to have strengthened fellowship became a cause of division.

Avoid any conduct that dishonors the sacred meal.

We may not commonly turn it into an occasion of gluttony now.

But do we sometimes turn it into **a support for self-righteous pride or a cover for hypocrisy**?

Do not rest in outward forms of worship.

**Examine the heart**.

## Christ's institution and proper reception of the Supper (verses 23–34)

Paul knew the Lord's Supper through **revelation from Christ**.

Its visible signs are **bread and wine**.

The food eaten is still **called bread** even when Christ calls it his body.

Henry understands this as clear evidence that **the bread is not literally transformed into flesh**.

Matthew records Christ commanding the disciples **all to drink from the cup (Matthew 26:27)**.

Henry takes this wording as opposing the withholding of the cup from any believer.

These outward signs represent **Christ's body broken and blood shed**, along with every blessing flowing from his death and atoning sacrifice.

Christ took bread and cup, gave thanks, broke the bread and distributed both bread and cup.

Believers are to **take, eat and drink**, doing so **in remembrance of him**.

Yet the visible actions are not the whole or even the principal part of the ordinance.

The believer must receive **Christ as Lord and Life**, surrender to him and draw nourishment from him through faith.

The Supper remembers **Christ dying for us** and **Christ now interceding at God's right hand** on the basis of his sacrifice.

It also celebrates God's grace in our redemption.

We declare that **Christ's death is our life**, the source of hope and spiritual comfort.

We proclaim his death and plead it as **the accepted sacrifice and ransom**.

This ordinance was not appointed for only a few years.

It continues as part of the church's life.

Paul then warns against receiving it with a corrupt attitude, maintaining fellowship with sin and death while claiming to renew covenant fellowship with God.

Such hypocrisy is seriously guilty and may lead to divine judgment.

But the warning must not discourage **genuine believers who fear their own weakness** from attending the Supper.

**The Holy Spirit did not cause this passage to be written to frighten sincere Christians away from their duty**.

Satan has often misused it for precisely that purpose.

Paul was speaking to believers and warning them about **temporal judgments** through which God disciplined his offending servants.

In judgment, **God remembers mercy**.

He often corrects those he loves.

It is better to endure suffering now than to remain miserable forever.

Therefore **self-examination is necessary** for a right approach to the Lord's table.

If we examine ourselves thoroughly, condemn what is sinful and correct our ways, we may avoid divine discipline.

Paul concludes by warning them not to repeat the disordered practices that brought dishonor upon the Supper.

May our worship never become an occasion for provoking the Lord's judgment.

**Full original-English CCEL witness read:** [CCEL, 1 Corinthians 11](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc.xxxviii.xi.html). Published `MHCC 0.1.1` remains the source of record; original-native keys/markup and independent editorial acceptance pending.

- **Verse 1:** Henry considers it **end of previous chapter**, imitation of Paul conditional on **his imitation of Christ** — retained.
- **2–16:** worship setting, Henry's **veil/angels interpretation**, order **for mutual good rather than slavery/tyranny**, Christian respect for lawful customs — retained as the commentator's interpretation.
- **17–22:** **schism in loveless same-congregation relations**, rich shaming poor at **love feast**, warning against pride/hypocrisy — retained.
- **23–34:** critical **bread remains bread** and **all believers receive cup**, external bread/wine vs inward faith, Supper **remembers death and present intercession**, disciplinary **temporal** judgments, **no exclusion of tender-conscienced believers**, self-examination — retained.
