# 1 Samuel 25 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Death of Samuel. | 1 |
| David's request; Nabal's churlish refusal. | 2–11 |
| David's intention to destroy Nabal. | 12–17 |
| Abigail takes a present to David. | 18–31 |
| He is pacified, Nabal dies. | 32–39 |
| David takes Abigail to wife. | 39–44 |

## Samuel dies (verse 1)

All Israel mourned Samuel, and with good reason.

He had prayed for the people every day.

People have hard hearts when they can bury faithful ministers without sorrow and fail to feel the loss of those who prayed for them and taught them the Lord's way.

## Nabal refuses David's request (verses 2–11)

We might never have heard of Nabal if nothing had happened between him and David.

His name means **“fool.”**

Wealth makes people appear impressive in the world's eyes, but to someone who judges rightly, Nabal was a very small man.

He had no honor or honesty. He was rude, quarrelsome, ill-tempered, oppressive and willing to use fraud or violence to gain and preserve possessions.

What reason have we to admire worldly wealth when such a man can be rich while a good man such as David lacks necessities?

David pointed to the protection and kindness he had given Nabal's shepherds.

David's followers were themselves distressed, indebted and discontented, while supplies were scarce.

His careful leadership had kept them from taking what belonged to others.

But Nabal responded angrily to a request for help.

Greedy people often try to cover one sin with another, abusing the poor as a way of excusing their refusal to help them.

God is not deceived by that behavior.

We should learn to endure insults and false portrayals cheerfully and patiently, as many excellent believers have had to do.

Nabal insisted that the food on his table belonged to him.

But we are mistaken if we imagine that owning something gives us unlimited moral authority to use it however we please.

We are only **stewards** of possessions entrusted to us by their true Owner.

## David plans revenge (verses 12–17)

God is kind even to the wicked and ungrateful. Why should we not act similarly?

David, however, decided to kill Nabal and everyone belonging to his household.

Could this really be David, who had spent so long suffering and should have learned patience?

Usually calm and thoughtful, he now became so enraged by a few offensive words that he was ready to destroy a whole family.

Even the best believers may show terrible corruption when God allows them to discover what is in their own hearts.

How urgently we need to pray: **Lord, do not lead us into temptation.**

## Abigail brings a gift and speaks wisely (verses 18–31)

Abigail's gift made up for Nabal's refusal.

She approached David with great humility, speaking as someone asking for pardon.

She could not defend her husband's conduct.

Rather than trusting the cleverness of her own arguments, she depended on God's grace to soften David's anger.

She reminded David that taking revenge on a weak and contemptible opponent like Nabal was beneath him.

Nabal would not do him good, but neither could he truly harm him.

God would preserve David and bring him through his present troubles to a glorious future.

It would therefore be wrong for David to take away anyone's life unjustly or unnecessarily, especially the lives of God's own people.

Abigail's strongest argument came last.

**Refusing revenge would protect David's peace and conscience.**

Many people have acted in fury and later regretted the decision a thousand times.

The sweetness of vengeance quickly becomes bitter.

Whenever we feel tempted to sin, we should ask how the action will appear to us afterward.

## David is restrained; Nabal dies (verses 32–39)

David thanked God for sending someone to stop him before he committed a grievous sin.

Whenever we receive helpful counsel, encouragement, warning or timely correction, we should recognize God's kindness in sending it.

We should be thankful for providential events that prevent us from sinning.

Most people consider it enough to endure criticism patiently.

Few are grateful for correction or praise the person who gives it as a friend.

Yet **the closer we have come to doing wrong, the greater the mercy of being stopped**.

Nabal, meanwhile, was drunk and felt completely secure.

He could not enjoy abundance with moderation or be pleasant with his companions without becoming beastlike through excess.

Nothing more surely reveals how little wisdom a person has—or destroys what wisdom remains—than habitual drunkenness.

The next morning his joy gave way to terror.

His heart, lively with wine the previous evening, became heavy as stone.

Such is the deception of fleshly pleasures: laughter quickly gives way to grief.

About ten days later, the Lord struck Nabal and he died.

David thanked God that he himself had been prevented from killing him.

Worldly disappointment, injured pride or a terrified conscience can end the pleasure of someone who lives for appetite and wealth.

But whatever instrument is used, death comes when God appoints it.

## David marries Abigail (verses 39–44)

Abigail believed that David would become Israel's king.

She highly valued his godly and excellent character.

Although he was now in distress, she regarded his marriage proposal as an honor and a benefit.

She agreed humbly, in a way consistent with the customs of the time, and was willing to share his trials.

Those who unite themselves to Christ must similarly be willing **to suffer with him now**, trusting that afterward **they will reign with him**.
