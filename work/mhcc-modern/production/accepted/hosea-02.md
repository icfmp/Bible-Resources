# Hosea 2 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The idolatry of the people. | 1–5 |
| God's judgments against them. | 6–13 |
| His promises of reconciliation. | 14–23 |

## Israel is charged with unfaithfulness (verses 1–5)

This chapter continues the picture of Israel as an unfaithful wife, with Hosea's children representing the people.

We should **recognize and love as brothers and sisters** everyone whom the Lord appears to place among his children, encouraging them in the mercy they have received.

Yet every Christian must also oppose wrongdoing through **example and conduct**, even among people to whom we belong and whom we should respect.

Unrepentant sinners will eventually lose the privileges they misuse and spend upon **their sinful desires**.

## God's discipline interrupts sinful pursuits (verses 6–13)

God warned what he would do to this unfaithful, idolatrous people.

They refused to turn back, so the promised consequences came upon them.

Their history is recorded to warn us.

If people overcome smaller obstacles in pursuing sin, God may raise up **greater obstacles**.

Those most determined to follow sinful paths often encounter the most frustration.

When a path is blocked by thorns, we may have reason to suspect that **the path itself is wrong**.

Obstacles placed in the way of sin can be great blessings.

They are **God's hedges**, protecting us by making wrongdoing difficult.

We should thank him for grace that restrains us and for the providential events that keep us from evil—even **pain, illness or calamity**, if they prevent further sin.

When created things disappoint our longing for happiness, those disappointments should lead us **back to the Creator**.

People who forget that their comforts come from God may discover that he removes those comforts **in mercy**, bringing them to recognize their folly and danger.

Sin and merriment cannot remain together indefinitely.

If people refuse to take sin away from their pleasures, **God will take pleasure away from their sins**.

If they destroy the honor of God's word and worship, it is just for him to destroy **their vines and fig trees**, taking away what makes them happy.

Removing the sacred feasts and Sabbaths may not disturb them—they already care little for such things.

But when God removes their **sensual pleasures**, sorrow will replace sinful celebration.

## God's promises of reconciliation (verses 14–23)

After judgment, the Lord promised to deal gently with Israel.

**Christ's promise of rest** invites us to take his yoke upon us.

Conversion may be encouraged by **comfort and kindness**, not only by the conviction of sin.

Yet often God brings us to the end of confidence in earthly joy and in our own strength.

When every other door closes, we finally knock **at the gate of mercy**.

Israel would then love God more sincerely.

Instead of calling him **Baali**, “my lord and master,” emphasizing authority, the people would call him **Ishi**, a name expressing **affection**.

Henry sees possible fulfillments in **the return from Babylon**, **Jewish conversion to Christ during the apostolic age**, and **a future general conversion of the Jewish nation**.

Believers can expect **greater tenderness and care from their holy God** than a beloved wife receives from even the kindest husband.

When the people put away idols and loved the Lord, no creature would finally harm them.

These promises can be understood of **spiritual Israel**, of every genuine believer receiving **Christ's righteousness**, and of future Jewish conversion.

There is also a call to live in a way that does not disgrace the God who says **“You are my people.”**

A household behaving shamefully dishonors its head.

If God calls us his children, let us respond, **“You are our God.”**

Discouraged sinner, do not answer God's love with unbelief.

When he says, **“You are my people,”** answer in faith, **“Lord, you are our God.”**
