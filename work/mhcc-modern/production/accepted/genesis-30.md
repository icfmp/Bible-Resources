# Genesis 30 — Modern English

| Original outline | Verses |

## Rivalry in Jacob's household (verses 1–13)

Rachel envied her sister.

Envy means grieving over the good another person enjoys.

Few sins are more offensive to God or more harmful to our neighbors and ourselves.

Rachel failed to recognize that God had made the difference between her and Leah, and that she herself had advantages in other respects.

Let us carefully guard our minds against every beginning and movement of envy.

We should never resent the blessings given to our fellow servants simply because our Master has chosen to be good to them.

Jacob loved Rachel. That is why he rebuked her when she spoke wrongly.

Faithful correction can be evidence of genuine affection.

God can take the place of anything created that we lose.

But it is sinful and foolish to put a created thing in God's place, giving it the trust that belongs to God alone.

At Rachel's insistence, Jacob took her servant Bilhah as another wife.

By the custom of the time, Bilhah's children could be regarded as Rachel's children.

If Rachel had not been driven by sinful passions, she would have recognized that her sister's children were closer to her and had a stronger claim upon her love.

But children whom she could claim authority over were more attractive to her than children whom she had greater reason to love.

One early expression of that authority was her pleasure in giving Bilhah's children names that expressed rivalry with Leah.

See how envy and competition become roots of bitterness and bring trouble even among close relatives!

At Leah's insistence, Jacob then took Leah's servant Zilpah as another wife.

Consider the power of jealousy and competition.

And admire the wisdom of God's original appointment, which joins one man and one woman in marriage.

God has called us to peace and purity.

## Rachel bears Joseph (verses 14–24)

The sisters' desire to become mothers of the promised Seed was good in itself.

But that desire could become excessive and disordered.

They also wanted the honor of many children and feared the shame of childlessness.

These desires helped produce their unworthy rivalry.

It seems clear that both sisters were influenced by God's promises to Abraham.

His descendants had been promised extraordinary blessings, and the Messiah was to come through his family.

## Jacob's new agreement with Laban (verses 25–43)

After fourteen years of service, Jacob was ready to leave with no provision except God's promise.

Yet Laban owed him a fair reward for years of valuable work, and God intended Jacob to receive provision from it.

Rather than agree to fixed wages with a man as selfish as Laban, Jacob entrusted his cause to God.

He appears to have acted honestly, keeping only those animals whose colors had been specified in the agreement.

Laban selfishly assumed that the flocks would produce very few animals with colors different from their parents'.

Jacob's actions after the agreement have often been described as clever management.

But Henry understands them as something done at God's prompting and as a sign of his power.

In one way or another, the Lord will defend the cause of people who are oppressed and honor those who place simple trust in his providence.

Laban had no just complaint against Jacob.

Jacob received nothing more than Laban had freely agreed to give him.

Indeed, far from being harmed, Laban had greatly benefited from Jacob's work.

May we receive every blessing with thanksgiving and prayer.

Since our blessings come from God's generosity, let them always lead us back to his praise.
