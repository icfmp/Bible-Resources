# Exodus 15 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The song of Moses for the deliverance of Israel. | 1–21 |
| The bitter waters at Marah, The Israelites come to Elim. | 22–27 |

## Moses sings of Israel's deliverance (verses 1–21)

This is the oldest song known to us, Henry says.

It is a holy song entirely directed toward honoring God, lifting up his name and celebrating his praise.

It does not seek to magnify a human being in the least.

Every part of it declares holiness to the Lord.

The song can also be understood as a picture and prophecy of the final destruction of the church's enemies.

Happy are the people whose God is the Lord!

They have work to do, temptations to resist and troubles to bear. Left to themselves, they are weak; but God's grace is their strength.

They often experience sorrow, yet find comfort in him. He is their song.

Sin, death and hell threaten them, but God is and will remain their salvation.

The Lord is almighty. Woe to those who fight against their Maker!

He is perfect beyond comparison—glorious in holiness. His holiness is his glory.

That holiness appears in his hatred of sin and his wrath against stubborn sinners.

It appears also in Israel's deliverance and his faithfulness to his promises.

He is fearful in praise: the very works that cause his servants to praise him are terrifying to his enemies.

He performs wonders beyond the ordinary course of nature.

His people marvel because they have received such undeserved favor—far more than they had any reason to expect.

These are wonders of power and wonders of grace.

In both, God deserves to be worshiped with humility.

## Bitter water at Marah; refreshment at Elim (verses 22–27)

In the wilderness of Shur, the Israelites had no water.

When they reached Marah, they found water, but it was too bitter to drink.

God can make the very things in which we expect the most comfort become bitter to us.

He often does so during our journey through the wilderness of this world, so that our needs and disappointments in created things may drive us to the Creator.

Only in his favor can we find lasting comfort.

In their distress the people became irritated and quarreled with Moses.

Hypocrites may show strong religious emotions and seem eager for worship, yet fall away when temptation comes.

Even true believers may be tempted to irritation, distrust and complaint when trials become severe.

In every trial, we should cast our cares upon the Lord and pour out our hearts before him.

A submissive will, a peaceful conscience and the Holy Spirit's comfort can make even the bitterest suffering bearable—indeed, sometimes pleasant.

Moses did what the people had neglected to do: he cried out to the Lord.

God graciously provided what they needed.

He directed Moses to a tree, which Moses threw into the water. Immediately the water became sweet.

**Some interpreters**, Henry explains, understand the tree as a picture of Christ's cross.

On that reading, the cross sweetens the bitter waters of suffering for believers and enables them to rejoice even in tribulation.

But a rebellious Israelite will fare no better than a rebellious Egyptian.

The warning of punishment is implicit; the promise is stated plainly.

God is the great Physician.

When we remain healthy, it is he who preserves us. When we recover from illness, it is he who restores us.

He is our life and the length of our days.

We must not forget that he rescues us from destruction and delivers us from our enemies so that we may serve him.

At Elim the Israelites found good water, and plenty of it.

God may allow his people to camp for a time beside the bitter waters of Marah, but that will not always be their condition.

Let us not lose heart in trouble.
