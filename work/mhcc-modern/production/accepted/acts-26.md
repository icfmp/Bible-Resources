## Paul defends himself before Agrippa (verses 1–11)

Christianity teaches us to explain the reason for our hope, while also giving proper honor to those entitled to it—**without flattery or fear of human beings**.

Agrippa knew the Old Testament well and was therefore better equipped to judge the dispute about whether Jesus was the Messiah.

Surely ministers preaching faith in Christ may expect to receive a patient hearing.

Paul affirmed that he retained **everything good** in the religious training he had received.

He had lived morally and avoided the public wickedness and cunning greed of some Pharisees.

He had also held sound religious beliefs and revered God's ancient promise to the fathers, making that promise the foundation of his hope.

Paul understood that **none of this former morality could justify him before God**.

Yet he mentioned it because it disproved his enemies' charges and could bring honor to Christ.

Though he counted these things as loss in order to gain Christ, they were relevant to his defense.

His Christian faith did not continue his youthful zeal for the ceremonial law.

The sacrifices and offerings had been fulfilled and superseded by **the great Sacrifice they foreshadowed**.

He no longer treated the ceremonial washings as binding, because Christ's priesthood had fulfilled the Levitical priesthood.

But the **great principles of true religion** remained those for which he had always cared.

**Christ and heaven** are two central doctrines of the gospel.

God has given eternal life, and that life is in his Son.

These were the matters promised to the fathers.

Israel's continual religious duties testified to faith in that promise.

The prospect of eternal life should move us to steadfast devotion.

The Sadducees opposed Paul for teaching resurrection; other Jewish leaders joined them because he proclaimed **Jesus as risen and as Israel's promised Redeemer**.

Many divine promises seem impossible only because people forget **the infinite power and perfection of the God who promised them**.

Paul acknowledged that during his days as a Pharisee, he had been a bitter enemy of Christianity.

Everything in his background seemed opposed to conversion.

Even people who were outwardly strict before conversion may later find much to repent of, including actions they previously regarded as religious duties.

## Paul recounts his conversion and mission (verses 12–23)

Paul became a Christian through **God's power**, when Christ revealed himself both **to him and within him**, even while Paul was actively pursuing sin.

He became a minister through **Christ's authority**, because the same Lord who appeared in heavenly glory commanded him to preach to Gentiles.

The world lies in darkness and must receive light.

People ignorant of the way to eternal peace must be taught it.

But the world also lies in sin and needs **sanctification and reform**.

It is not enough to open people's eyes; **their hearts must be renewed**.

They must be brought not only from **darkness to light**, but also from **Satan's power to God**.

Everyone genuinely turned from sin to God receives more than pardon: a rich eternal inheritance.

Forgiveness opens the way to that inheritance.

**Nobody can be happy without holiness**. Those who will be saints in heaven must begin to be saints while on earth.

We are made holy and saved **through faith in Jesus Christ**.

By faith we rely on him as **the Lord our Righteousness** and surrender ourselves to him as **the Lord who rules us**.

Through this faith we receive forgiveness, the gift of the Holy Spirit and eternal life.

Christ's cross offended the Jewish leaders.

They were enraged that Paul proclaimed the fulfillment of Old Testament prophecies, including that Christ would be **the first or principal one raised from the dead**.

The prophets also foretold that the Messiah would bring Gentiles to know God.

What reasonable objection could anyone have to this?

A genuine convert can explain the reasons for Christian hope and the change in personal life.

Yet people who preach repentance and conversion have often suffered persecution for doing so.

## Festus and Agrippa hear Paul's appeal (verses 24–32)

We should speak **truthfully and soberly** in every situation, without being unsettled by unjust criticism.

Faithful gospel workers are often dismissed as dreamers or mad people because they believe wonderful events and insist that others, regardless of social status, need the same faith and diligence for salvation.

The apostles, prophets and even **the Son of God** faced such accusations.

Those made wise for salvation by divine grace need not be shaken by them.

Agrippa saw much evidence in favor of Christianity.

For a time his reasoning and judgment were persuaded, but **his heart was not changed**.

His attitude and conduct remained far from the humility and spiritual character of the gospel.

Many people become **almost persuaded** to take faith seriously.

They recognize the excellence of God's ways and feel strong convictions about their duties, yet they fail to act on them.

Paul urged everyone to become truly Christian.

Christ's grace is sufficient for all, and faith in him is absolutely necessary for salvation.

The gospel offers deliverance from spiritual bondage to Gentiles and the lost world.

Yet people are often unwilling to admit they need a transforming work of grace **in their own hearts**.

Beware of hesitation that becomes fatal.

Being **almost a Christian** is very different from possessing the faith and life of a true believer.
