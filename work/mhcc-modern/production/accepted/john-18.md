# John 18 — Modern English

## Jesus is arrested in the garden (verses 1–12)

Sin first entered the human story in **the garden of Eden**. There the curse was pronounced and a Redeemer promised.

Now in another garden, that promised descendant entered conflict with the ancient serpent.

Jesus would also be buried in a garden.

Whenever we walk in a garden, we may remember the sufferings Christ endured in one.

Jesus knew everything about to happen. Yet he stepped forward to ask the crowd whom they sought.

When people wanted to force him to wear an earthly **crown**, he withdrew (**John 6:15**). When they came to force him to a **cross**, he freely presented himself.

He had come into this world **to suffer**, and would enter the next **to reign**.

Jesus demonstrated his power when the arresting party fell backward. He could have struck them dead but refused.

The soldiers' willingness to allow the disciples to escape quietly after the attempted resistance must also be attributed to divine power.

Jesus gives an example of **meekness in suffering** and complete submission to God's will.

Our earthly trials are like a cup. They are given to us by **a Father**, whose authority is right, whose affection is kind and whose purposes cannot wrong us.

If Jesus accepted his appointed cup, how can we distrust the love of our Father when he appoints much lighter burdens to us?

We were bound by the bonds of our sins and the yoke of our transgressions.

To free us from that bondage, Christ became **a sin offering** and willingly allowed himself to be bound.

**We owe our freedom to his bonds**. Thus the Son makes his people free.

## Jesus before Annas and Caiaphas; Peter's denials (verses 13–27)

Peter denied his Master. The other Gospels supply further details about this event.

Beginning to sin is like **opening a floodgate**: a small beginning can quickly become destructive.

Lying is particularly fruitful in evil. A lie often requires another to support it, and then another.

When God clearly calls us into danger, we may trust him to help us honor him.

But when we enter danger without a clear call of duty, we have reason to fear being left to dishonor ourselves.

Jesus' accusers did not even address the miracles through which he had helped so many people and confirmed his teaching.

They rejected his truth while deliberately refusing to look at its evidence.

Jesus appealed to the people who had heard him openly. His doctrine can safely be submitted to those who truly know it and judge fairly.

Even when wronged, we must not react with passionate resentment.

Jesus reasoned calmly with the person who struck him. We may follow that example.

## Jesus stands before Pilate (verses 28–40)

### The accusation and predicted manner of death (verses 28–32)

The authorities knew it was unjust to execute a man who had done such good, yet they wanted to avoid **the public disgrace** of their actions.

Many people fear the scandal of wrongdoing more than the wrongdoing itself.

Jesus had predicted being delivered to the Gentiles and put to death.

He had also said he would be **lifted up**.

If condemned under the Jewish legal process, he would have been stoned; Roman crucifixion fulfilled his prediction.

Our own manner and time of death are likewise known to God, though concealed from us.

That knowledge should release us from anxiety: **Lord, let what you have appointed happen when and how you have appointed it**.

### The King whose kingdom is not worldly (verses 33–40)

Pilate asked whether Jesus was the King of the Jews—the long-expected Messiah.

Jesus answered with a question, not to evade Pilate, but to make him consider what he was doing.

Jesus had never claimed earthly political authority, and no charge of treasonous conduct could be established against him.

He explained his kingdom.

Its **nature is spiritual**: it is established in human hearts and consciences, with spiritual wealth, power and glory.

Its **supports and weapons are spiritual**. It does not need violence or use force to advance. It opposes not earthly kingdoms but **sin and Satan's reign**.

Its **purpose is spiritual**.

As the Truth, Christ is also King: he conquers through truth's compelling evidence and rules through truth's rightful authority.

Those who belong to the truth are his subjects.

Pilate asked, **“What is truth?”**

When we read Scripture and listen to preaching, we should ask that question sincerely and pray to be led into all truth.

Many ask it without the patience to keep seeking or humility to receive the answer.

Pilate's statement that Jesus was innocent confirms that Christ was condemned as a criminal despite deserving none of the accusations.

It also reveals **the purpose of his death: to become the sacrifice for our sins**.

Pilate tried to satisfy every side, guided more by worldly calculation than by justice.

Sin is a robber, yet people foolishly choose it over Christ, who alone can truly enrich them.

Let us answer false accusations as Jesus did, and never turn away from him as though crucifying him again.
