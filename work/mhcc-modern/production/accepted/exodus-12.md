## The Passover is instituted (verses 1–20)

The Lord makes everything new for people whom he releases from Satan's slavery and brings into his family.

The time of their deliverance becomes the beginning of a new life.

God instructed every Israelite household to kill a lamb on the night they would leave Egypt.

Where a family was small, two or three families could share one lamb.

They were to eat it in the manner God prescribed and put some of its blood on their doorposts.

This would distinguish their homes from Egyptian homes.

When the Lord's angel killed the firstborn of Egypt, he would *pass over* every house marked with the lamb's blood.

This is where the name of the holy feast comes from.

Israel was to celebrate the Passover every year, remembering its preservation and deliverance from Egypt and recognizing in it a striking picture of Christ.

The people's safety was not a reward for their righteousness.

It was a gift of mercy.

Through this ordinance they learned that God's blessings came to them through the shedding and application of blood.

**First, the Passover lamb pointed to Christ.**

Christ is our Passover (1 Corinthians 5:7).

He is the Lamb of God (John 1:29), and the book of Revelation often calls him the Lamb.

The animal was to be in the prime of life.

Christ gave himself in the midst of his days, not as an infant at Bethlehem.

The lamb had to be without blemish.

Jesus likewise had no fault; even the judge who condemned him declared him innocent.

The lamb was chosen four days beforehand.

This pictured the appointment of Christ as Savior, both in God's purpose and in his promise.

The lamb was killed and roasted over fire.

This pictured Christ's painful suffering and death, even death on the cross.

God's wrath is like fire, and Christ became a curse for us.

Not one of the lamb's bones was to be broken.

This was fulfilled in Christ (John 19:33) and pointed to his unbroken strength.

**Second, the sprinkled blood pointed to the application of Christ's death.**

The lamb's blood had to be put on the doors.

This pictured the personal application of the benefits of Christ's death.

We must receive the atonement (Romans 5:11).

Faith is like the bunch of hyssop with which the blood is applied.

Through faith we receive for ourselves God's promises and the benefits of Christ's blood contained in them.

The blood was placed openly on the doorposts.

This pointed to our public profession of faith in Christ.

It was not placed on the threshold.

This warns us never to trample underfoot the blood of the covenant.

It is precious blood, and it must be precious to us.

The blood on the doors protected Israel from the destroying angel, who had no authority in the houses where it was found.

In the same way, Christ's blood protects believers from God's wrath, the curse of the law, and eternal condemnation (Romans 8:1).

**Third, eating the lamb pictured our duty to receive Christ by faith.**

The lamb was not merely to be looked at.

It had to be eaten.

So we must receive Christ as our own by faith and draw spiritual strength and nourishment from him, as we draw nourishment from food (John 6:53, 55).

The whole lamb had to be eaten.

Those who feed on Christ by faith must receive the whole Christ.

They must accept not only his crown but also his yoke and his cross.

The lamb had to be eaten that night, not saved until morning.

Christ is offered to us today, and we must receive him while it is still called today, before the sleep of death overtakes us.

Bitter herbs accompanied the meal, recalling the bitterness of slavery in Egypt.

We must receive Christ with grief and brokenness of heart as we remember our sin.

Christ will be sweet to us when sin has become bitter.

The Israelites ate standing, with their staffs in hand, ready to depart.

When we feed on Christ through faith, we must leave sin's rule behind and loosen our attachment to the world and everything in it.

We must give up everything for him, knowing that this is no loss (Hebrews 13:13–14).

**Fourth, the feast of unleavened bread pictured the Christian life (1 Corinthians 5:7–8).**

Once we have received Jesus Christ as Lord, we should continue finding our joy in him.

No work was to interrupt the feast.

In the spiritual application, we should allow no worldly anxiety that is incompatible with this holy joy or diminishes it.

The Jews took great care that no leaven remained in their houses.

Our celebration must likewise be free of malice and full of love, free of hypocrisy and full of sincerity.

The feast was established as a lasting ordinance.

Throughout our lives, we must keep feeding on Christ, rejoicing in him, and gratefully remembering the great things he has done for us.

## Israel is instructed to observe the Passover (verses 21–28)

On the night Egypt's firstborn were to die, no Israelite was to leave the house until the time came to depart from Egypt.

Their safety rested in the blood that had been applied.

To move outside its protection would put them in danger.

They were to remain inside and wait for the Lord's salvation.

That is a good thing for us to do as well.

In later generations, parents were to teach their children carefully what this ceremony meant.

It is good for children to ask questions about God.

Those who ask about the right way will find it.

The annual celebration looked in two directions.

First, **backward**, so that the people would remember what God had done for them and their ancestors.

Past mercies shown to us or to earlier generations must never be forgotten.

Remembering them brings praise to God and strengthens faith.

Second, **forward**, as a pledge of the great sacrifice the Lamb of God would make when the time was fulfilled.

Christ our Passover was sacrificed for us.

His death is our life.

## Egypt's firstborn die, and Israel is released (verses 29–36)

For three days and nights the Egyptians had lived in terror because of the darkness.

Now their rest was broken by a much more dreadful disaster.

The firstborn—the joy and hope of their families—were struck down.

The Egyptians had killed Hebrew children.

Now God struck their children.

The judgment reached from Pharaoh's throne to the dungeon.

Princes and ordinary laborers are equal before God's judgments.

The destroying angel entered every dwelling not marked with blood.

Not one such house escaped death.

Imagine the great cry that filled Egypt, the long, agonizing wailing from family after family.

Something similar will happen when the Son of Man comes to judge sinners for the last time.

God's sons, his firstborn people, were now set free.

People would do well to accept God's terms from the beginning, because he will never submit to theirs.

Pharaoh's pride was brought low, and he yielded.

God's word will stand.

Argument and delay accomplish nothing against it.

In their terror the Egyptians were willing to give generously to secure Israel's favor and make the people leave quickly.

In this way God ensured that Israel was paid for its hard labor and provided with what it needed for the journey.

## Israel sets out for Succoth (verses 37–42)

The Israelites departed without delay.

A mixed multitude went with them.

Some may have wanted to escape a country devastated by plagues.

Others may have followed from curiosity, and perhaps a few because they loved Israel and its faith.

But among the Israelites there had always been some who were not truly Israelites.

There are still hypocrites in the church.

Henry dates this great event to 430 years after the promise to Abraham (Galatians 3:17).

For all that time, the promised settlement had not yet come.

God's promises may not be fulfilled quickly, but they will be fulfilled at the proper time.

That night belonged to the Lord.

Its remarkable events were to be commemorated through every generation.

The great things God does for his people are not meant to be talked about for a few days and then forgotten.

They should be remembered through the ages—especially Christ's work of redemption.

Israel's first Passover night was indeed a night to remember.

But the last Passover night, when Christ was betrayed and the original Passover along with the other Jewish ceremonies came to an end, is still more worthy of remembrance.

Then a yoke heavier than Egypt's was broken from our necks.

A land better than Canaan was placed before us.

That redemption will be celebrated in heaven forever.

## Who may share in the Passover (verses 43–51)

In later times, all the congregation of Israel was required to keep the Passover.

Everyone who shares in God's mercies should join in giving thanks and praise.

The New Testament Passover, the Lord's Supper, should not be neglected.

Foreigners who received circumcision could take part in Israel's Passover.

This was an early indication of God's favor toward the Gentiles.

It taught Israel that membership among a people favored by God, not simply physical descent from Abraham, gave someone access to the people's privileges.

Christ our Passover has been sacrificed for us (1 Corinthians 5:7).

His blood is the only ransom for our souls.

Without the shedding of his blood, there is no forgiveness of sins.

Without its application to us, there is no salvation.

Have we, through faith in Christ, placed ourselves under the protection of his atoning blood and found shelter from the judgment we deserve?

Do we remain near him, continually depending on him?

Do we profess our faith in the Redeemer and our debt to him so openly that everyone who sees us can tell to whom we belong?

Are we ready to serve him, walk in his ways, and separate ourselves from his enemies?

These questions matter enormously for our souls.

May the Lord guide our consciences to answer them truthfully.
