# 2 Corinthians 5 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The apostle's hope and desire of heavenly glory. | 1–8 |
| This excited to diligence. The reasons of his being affected with zeal for the Corinthians. | 9–15 |
| The necessity of regeneration, and of reconciliation with God through Christ. | 16–21 |

## Our future home and the presence of Christ (verses 1–8)

Faith assures the believer that life does not end at the grave.

Through God's grace, we also possess a solid hope of **heaven as our home, place of rest and shelter**.

Our Father's house has many dwelling places. God himself is its Architect and Builder.

Heaven's joy is what God has prepared for those who love him.

Our present bodies are like **temporary tents** or poor houses of clay, decaying and slowly collapsing on foundations of dust.

The body can feel like a heavy burden, especially when sickness and suffering come.

But believers groan most deeply because of **the body of sin**—the corrupt desires that still remain within them and continually trouble their souls.

Death will remove our earthly bodies and comforts, and it will end the outward troubles of this life.

But believers will be clothed with **praise, righteousness and glory**.

The Holy Spirit's present graces and comforts are **pledges** of the everlasting life and consolation ahead.

God is already with us through his Spirit and his appointed means of grace. Yet this is not the same as being with him **in the full way we hope to enjoy in heaven**.

Now we live by **faith**; there we shall live by **sight**.

Until then, it is both our duty and our good to continue walking by faith.

These verses clearly teach the happiness enjoyed by the **souls of believers when they are absent from their bodies** and present where Jesus reveals his glory.

We are bound both to our present bodies and to our Lord.

Each has a claim upon our affections.

But how much more powerfully does Christ call the souls he has loved, chosen and received from the Father to be united closely with himself!

Compared with being separated from the Lord, why should death itself be our greatest fear?

## Future judgment and Christ's love inspire service (verses 9–15)

The hope of heaven should stir us toward **greater effort and obedience**, never toward laziness or sinful complacency.

Remember the coming judgment—the solemn terror of the Lord.

Knowing how dreadful the Lord's vengeance will be against those who persist in evil, Paul and his companions used every proper argument and appeal to persuade people **to believe in Jesus and live as his disciples**.

Their zeal served God's honor and the welfare of the church.

Christ's love will produce similar devotion in us when we consider it carefully.

All humanity was lost, dead in sins, enslaved to evil and unable to rescue itself.

Without Christ's death, we would have remained in that miserable condition forever.

Therefore the goal of our living and acting must not be **ourselves but Christ**.

A Christian's life belongs to him.

Sadly, many show that their professed faith and love are hollow by continuing to live entirely **for themselves and this world**.

## A new creation reconciled to God through Christ (verses 16–21)

A renewed person lives by **new principles**, follows **new rules**, seeks **new ends** and keeps **new company**.

The believer becomes a **new creation**.

The heart is not merely repaired or adjusted; God gives an entirely **new heart**.

We are God's workmanship, created in **Christ Jesus for good works**.

We remain the same human beings, yet our character and conduct are transformed.

Regeneration means much more than an external improvement in behavior.

Someone who once saw no beauty in Christ and felt no desire for him now **loves the Savior above everything**.

Before regeneration, the heart is hostile to God, and God is justly offended by sin.

Yet reconciliation is possible.

Through **Jesus Christ**, the God whom we have offended has reconciled us to himself.

By divine inspiration the Scriptures were written as **the message of reconciliation**, announcing that peace has been made through the cross and explaining how we can receive its blessings.

God gains nothing from our hostility or our reconciliation; he loses nothing if we remain enemies.

Nevertheless, he graciously **appeals to sinners to lay aside their hostility and accept his salvation**.

Christ knew no sin.

Yet he was **made sin for us**—not made a sinner, but made **a sin offering, a sacrifice for sin**.

The purpose was that we might become **the righteousness of God in him**, freely justified by divine grace through the redemption accomplished in Jesus Christ.

How could we ever consider any sacrifice, labor or suffering too great for the One who gave his beloved Son as an offering for our sins so that we might be declared righteous in him?
