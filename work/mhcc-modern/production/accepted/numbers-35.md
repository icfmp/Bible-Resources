# Numbers 35 — Modern English

## Cities for the Levites (verses 1–8)

The cities assigned to the priests and Levites served two purposes.

They gave them places to live, and they placed **teachers of God's law** throughout Israel.

The ceremonial sacrifices of the tabernacle and later the temple were offered in one place.

But teaching God's word, praying and praising him were **not** confined to one location.

Each tribe gave cities from its own inheritance.

In doing so, each expressed gratitude toward God.

Every tribe also benefited from having Levites nearby, ready to teach the knowledge of the Lord.

No part of the country needed to remain in spiritual darkness.

Under the gospel, those who receive instruction from God's word should share their material blessings with those who teach them (Galatians 6:6).

Congregations should free ministers from distractions that prevent them from attending to the responsibilities of their calling.

Then ministers can give themselves fully to their work.

They should also use opportunities to show kindness, win people's trust and encourage them to listen to God's truth.

## The cities of refuge and the law concerning killing (verses 9–34)

The law expressed God's strong hatred of murder and provided a way for murderers to be punished.

In the society described here, a close relative of someone killed could act as the **avenger of blood**, pursuing a known murderer.

But the law made a crucial distinction.

It did **not** distinguish a killing committed in sudden rage from one carefully planned in advance: both could be murder.

The distinction was between **deliberately striking someone with an instrument likely to kill** and causing death **without intending it**.

Only in the second case did the city of refuge offer protection.

Murder, whatever disguise it wears, defiles a country.

Henry condemns killings that were tolerated or left unpunished under names such as **dueling** and **prizefighting**.

There were six cities of refuge.

From any part of Israel, a person could reach one of them in less than a day's journey, Henry says.

A person who caused an accidental death could flee there and remain safe until a fair trial.

If cleared of murder, that person was protected from the avenger of blood.

Yet the person still had to remain inside the city's boundaries until the death of the high priest.

That restriction points us to Christ.

Only the death of the **great High Priest** brings forgiveness of sins and the sinner's complete freedom.

Henry understands the cities of refuge as clear pictures of the salvation Christ provides.

The Old and New Testaments allude to this provision (Zechariah 9:12; Hebrews 6:18).

He identifies **six ways** the cities point to the gospel.

**First, their high towers offered protection.** Christ has been lifted up on the cross and exalted to the Father's right hand as Prince and Savior, bringing repentance and forgiveness.

**Second, their roads were made smooth and clear.** The path to the Redeemer is open; its true obstacle is the unbelief of the sinful heart.

**Third, signs pointed travelers toward the city.** Ministers of the gospel are appointed to direct sinners toward Christ.

**Fourth, their gates stood open day and night.** Christ promises never to turn away anyone who comes to him.

**Fifth, each city sustained those who entered it.** Believers can live by faith on Christ, whose flesh is true food and whose blood is true drink.

**Sixth, the cities offered refuge to everyone who qualified to flee there.** The gospel likewise shows no partiality.

No person can claim to be so righteous that God's anger is undeserved.

Nor is any soul excluded from hoping for eternal life through simple faith in God's Son.

The riches of Christ's salvation demand our earnest attention and grateful response.
