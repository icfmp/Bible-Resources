# Job 5 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Eliphaz urges that the sin of sinners in their ruin. | 1–5 |
| God is to be regarded in affliction. | 6–16 |
| The happy end of God's correction. | 17–27 |

## Eliphaz argues that sinners bring ruin upon themselves (verses 1–5)

Eliphaz challenged Job to answer his arguments.

Had any of God's holy servants suffered the kinds of judgments Job was experiencing, or behaved as Job did under them?

The word **saints** means holy or, more precisely, consecrated people.

Henry sees it as a term used through the ages for God's people, whose reconciliation is established through **the covenant sacrifice**.

Eliphaz rightly held that sin leads sinners toward destruction.

People destroy themselves through their sinful desires.

He therefore assumed Job must have acted foolishly to bring about his current troubles.

Eliphaz's language alluded plainly to Job's earlier wealth.

But **there was no evidence of Job's wickedness**.

Applying these truths to Job was **unfair and harsh**.

## Eliphaz teaches Job to look to God in suffering (verses 6–16)

Eliphaz reminded Job that troubles do not occur at random or arise ultimately from secondary causes.

Prosperity and suffering do not alternate with the regularity of day and night or summer and winter.

They come under God's wise direction.

We must not attribute suffering to **blind fortune**, for it is governed by God.

Nor should we attribute our sins to **fate**, since they arise from ourselves.

Human beings are born in sin and therefore born to trouble.

Sin and trouble are the only things in this world to which we are born with a natural claim.

Outward acts of sin are like sparks flying out from the furnace of our original corruption.

Our bodies are fragile and earthly comforts do not last.

Troubles rise from this condition like sparks rising from a fire: numerous and often coming one after another.

Eliphaz rebuked Job for arguing with God rather than seeking him.

**When afflicted, pray.**

Prayer relieves the heart and brings healing to its wounds.

Eliphaz spoke about **rain**, something we often take for granted.

Yet if we reflect on how it is produced and what life it sustains, we recognize a wonderful work of divine power and kindness.

We fail to notice the Giver of ordinary comforts because they appear so regularly.

God's providential treatment of some people encourages others to hope even in the worst circumstances.

He brings help to the helpless and hope to the hopeless.

He can also confound people who persist boldly in wickedness and make them acknowledge the justice of his judgments.

## Eliphaz describes the blessing of God's discipline (verses 17–27)

Eliphaz told Job not to despise the Almighty's correction.

Such discipline can come from a **father's love** for the child's good.

We should receive it as a message from heaven.

Eliphaz urged Job to submit and encouraged him with promises.

A good person can remain blessed amid suffering because fellowship with God and the hope of heaven are not lost.

Indeed, affliction can bring blessings.

It restrains sinful desires, loosens attachment to the world, draws the heart nearer to God, opens the Bible and brings us to our knees.

God may wound his people, yet he also sustains them during their trials and eventually delivers them.

**Making a wound can be part of a cure**.

Eliphaz promised much to Job if Job would humble himself.

Trouble cannot truly harm the godly when the Lord keeps them from sin.

Christ's servants may not always be delivered **out of** outward trouble, but they can be delivered **by means of it**.

Though they seem overcome by a particular trial, they remain conquerors in God's sight.

Slander cannot rob them of the blessings God intends.

He provides the wisdom and grace needed for their responsibilities.

The greatest blessing in our work and enjoyment is to be kept from sin.

God's people will finish their course with joy and honor.

A person has lived long enough when the assigned work is complete and the soul is prepared for the world to come.

Dying at the appointed time can be a mercy, like grain gathered when it has ripened, neither too early nor left standing too long.

Our times belong in God's hands.

Believers must **not** expect great riches, unusually long lives or freedom from trials.

But God will arrange everything for their ultimate good.

Job's experience teaches how difficult it is to maintain steady faith during severe trials.

When life goes well, faith may have little opportunity to exercise its strength.

But when God allows storm after storm, and even seems distant while we pray, **trusting him when we cannot trace his ways** becomes the patience of the saints.

Blessed Savior, how precious it is to look to you, the **Author and Perfecter of faith**, in those moments!
