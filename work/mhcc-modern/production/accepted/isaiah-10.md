# Isaiah 10 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Woes against proud oppressors. | 1–4 |
| The Assyrian but an instrument in the hand of God for the punishment of his people. | 5–19 |
| The deliverance from him. | 20–34 |

## Judgment against unjust rulers (verses 1–4)

Henry treats these opening verses as **a continuation of the warning in chapter 9**.

Woe to powerful rulers who devise and impose unjust laws!

Woe also to the officials who draft those decrees and record them for enforcement!

No authority or bureaucratic form can make oppression right before God.

When divine judgment comes, where will such sinners flee, and how will they escape?

## Assyria as an instrument of God's Providence (verses 5–19)

See what a change sin had produced!

The Assyrian king imagined he was acting entirely by his own will.

But the tyrants of this world can become **instruments of God's Providence**.

The Lord intended to correct his people for hypocrisy, bring them nearer to himself and awaken genuine repentance.

**That was not Sennacherib's purpose.**

He was pursuing greed and ambition.

He boasted that he had defeated many nations through his own intelligence and military strength.

He did not recognize that God had given him his position and placed the rod of power in his hand.

His victories had seemed effortless, like taking eggs from a nest while the birds remained silent.

After taking Samaria, he supposed Jerusalem would inevitably fall.

It was shameful that Jerusalem had adopted images and idolatry, allowing pagan neighbors to surpass her in those very practices.

Christians behave just as foolishly when they compete with unbelievers in worldly vanity instead of valuing the gifts that should distinguish God's people.

For an ax or saw to boast against the one using it would be absurd. **Sennacherib's boasting against Jehovah was even more absurd.**

When the Lord allows his people to experience suffering, he calls them to **remember and confess their sins**, become humble and return to duty.

The intended fruit of affliction is **the removal of sin**.

Once that purpose has been accomplished, God may mercifully remove the trial.

The attack on Zion would ultimately fail.

The Lord would become a **consuming fire** against workers of evil, destroying their strength.

The collapse of the Assyrian forces would resemble an army thrown into confusion when its standard-bearer faints.

Who can stand against the holy Lord?

## The remnant delivered from Assyria (verses 20–34)

Trials can teach believers to stop making created things their final security.

Only those who **return sincerely to God**, rather than merely professing faith, can rest upon him with true comfort.

The Lord would justly bring destruction upon a people who provoked him, but he would also **set limits** to that punishment.

His people should not surrender to fear.

God's anger toward them is brief; when it passes, human anger need no longer terrify them.

The rod used for discipline would not merely be put aside. It would be **thrown into the fire**.

To strengthen his people's faith, Isaiah recalled God's earlier victories over their enemies.

He promised deliverance from Assyria.

Henry records that **some interpreters** see a further application to the Jews' release from captivity and a greater deliverance from **sin and Satan's tyranny**.

He also considers the phrase **“because of the anointing”** in relation both to faithful Israelites possessing God's grace and to **the Messiah, God's Anointed One**.

Verses **28–34** describe prophetically Sennacherib's advance toward Jerusalem.

There the Lord, in whom Hezekiah trusted, cut down the invader's army **as a forest is cut down**.

The same divine care extends to Christ's church in every age.

Because of the anointing of the great Redeemer, the yoke of every power opposing Christ must finally be broken.

Those who share in **the Holy Spirit's anointing** can be assured of complete and eternal deliverance.
