## Jacob gathers his sons (verses 1–2)

All Jacob's sons were still alive.

By calling them together, he instructed them to remain united in love and not blend into the Egyptian people.

He also foretold that they would not be separated from one another, as the sons of Abraham and of Isaac had been. Instead, they would form one people.

We should not read Jacob's words merely as an expression of personal affection, resentment, or favoritism.

Henry understands them as the words of the Holy Spirit, declaring God's purposes for the character, circumstances, and future situation of the tribes descended from Jacob's sons.

Their later history shows how those purposes unfolded.

## Reuben, Simeon, and Levi (verses 3–7)

Reuben was the firstborn, but through a serious sin he forfeited his birthright.

Jacob described him as unstable as water.

People often fail to prosper because they never settle firmly on a course.

Reuben's sin brought lasting disgrace on his family.

If we avoid doing evil, we need not fear having our deeds brought to light.

Simeon and Levi were hot-tempered and revengeful.

Their murder of the Shechemites revealed their character, and Jacob condemned that barbaric act.

Our souls are our honor. Their powers distinguish us from the animals that perish and raise us above them.

We should sincerely detest people who delight in bloodshed and harm.

“Cursed be their anger.”

Jacob cursed not their persons, but their sinful passions.

“I will divide them.”

For Levi, that judgment was later turned into a blessing.

The Levites served God acceptably when they acted with zeal against those worshiping the golden calf (Exodus 32).

God set them apart for the priesthood, and in that role they were scattered among the people of Israel.

## Judah and the promised Messiah (verses 8–12)

The name *Judah* means “praise.”

God was praised when Judah was born (Genesis 29:35), praised by him, and praised in him.

It was fitting that his brothers should praise him.

Judah would become a strong and courageous tribe.

He was pictured not as a lion roaming furiously and causing distress, but as one resting peacefully in the satisfaction of his power and success.

That is a picture of true greatness.

Judah would become the royal tribe, the line from which the Messiah, the Prince, would come.

*Shiloh*, the promised Seed through whom the earth would be blessed, would come from Judah.

Henry gives the name the sense of “the peaceful and prosperous One” or “Savior.”

From far away in history, the dying Jacob saw the day of Christ.

That hope comforted and sustained him on his deathbed.

Judah retained authority until Christ's coming.

After Christ's crucifixion, according to Henry, that authority was diminished. As Jesus had foretold, Jerusalem was destroyed, and the remaining Jewish people, scattered and afflicted, were mingled together.

Much of the blessing pronounced over Judah applies to our Lord Jesus.

Christ has an abundance of everything that nourishes and refreshes the soul, sustains spiritual life, and fills it with joy.

He is the true Vine.

Wine is the appointed symbol of his blood: blood shed for sinners, received through faith, and truly life-giving.

All the blessings of the gospel are offered like wine and milk, without money or payment.

Every thirsty soul is welcome (Isaiah 55:1).

## Zebulun, Issachar, and Dan (verses 13–18)

If prophecy declares that Zebulun will become a haven for ships, we may be certain that Providence will place his people there.

God determines the boundaries of the places where we live.

It is our wisdom and duty to accept our circumstances and make good use of them.

If Zebulun lives near the sea, let him make that shore a haven for ships.

Issachar saw that the land was pleasant.

It offered both beautiful views and fruitful harvests to reward his labor.

With the eyes of faith, let us see how good the rest of heaven is and how delightful that promised land will be.

That hope makes our present duties easier.

Dan would gain advantage over his enemies through skill, strategy, and surprise, like a serpent striking a traveler's heel.

Jacob was almost spent and near fainting.

He refreshed himself with the words, “I have waited for your salvation, O Lord!”

The salvation he awaited was Christ, the promised Seed.

As Jacob prepared to be gathered to his people, he longed for the One to whom the people would be gathered.

He openly confessed that he sought heaven, the better country (Hebrews 11:13–14).

Now he was about to enjoy that salvation, and it comforted him to know that he had waited for it.

We must wait upon Christ as our way to heaven, and wait for heaven as our rest in Christ.

For a dying believer, it is a deep comfort to have waited for the Lord's salvation, because at last he will receive what he has been waiting for.

## Gad, Asher, and Naphtali (verses 19–21)

In speaking of Gad, Jacob alluded to his name, which means “troop,” and foretold the character of his tribe.

God's cause and God's people may for a time seem beaten down and defeated, but they will finally be victorious.

This is also a picture of the Christian's struggle.

The grace at work in the soul may seem to lose its battles when bands of sinful desires overcome it.

But the cause belongs to God.

In the end, grace will prevail, making the believer more than a conqueror (Romans 8:37).

Asher would become a prosperous tribe.

His inheritance bordered on Carmel, a place proverbial for its fruitfulness.

Naphtali is described as a deer set free.

Henry takes this as a description of the tribe's character.

Unlike the hard-working ox or donkey, it would prize freedom and ease.

It would be lively and quick to act, though less distinguished by steady, persistent labor.

It would also resemble a petitioner who uses gracious words to ask for mercy.

People with different temperaments and gifts should not criticize or envy one another.

## Joseph and Benjamin (verses 22–27)

Jacob's blessing upon Joseph was especially rich.

What he said was both a history of Joseph's past and a prophecy of what would come.

Jacob recalled the many hardships and fiery arrows of temptation Joseph had endured.

Through those trials, Joseph's faith had not failed.

He carried his burdens steadily and did nothing unworthy of his calling.

All the strength we have to resist temptation and bear suffering comes from God.

His grace is enough.

Joseph became the shepherd of Israel, caring for his father and family.

He was also the stone of Israel, the foundation and strong support on which they depended.

In these ways Joseph foreshadowed the Good Shepherd and the tested Cornerstone of God's whole church.

The blessings promised to Joseph's descendants pointed toward the vast and everlasting blessings given to Christ's spiritual offspring.

Jacob blessed all his sons, but Joseph especially, calling him the one “separated from his brothers.”

That separation meant more than his years in Egypt.

He had also been raised to exceptional honor and had shown unusual devotion to God.

Of Benjamin Jacob said, “He shall devour like a wolf.”

The words came through the Spirit of prophecy, not through Jacob's natural preferences.

Otherwise he would have spoken more tenderly of Benjamin, whom he loved so dearly.

Instead, he foretold that Benjamin's descendants would become a warlike people—strong, bold, active, and enriched by the spoils of their enemies.

The apostle Paul belonged to this tribe (Romans 11:1; Philippians 3:5).

Early in life he attacked like a wolf, devouring prey as a persecutor.

Later he distributed the spoils as a preacher of the gospel.

In doing so, he shared in the victory of the Lion of Judah and helped to advance his triumphs.

## Jacob gives his final instructions and dies (verses 28–33)

Jacob blessed each son according to the blessings God intended eventually to give his descendants.

He instructed them about his burial place because he believed God's promise that Canaan would one day belong to his offspring.

When he had finished speaking his blessings and instructions, his testimony was complete.

He then prepared for the work of dying.

He drew his feet up into the bed.

This was not only a sign of patient submission to death, but also of someone gladly settling down to rest after a long and weary life.

He freely entrusted his spirit to God, the Father of spirits.

If God's people are our people, death will gather us to them.

Under the care of Israel's Shepherd, we will lack nothing necessary for body or soul.

We will remain here until our work is finished.

Then, breathing our souls into the hands of the One whose salvation we have awaited, we will depart in peace and leave a blessing to our children.
