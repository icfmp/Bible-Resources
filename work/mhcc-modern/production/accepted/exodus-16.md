# Exodus 16 — Modern English

| Original outline | Verses |

## Israel complains; God promises bread from heaven (verses 1–12)

By the middle of the second month, the provisions Israel had brought from Egypt were gone. The people began to complain.

It is nothing new for people to describe the greatest kindness they have received as though it were the greatest injury.

They valued their deliverance so little that they wished they had died in Egypt—by the Lord's hand, meaning by the plagues that killed the Egyptians.

We cannot suppose that they had lived in plenty in Egypt. Nor did they need to fear starvation in the wilderness while they still possessed flocks and herds.

No one reasons more absurdly than people who are determined to complain.

When we begin to fret, we should remember that God hears every complaint we make.

Yet the Lord promised them a prompt and continuing supply of food.

He tested whether they would trust him, content to receive enough bread for each day as it came.

He was also testing whether they would serve him, and their ingratitude became plain.

When God struck Egypt with plagues, he showed that he was its Lord.

When he provided food for Israel, he showed that he was **their God**.

## Quail and manna are provided (verses 13–21)

Quail came in the evening. The people were able to catch as many as they needed without difficulty.

Manna came down with the dew.

The Israelites called it *manna*, or *manhu*: “What is this?”

They could say of it, “This is the portion God has assigned us. We will receive it thankfully.”

It was pleasant and wholesome food.

It came from heaven. After the dew evaporated, the people saw small round pieces, like frost, resembling coriander seed and having the color of pearls.

Manna fell on six days of the week, with a double amount on the sixth.

If it was kept beyond one day, it developed worms and became foul—except when kept for the Sabbath.

The Israelites had never seen anything like it before.

They could grind it in a mill, crush it in a mortar, make it into cakes and bake it.

It continued for all forty years of their wilderness journey and wherever they camped, ceasing when they reached Canaan.

These features show that manna was different from anything familiar in their own day or ours.

They had to gather it every morning.

**First, this teaches diligence and prudence.**

We should provide food for ourselves and our families through honest, peaceful work, not through idleness or deceit.

God's generosity does not remove our responsibility to act.

Even when bread literally rained from heaven, the people had to gather it before they could eat it.

**Second, this teaches contentment.**

We should be satisfied with enough.

Those with the most possessions still need only food and clothing for themselves. Those with fewer possessions can generally enjoy these as well.

Thus, in actual enjoyment of life's necessities, the difference between rich and poor is not as great as the difference between what they possess.

The person who gathers much has nothing left over, and the one who gathers little does not lack what is needed.

**Third, this teaches dependence on Providence.**

They could go to sleep peacefully even with no bread anywhere in the camp.

They could trust God to provide their daily bread the next morning.

Their food was safer and more certain in God's storehouse than in their own tents—and would arrive fresher and sweeter.

See the folly of hoarding out of distrust.

People who thought themselves wiser than their neighbors saved manna for the next day in case God failed to provide it.

But their stored manna bred worms and became useless.

What is selfishly and fearfully hoarded may become the very thing most thoroughly wasted.

Such riches become corrupted (James 5:2–3).

The same wisdom, power and goodness that supplied food from heaven each day to Israel continues to bring food from the ground each year through the ordinary course of nature.

He gives us richly everything we need to enjoy.

## Manna and Sabbath rest (verses 22–31)

Here Scripture speaks of a Sabbath on the seventh day.

Henry emphasizes that this day was known before the law was given at Sinai, before Israel left Egypt—indeed, from the beginning (Genesis 2:3).

Setting apart one day in seven for holy activity, together with holy rest so that the activity may be enjoyed, is, in his account, one of God's most ancient laws for humanity.

God told Israel to rest on the seventh day and ensured that this obedience would not leave them worse off.

No one ultimately loses by serving God.

They were to gather and prepare enough manna on the sixth day for two days.

This teaches us to organize our family responsibilities so that they interfere as little as possible with Sabbath worship.

Necessary tasks can still be done that day.

Nevertheless, we should seek to minimize such tasks, so that we may give ourselves more fully to preparing for the life to come.

When the Israelites kept manna against God's command, it spoiled.

When they kept it according to his command, it remained good and sweet.

Everything is made holy by God's word and prayer.

God sent no manna on the seventh day.

They were not to expect it or go out gathering it. This showed that its daily appearance was a miracle.

## A portion of manna is kept as a memorial (verses 32–36)

God had fed his people with manna throughout their journey. He commanded them to preserve the memory of that mercy.

Bread already eaten should not be forgotten.

God's extraordinary works and ordinary kindnesses deserve to be remembered.

God's word is the manna by which our souls are nourished (Matthew 4:4).

The Spirit's comforts are hidden manna (Revelation 2:17).

Like the manna in the wilderness, these gifts come from heaven and sustain the spiritual life of believers on their journey through this world.

Christ, as made known in the word, must be received and applied to the soul.

The means of grace must actually be used.

Every one of us must gather for ourselves.

We should begin gathering early in life and early whenever opportunities are offered.

If we let those opportunities pass, it may become too late.

Manna was meant to be eaten, not hoarded.

Those who have received Christ must live upon him through faith rather than receive his grace without benefit.

There was enough manna for everyone, but no one had too much.

Likewise, in Christ there is enough for us all, but not more than we need.

Those who ate the manna became hungry again. They eventually died, and God was displeased with many of them.

But those who feed on Christ by faith will never hunger and will die no more.

God will be pleased with them forever.

Let us earnestly seek the Holy Spirit's grace to turn everything we know about Christ crucified into genuine spiritual nourishment through faith and love.
