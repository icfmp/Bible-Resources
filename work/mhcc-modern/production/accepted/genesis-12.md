# Genesis 12 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God calls Abram and blesses him with a promise of Christ. | 1–3 |
| Abram leaves Haran. | 4–5 |
| He travels through Canaan and worships God there. | 6–9 |
| Famine drives Abram into Egypt, where he calls Sarai his sister. | 10–20 |

## God calls Abram and promises his blessing (verses 1–3)

God chose Abram out of the people around him, who worshipped idols.

His purpose was to preserve a people for himself, among whom the worship of the true God would continue until Christ came.

From this point on, Abram and his descendants become almost the only subject of the Bible's history.

God tested whether Abram loved him above everyone and everything else, and whether he would willingly leave all in order to follow him.

Abram's relatives and his father's household were a constant temptation. He could not stay among them without risking the spread of their idolatry into his own life.

Those who leave their sins and turn to God gain more than words can describe.

God's command to Abram resembles the gospel call. Natural affection must give way to divine grace when the two conflict.

We must forsake sin and everything that leads us into it, especially corrupting company.

God accompanied this command with great and precious promises. His commands come with promises for those who obey.

First, **“I will make you a great nation.”** God took Abram away from one people and promised to make him the head of another.

Second, **“I will bless you.”** Those who believe and obey are certain to inherit God's blessing.

Third, **“I will make your name great.”** God will certainly honor those who faithfully obey him.

Fourth, **“You will be a blessing.”** Godly people bring good to the nations and communities in which they live.

Fifth, **“I will bless those who bless you, and curse the one who curses you.”** God will not allow anyone who serves his people to lose by doing so.

Sixth, **“In you all the families of the earth will be blessed.”**

Jesus Christ is the world's greatest blessing—the greatest blessing it has ever received.

Every true blessing this world now enjoys or will ever enjoy comes through Abram and his descendants.

Through them we have received the Bible, the Savior, and the gospel.

They are the rootstock into which the Christian church has been grafted.

## Abram leaves Haran (verses 4–5)

Abram believed that the blessing of the Almighty would more than compensate for everything he left behind.

God would supply all his needs and more than satisfy his deepest desires. Abram knew that disobedience could lead only to misery.

Believers who are justified by faith in Christ have peace with God.

They continue toward Canaan, neither discouraged by difficulties on the road nor drawn away by the pleasures they meet along it.

Those who begin the journey to heaven must persevere to the end.

Whatever we undertake in obedience to God's command and humble dependence on his providence will ultimately succeed and bring comfort.

Canaan was more than an outward possession, like other countries.

It was a picture of heaven. That is why the patriarchs valued it so deeply.

## Abram worships God as he travels through Canaan (verses 6–9)

Abram found the land inhabited by Canaanites, who were troublesome neighbors.

He continued traveling from place to place.

Sometimes faithful people must live unsettled lives, moving repeatedly from one place to another.

Believers should remember that they are strangers and pilgrims in this world (Hebrews 11:8, 13–14).

Yet notice how much comfort Abram found in God.

He had little reason to enjoy fellowship with the Canaanites. But he found abundant delight in communion with the God who had brought him there and had not abandoned him.

We maintain fellowship with God through his word and prayer.

God makes himself and his favor known to his people gradually.

Earlier, he had promised to *show* Abram the land. Now he promised to *give* it to him.

As grace grows, comfort grows with it.

Abram seems to have understood the promise as a pledge of a better land, of which Canaan was a picture. He looked for a heavenly country (Hebrews 11:16).

As soon as Abram reached Canaan, though he was a stranger and traveler there, he established and maintained worship in his household.

He was concerned not only with the outward ceremonies of religion, such as offering sacrifices, but with sincerely seeking God and calling upon his name.

That is the spiritual sacrifice God delights to receive.

Abram also proclaimed the name of the Lord.

He taught his household and his neighbors to know the true God and his holy ways.

Family worship is not a recent invention. It is an ancient practice of God's people.

Abram was wealthy and responsible for a large household. He was unsettled and surrounded by enemies.

Yet wherever he pitched his tent, he built an altar.

Wherever we go, let us never leave our faith behind.

## Abram's failure in Egypt (verses 10–20)

No place on earth is free from trials, and no human character is free from flaws.

Even Canaan, the glory of all lands, suffered famine.

And even Abram, the father of the faithful, showed unbelief and all the troubles it brings.

Perfect happiness and perfect purity belong only to heaven.

When Abram had to leave Canaan for a time, he went to Egypt rather than returning to the country he had left. He intended to remain only as long as necessary.

But in Egypt he disguised his relationship with Sarai.

He used misleading words and taught his wife and servants to do the same.

He concealed the truth so thoroughly that he effectively denied it. In doing so, he exposed both Sarai and the Egyptians to sin.

Abram was especially known for his faith. Yet he fell into this failure because he did not trust God's providence—even though God had already appeared to him twice.

If strong faith can be shaken like this, what will become of weak faith?

How often God must rescue us from danger and distress we bring upon ourselves through our own sin and foolishness!

Without his deliverance, we would be ruined. He does not deal with us as we deserve.

God's corrections are blessings when they stop us on a sinful path and bring us back to duty.

That includes the duty to return whatever we have wrongfully taken or kept.

Pharaoh was right to rebuke Abram: “What have you done?”

What an unworthy action for someone wise and godly!

When professing Christians behave unfairly, deceive others, or speak in a way that borders on lying, they must expect to be corrected.

They should even thank those who have the courage to tell them the truth.

Pharaoh treated Abram kindly when he sent him away.

Far from planning to kill Abram, as Abram had feared, Pharaoh took special care to protect him.

We often torment ourselves with fears that have no foundation. We are afraid when there is nothing to fear.

Pharaoh also ordered his servants not to harm Abram.

Those in authority must do more than avoid harming others themselves. They must restrain their servants and everyone under their care from doing harm too.
