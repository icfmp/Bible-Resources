# Job 33 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Elihu offers to reason with Job. | 1–7 |
| Elihu blames Job for reflecting upon God. | 8–13 |
| God calls men to repentance. | 14–18 |
| God sends afflictions for good. | 19–28 |
| Elihu entreats Job's attention. | 29–33 |

## Elihu offers to reason with Job (verses 1–7)

Job had wanted an impartial judge to hear his case.

In one sense Elihu was an answer to that request: he was another human being like Job.

To persuade people rightly, we must use **reason rather than terror**, fair arguments rather than force.

## Elihu corrects Job's accusations against God (verses 8–13)

Elihu charged Job with speaking against God's justice and goodness.

When we hear statements that dishonor God, we should testify against them.

Job had described God as harshly recording his wrongs.

Elihu said he had spoken improperly and ought to humble himself and withdraw those accusations through repentance.

God is not accountable to human beings.

It is unreasonable for weak and sinful creatures to quarrel with the infinitely wise, powerful and good God.

He acts with perfect justice, wisdom and goodness, even when we cannot see the reasons.

## God calls people to repentance (verses 14–18)

God speaks through **conscience, Providence and ministers**.

Elihu considered all three.

At that time, Henry says, **no written divine revelation was known to be available**; written Scripture is now our principal guide.

When God intends a person's good, he may use inward conviction to open the heart, as he opened **Lydia's heart**, and open the ears so that a warning is heard.

These messages aim to keep people from sin, especially **pride**.

Sinners who indulge sinful plans and pride are moving toward destruction.

Turning them away from sin rescues them from final judgment.

An awakened conscience that restrains sin is a great mercy.

## God uses affliction for the soul's good (verses 19–28)

Job concluded from his illness that God was angry with him.

His friends made the same assumption.

Elihu explained that the Lord often permits **bodily suffering for spiritual good**.

Understanding this can help us respond wisely to sickness as a means by which God speaks.

Pain entered the world through sin.

Yet through divine grace, bodily suffering can help the soul.

When an affliction has accomplished its purpose, it may be removed.

A **ransom or atoning sacrifice** has been found.

Henry identifies **Jesus Christ** as both **the Messenger and the Ransom** of which Elihu speaks, just as Job had called him his Redeemer.

Christ is both **the One who purchases redemption and its price**, **the Priest and the Sacrifice**.

A human soul is so valuable, and sin has brought such ruin, that nothing less than the blood of God's Son could provide redemption.

He gave his life as a ransom for many.

When recovery from illness follows forgiveness of sin, that recovery is especially precious.

Everyone who truly repents will find mercy with God.

The works of darkness bear no good fruit.

Sin's apparent gains are much less than the harm it brings.

We must confess sin to God with a broken and humble heart (**1 John 1:9**).

Confession has **three parts**.

**First**, acknowledge **the fact of sin** rather than making excuses.

**Second**, acknowledge **its wrongness**: I have distorted what is right.

**Third**, acknowledge **its folly**: I acted foolishly and without understanding.

We have every reason to confess this way.

## Elihu asks Job to listen (verses 29–33)

Elihu taught that God's gracious purpose toward human beings is to rescue them from everlasting misery and bring them into lasting happiness.

Whatever he uses to turn us away from ruin deserves our thanksgiving, even when the experience itself is painful.

Those who finally perish are without excuse because they refused the healing offered to them.
