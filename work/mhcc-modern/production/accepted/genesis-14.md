# Genesis 14 — Modern English

## The battle of the kings and Lot's captivity (verses 1–12)

Wars between nations often occupy a large place in historical accounts.

But this particular war would not have been recorded in Scripture if Abram and Lot had not been involved.

Lot had chosen to live in prosperous Sodom because he wanted its advantages, although the city was full of wickedness.

Among all the descendants of Canaan, its inhabitants were especially ready for God's vengeance.

The invaders came from Chaldea and Persia, which were then still small kingdoms.

They captured Lot along with the other inhabitants and took his possessions.

Lot was a righteous man and Abram's nephew. Yet he suffered alongside his wicked neighbors.

Neither our own godliness nor our relationship with those favored by God can guarantee that we will escape a time of public judgment.

Many upright people suffer because they live among wicked neighbors.

We should therefore wisely separate ourselves from such company, or at the very least show clearly that we do not share their sins (2 Corinthians 6:17).

As such a close relative of Abram, Lot ought to have been Abram's companion and disciple.

Having chosen to live in Sodom, he had only himself to blame when he shared in Sodom's losses.

When we leave the path of duty, we place ourselves outside the protection we might otherwise expect from God.

We cannot reasonably expect that a decision driven by sinful desires will end in comfort.

The invaders also took Lot's goods.

God is right to take away things we enjoy when we have allowed those very things to rob us of the enjoyment of him.

## Abram rescues Lot (verses 13–16)

Abram seized this opportunity to prove that his friendship toward Lot was genuine.

We should be ready to help people in distress, especially relatives and friends.

Even when others have failed to do their duty toward us, we must not neglect our duty toward them.

Abram rescued the captives.

Whenever we have the opportunity, we should do good to everyone.

## Melchizedek blesses Abram (verses 17–20)

Melchizedek is described as king of Salem, which is thought to have been the place later known as Jerusalem.

It is generally understood that he was an ordinary human being.

The apostle's words in Hebrews 7:3 mean only that Scripture says nothing about Melchizedek's ancestors.

That silence is intended to lift our thoughts toward Christ, whose generation cannot be fully declared.

Bread and wine were suitable refreshments for Abram's exhausted followers.

It is remarkable that Christ later appointed those same things as signs commemorating his body and blood—the true food and drink of the soul.

Melchizedek blessed Abram in God's name.

He also blessed God in Abram's name.

We should thank God for the blessings received by others as sincerely as we thank him for our own.

Jesus Christ, our great High Priest, is the Mediator of both our prayers and our praises.

He does not merely present ours to God; he also offers his own prayers and praises on our behalf.

Abram gave Melchizedek a tenth of the spoils (Hebrews 7:4).

When God shows us an extraordinary mercy, it is right to express our gratitude through a special act of godly charity.

Jesus Christ, our great Melchizedek, deserves our homage and humble acknowledgment as both King and Priest.

Not merely a tenth, but everything we have, should be given over to him.

## Abram refuses the king of Sodom's offer (verses 21–24)

Notice the grateful offer made by the king of Sodom: “Give me the people, and take the goods.”

Gratitude teaches us to repay, as far as we can, those who have worked hard, faced danger, and spent their resources for our benefit.

But Abram generously refused the offer.

He explained why: he did not want the king to be able to say, “I made Abram rich.”

That would cast doubt on God's promise and covenant, as though God could not have enriched Abram without the spoils of Sodom.

For the sake of their testimony, God's people must avoid conduct that appears petty, mercenary, greedy, or selfish.

Abram could trust the Possessor of heaven and earth to provide for him.

## Chapter-level editorial review

Compared in a separate pass with the [original Concise reading text for Genesis 14](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc/mhcc.ii.xiv.html). The source for the future derivative remains published `MHCC 0.1.1`.

- **1–12:** war only recorded because of Abram and Lot, Lot's choice of rich wicked Sodom, invaders from small Chaldean/Persian kingdoms, godliness not immunity to public judgment, 2 Corinthians 6:17, Lot's responsibility and loss of earthly goods that drew his heart from God—all retained.
- **13–16:** Abram helping despite Lot's earlier failure, special obligations to kin and general obligation to do good to everyone—all retained.
- **17–20:** Salem/*supposed* Jerusalem, Melchizedek as *generally thought* human, Hebrews 7:3 based on **silence about ancestry** rather than literal absence, bread/wine and Christ's memorial, double blessing, Mediator of both prayers and praises, Hebrews 7:4, offerings as gratitude and Christ deserving all—all retained.
- **21–24:** grateful offer as recompense for cost and risk, Abram's refusal and its specific reason, avoiding any implication that Sodom rather than God's covenant made Abram rich, faithful stewardship—all retained.

Four original exposition groups preserved. The original has a visible repeated word *“promise promise”* in the final paragraph of one digital witness; it supplies no second meaning and was modernized once as a clear phrase. The repetition is documented here, not silently treated as a new authorial argument; original source remains unmodified. Separate same-assistant editorial pass, not external review.
