# 2 Samuel 2 — Modern English

## David is made king at Hebron (verses 1–7)

After Saul died, many people gathered around David at **Ziklag** (**1 Chronicles 12:22**).

But David trusted the Lord, who had promised him the kingdom, to give it in the proper time and way.

Confidence in God's promise does not discourage diligent effort.

It encourages it.

Someone chosen for the crown of life should not say, “Then I need do nothing.”

Rather, that person should say, **“Then I will do everything God directs me to do.”**

David made this use of his calling, and all whom God chooses should do likewise.

When we travel or move to a new home, it is a great comfort to know the Lord goes before us.

We may have that confidence when faith and prayer set him before us.

God directed David's steps as promised.

David's rise was gradual.

In the same way, the kingdom of the Messiah, the Son of David, advances by stages.

Christ already is Lord of all, though we do not yet see everything subjected to him.

## Abner supports Ish-bosheth; civil war begins (verses 8–17)

Most of Israel refused to recognize David.

The Lord used that rejection to train his servant for later responsibility and usefulness.

David's conduct under repeated difficulties displayed the strength of genuine godliness.

He was a picture of Christ, whom Israel would not receive even though the Father had anointed him as Prince and Savior.

Abner suggested that the young soldiers should **“play”** before the leaders.

But he was proposing a deadly contest.

Fools make a game out of sin.

Anyone who treats human bloodshed as entertainment is unworthy of being called human.

## Asahel pursues Abner and dies (verses 18–24)

Death often approaches by a path we do not expect.

The very abilities we take the greatest pride in can become our undoing.

**Asahel's speed**, which he trusted so confidently, did not save him.

Instead, it hastened his death.

## The rival armies withdraw (verses 25–32)

Abner appealed to Joab to consider the terrible consequences of civil war.

Those who make light of such unnatural conflict will eventually find it bitter for everyone involved.

People easily appeal to reason when it benefits them, though they refused to listen to reason when it worked against their interests.

Notice how quickly a change in circumstances can change a person's opinion.

What seemed enjoyable in the morning can appear dreadful by night.

Those most eager to enter a dispute often regret it before it ends.

They would have done better to avoid the quarrel at the start, as Solomon counsels.

The same is true of every sin.

Would that people considered early enough how bitter its outcome will be!

The account mentions Asahel's burial.

People distinguish the graves and remains of important individuals from those of others.

But at the resurrection only one distinction will endure forever: **godly or ungodly**.
