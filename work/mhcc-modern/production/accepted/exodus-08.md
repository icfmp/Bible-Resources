## The plague of frogs (verses 1–15)

Pharaoh was struck by a plague of frogs.

Their overwhelming numbers made them a severe affliction to the Egyptians.

God could have sent lions, bears, wolves, or birds of prey against the land.

Instead, he chose creatures that people usually despise.

Whenever God pleases, he can turn even the smallest parts of creation against us.

In this way he humbled Pharaoh.

The Egyptians could neither eat, drink, nor sleep in peace.

Wherever they went, the frogs disturbed them.

God's curse pursues a person everywhere and weighs heavily on everything he does.

Under this plague, Pharaoh finally gave way and promised to let the people go.

Those who defy God and despise prayer will sooner or later be forced to recognize their need of both.

But when Pharaoh saw that he had relief, he hardened his heart once more.

Until God's grace renews the heart, the impressions made by suffering do not last.

Conviction fades, and promises are forgotten.

Until the climate changes, what thaws in the sunshine will freeze again in the shade.

## The plague of gnats (verses 16–19)

The insects came from the dust of the ground.

God can take any part of creation and make it an instrument of judgment against rebels.

Even the dust obeys him.

These insects were both painful and humiliating for the Egyptians.

Their priests took extraordinary care to keep themselves free from vermin.

The plagues against Egypt were connected with the nation's sins, or were made especially severe by its customs.

Pharaoh's magicians tried to reproduce this sign but failed.

They were forced to admit, “This is the finger of God!”

The power that checks and restrains human beings must come from God.

Sooner or later even God's enemies will have to acknowledge his power.

Yet Pharaoh only became more stubborn.

## The plague of flies (verses 20–32)

Pharaoh rose early to continue his false religious devotion to the river.

Should we, then, choose more sleep and idleness when there is work to do for the Lord?

The plague of flies would distinguish the Egyptians from the Hebrews.

The Lord knows those who belong to him.

He will make clear that he has set them apart for himself—perhaps in this world, and certainly in the next.

Pharaoh unwillingly began negotiating with Moses and Aaron.

He agreed to allow sacrifices to Israel's God, provided they took place inside Egypt.

But it would be offensive to God for Israel to offer the Egyptians' sacrifices.

And it would be offensive to the Egyptians if Israel offered to God the calves or oxen that were objects of Egyptian worship.

Those who wish to offer God acceptable worship must separate themselves from the wicked and profane.

They also need to withdraw from the distractions and temptations of the world.

Israel could not keep the Lord's feast among the brick kilns or beside Egypt's pots of meat.

The people had to sacrifice as God commanded—not according to some different arrangement.

Although they were enslaved to Pharaoh, they still had to obey God.

Pharaoh then agreed to let them go into the wilderness, but only if they stayed close enough for him to bring them back.

That is how some sinners behave.

For a moment, frightened by conviction, they part with their sins.

But they do not want those sins to go too far away.

When their fear fades, they return to them.

Moses promised that the plague would be removed and warned Pharaoh not to act deceitfully again.

God is not mocked.

If we think we can trick him with pretend repentance and an insincere surrender, we only deceive ourselves—and with disastrous results.

Pharaoh returned to his stubbornness.

Deeply rooted sinful desires break even strong restraints and lead people to break their promises.

Many seem genuinely serious about repentance, but keep something in reserve: a secret, beloved sin.

They refuse to recognize the danger of eternal misery.

They may turn from other sins, do many good works, give generously, and even submit to painful self-denial.

They may leave a cherished sin alone for a while, allowing it to go only a short distance.

But they will not decide to part with everything sinful and follow Christ, bearing the cross.

Instead, they risk everything to keep their sin.

They are sorrowful but turn away from Christ, determined to keep the world for now.

They hope that some later opportunity will allow them to receive salvation without so costly a surrender.

But at last the poor sinner may be swept away in wickedness, left without hope and forced to lament his folly.
