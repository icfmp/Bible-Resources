## The purpose of the offerings (verses 1–2)

From the fall of humanity until Christ came, offering sacrifices was part of true worship.

Until Israel lived in the wilderness, however, no very detailed regulations seem to have been given for those offerings.

The overall purpose of these laws is clear.

The sacrifices pointed forward to Christ.

They also portrayed the duties, character, privileges and fellowship with God of those who believe in him.

Hardly anything Scripture says about the Lord Jesus is without some connection to his people.

Leviticus begins with the laws of sacrifice.

The oldest form of sacrifice was the burnt offering.

God now gave Moses instructions about it.

These laws took for granted that the people would be willing to bring offerings to the Lord.

Even natural human understanding tells us that people owe honor to their Maker as their Lord.

Sacrifices had been instituted immediately after the fall.

## Burnt offerings from the herd (verses 3–9)

When properly understood, the Levitical ordinances used visible things from the natural world to picture spiritual mysteries and events that were yet to come.

Without that understanding, the ceremonies would appear meaningless.

They pointed to the sufferings of the Son of God, who would become a sacrifice for the sins of the world.

A burning animal was only a faint image of the everlasting misery we have deserved.

Our blessed Lord bore the burden of our guilt in both body and soul when he died.

Consider the details.

**First, the animal had to be without blemish.**

This pointed to the purity and strength of Christ and to the holy lives his people should lead.

**Second, its owner had to offer it freely.**

Service that pleases God must flow from love.

Christ willingly offered himself for us.

**Third, it was offered at the tabernacle entrance.**

There stood the bronze altar that sanctified the gift.

The offerer came to the entrance as someone unworthy to go farther in.

In doing so, the sinner acknowledged that communion with God is impossible apart from sacrifice.

**Fourth, the offerer laid a hand upon the animal's head.**

That action expressed the desire and hope that God would accept the offering as atonement.

**Fifth, the animal was killed before the Lord.**

The act was to be done reverently, in an orderly way that honored God.

It also signified that Christians must put to death the flesh with its corrupt desires and passions.

**Sixth, the priests sprinkled blood upon the altar.**

Since life is in the blood, blood made atonement.

This pointed to our consciences being pacified and cleansed when we receive, through faith, the blood of Jesus Christ sprinkled upon them.

**Seventh, the animal was cut into pieces and burned upon the altar.**

The burning pictured Christ's severe sufferings.

It also pictured the burning love and devotion with which Christians should offer their entire selves—spirit, soul and body—to God.

**Eighth, the offering was called a sweet-smelling sacrifice.**

God received it with pleasure because it obeyed his command and pointed to Christ.

In the same way, believers' spiritual sacrifices become acceptable to God through Jesus Christ (1 Peter 2:5).

## Burnt offerings from sheep, goats or birds (verses 10–17)

People who could not afford to offer a bull could bring a sheep or a goat.

Those too poor to bring even those animals could offer a turtledove or a pigeon, and God would receive it.

The animals chosen were gentle, mild and harmless.

They pointed to Christ's innocence and meekness, qualities that should also appear in Christians.

A poor person's offering pictured Christ's atonement just as fully as an expensive sacrifice.

It also expressed repentance, faith and devotion to God no less completely.

We have no excuse for refusing the reasonable and joyful service God now requires.

Yet even a broken heart, praise or thanksgiving cannot be offered to God unless he first gives us what we need to offer it.

An Israelite could not bring a bull or goat without first receiving it from God.

The more we do in the Lord's service, the more we owe him for the desire, ability and opportunity to do it.

In many matters, God allows us to decide how much time or wealth we will devote to his service.

But where he has placed much within someone's power, a miserly offering does not express a willing heart and is not acceptable.

Let us devote both body and soul to serving him, whatever he may call us to give, risk, do or endure for his sake.
