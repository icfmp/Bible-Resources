# Jonah 4 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Jonah repines at God's mercy to Nineveh, and is reproved. | 1–4 |
| He is taught by the withering of a gourd, that he did wrong. | 5–11 |

## Jonah resents God's mercy (verses 1–4)

God's people usually celebrate and praise **his mercy toward sinners**.

But Jonah made that mercy the reason for accusing God.

He spoke as though showing compassion were a defect in God's nature, when it is actually **one of his greatest glories**.

We all owe our rescue from judgment to his **pardoning, sparing mercy**.

Jonah wished for death.

Henry calls that wish **foolish, passionate and deeply corrupted**.

Pride and lack of charity remained within the prophet.

He had neither expected nor desired **Nineveh's salvation**, but had only wanted to announce and witness its destruction.

He had not been humbled enough concerning his own sins.

Nor had he entrusted his personal honor and safety to the Lord.

In this frame of mind he failed to see **the great good accomplished through his service** and the glory of God's mercy.

We should frequently examine ourselves.

Is this the right thing to say or do?

Can I justify the way I am acting?

Is it right to become angry so quickly, so often or for so long?

Is it right to insult other people during anger?

Is it right to resent **God's mercy toward repentant sinners**?

That was Jonah's sin.

How could we resent something that glorifies God and advances his kingdom?

The conversion of sinners brings **joy in heaven**.

It should bring **joy to us**, not grief.

## God teaches Jonah through the plant (verses 5–11)

Jonah went outside Nineveh but stayed near the city, apparently still wishing to see it destroyed.

People who habitually complain often create troubles for themselves so that they will always have something to resent.

Notice God's tenderness toward his people **even when they are foolish and stubborn**.

A small gift received at exactly the needed moment can become an enormous blessing.

**A gourd in the right place may help us more than a cedar tree.**

Even the smallest creatures can become great troubles or great comforts, depending on God's purpose.

People ruled by intense passions may be thrown down by a tiny disappointment or lifted up by a tiny pleasure.

Created comforts are **perishable**.

**A small worm at the root** can destroy a large and useful plant.

So our earthly comforts may disappear even when we cannot discover the immediate cause.

Sometimes the gift remains with us, but **the joy it brought is gone**.

The creature remains, but the comfort has disappeared.

God sent a wind that made Jonah feel the plant's absence.

Those who love to complain will find no shortage of things to complain about.

When Providence removes loved ones, possessions and pleasures, we must not resent God.

The strongest reason to silence our complaining is this:

**When our gourd is gone, our God has not gone.**

Jonah treated **sin and death** lightly while angry.

But **one soul is worth more than the whole world**, and certainly more than many plants and earthly comforts.

We should care more about our own and others' immortal souls than about the wealth and enjoyment of the world.

God's readiness to show mercy gives us every reason to hope.

Those who grumble may want God's grace reserved only for themselves and people like them.

But **one Lord governs all people and is rich in mercy toward everyone who calls upon him**.

When we are astonished at God's patience toward this stubborn prophet, let us examine our own hearts.

Remember **our own ingratitude and obstinacy**, and marvel at how patiently God deals with us.
