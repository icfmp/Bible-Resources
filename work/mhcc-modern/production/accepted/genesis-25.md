## Abraham's last years and death (verses 1–10)

Even the greatest and most faithful believers do not experience remarkable events every day.

Some years pass quietly, leaving little to record. Abraham's later years were like this.

We are told about his children by Keturah and the arrangements he made for his property.

After these sons were born, he put his household affairs in order with wisdom and fairness.

He did it while he was still alive.

It is wise to carry out whatever responsibilities we can while we have the opportunity to do so.

Abraham lived 175 years.

A full century had passed since he entered Canaan, and throughout that time he lived as a stranger in a foreign land.

Whether our lives are long or short matters little, provided we leave our families a good example and a testimony to the Lord's faithfulness and goodness.

Isaac and Ishmael buried their father together.

It appears that Abraham had helped bring the two brothers together before he died.

Let us not finish the story of Abraham's life without blessing God for such a remarkable testimony of faith's triumph.

## Isaac receives God's blessing; Ishmael's descendants (verses 11–18)

Ishmael had twelve sons, whose descendants formed distinct tribes.

They populated a large region between Egypt and Assyria, known as Arabia.

The size and strength of his family fulfilled God's promise to Hagar and Abraham concerning Ishmael.

## The birth of Esau and Jacob (verses 19–26)

Isaac seems to have faced fewer trials than his father and to have spent much of his life in quiet circumstances.

Jacob and Esau were children received through prayer.

Their parents had remained childless for a long time, but God finally answered them.

God's promises will certainly be fulfilled, yet their fulfillment often seems slow.

Believers' faith is tested and their patience exercised during the waiting.

And blessings long awaited become even sweeter when they arrive.

Isaac and Rebekah remembered God's promise that all nations would be blessed through their descendants.

They therefore longed for children and watched closely for signs of what those children would become.

In all our uncertainties, we should seek the Lord through prayer.

In many struggles with sin and temptation, we may take Rebekah's words upon our own lips: “If this is so, why am I like this?”

If I am God's child, why am I so careless or driven by sinful desires?

And if I am not God's child, why do I fear sin so deeply and feel its burden so heavily?

## The contrasting characters of Esau and Jacob (verses 27–28)

Esau became a skillful and successful hunter.

He pursued wild animals so effectively that he became a conqueror who ruled over his neighbors.

Jacob was a quiet and straightforward man.

He preferred the genuine pleasures of a private life to outward show and false delights.

In his heart he lived as a stranger and pilgrim, and he remained a shepherd all his days.

Isaac and Rebekah had only these two sons.

One became his father's favorite, the other his mother's.

Godly parents may naturally feel especially drawn toward a child who loves the Lord.

Yet they must never show unfair favoritism.

Let affection lead them to treat every child justly and equally. Otherwise, serious trouble will follow.

## Esau sells his birthright (verses 29–34)

Here we read about the bargain made between Jacob and Esau.

The birthright belonged to Esau by birth, but to Jacob by God's promise.

It concerned spiritual privileges.

Jacob desired the birthright, but he tried to obtain it by dishonest means, inconsistent with his otherwise straightforward character.

He was right to desire the best gifts earnestly.

He was wrong to take advantage of his brother's need.

The question was not who would inherit their father's ordinary possessions. That inheritance was not what Jacob sought in this bargain.

The birthright included the future possession of Canaan by his descendants and the covenant made with Abraham concerning Christ, the promised Seed.

Jacob valued these promises because he believed.

Esau despised them because he did not believe.

We should agree with Jacob in valuing the birthright but carefully reject his dishonest way of obtaining it.

Even the greatest advantage must never be sought through deceit.

Jacob's stew attracted Esau's attention.

“Let me have some of that red food!” he said.

That is why he was called *Edom*, meaning “red.”

The desire to satisfy bodily appetites has ruined thousands of precious souls.

Those who let their hearts follow what their eyes desire (Job 31:7), or live to serve their stomachs, will eventually suffer for it.

If we practice denying ourselves, many temptations lose much of their power.

We should not imagine that Esau was actually dying of hunger in Isaac's household.

His words seem rather to mean, “I am going to die one day.”

He was saying, in effect: “I may never live to inherit Canaan or enjoy those supposed future blessings. What difference does it make who receives them after I die?”

These were the words of a godless man, as the apostle describes him (Hebrews 12:16).

That is why Scripture condemns his contempt for the birthright.

It is foolish beyond measure to give up our share in God, Christ, and heaven for the wealth, honors, and pleasures of the present world.

Such a bargain is as bad as selling a birthright for a bowl of stew.

Esau ate and drank, satisfied his appetite, then got up and went away without serious thought or regret.

In that way he despised his birthright.

By continuing to neglect it, despising it, and justifying what he had done, he made the bargain irreversible.

People are not ruined merely because they do wrong.

They are ruined because they do wrong and refuse to repent.

## Chapter-level editorial audit

Compared in a separate reading pass against the [original Concise Genesis 25](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc/mhcc.ii.xxv.html). `MHCC 0.1.1` remains the pinned primary source.

- **1–10:** unremarkable final years, Keturah, orderly estate arrangements, 175 years/100 years in Canaan, good example, joint burial by Isaac and Ishmael and possible earlier reconciliation—all retained.
- **11–18:** Ishmael's twelve tribes, Arabia between Egypt and Assyria and fulfillment of the promise to Hagar and Abraham—all retained.
- **19–26:** Isaac's relatively quiet life, childlessness and answered prayer, delayed promise, Rebekah's question and both alternative spiritual self-examinations—all retained.
- **27–28:** Esau the hunter and ruler, Jacob the quiet pilgrim/shepherd, divided parental favorites and warning against favoritism even in godly households—all retained.
- **29–34:** birthright by birth versus promise, **spiritual rather than merely material inheritance**, Jacob's right aim and wrong method, Edom, Job 31:7 and Hebrews 12:16, Esau's *future-death* meaning rather than actual starvation, need for repentance rather than justified persistence—all retained.
