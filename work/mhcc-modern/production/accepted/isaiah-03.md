# Isaiah 3 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The calamities about to come upon the land. | 1–9 |
| The wickedness of the people. | 10–15 |
| The distress of the proud, luxurious women of Zion. | 16–26 |

## Judah is deprived of its supports (verses 1–9)

God was about to remove **every support** on which Judah depended.

Jerusalem and the land would become desolate because the people's words and deeds were rebellions against the Lord, **even at his holy temple**.

When people refuse to rely upon God, he may take away their other sources of security. Then they discover how weak they truly are.

**Jesus Christ is the Bread of Life and the Water of Life**. If he is our support, we possess a lasting portion that cannot be taken away (**John 6:27**).

Henry draws three lessons from the threatened judgment.

**First**, the condition of unrepentant sinners is deeply miserable.

**Second**, it is the **soul** that sin most seriously damages.

**Third**, people who continue in sin bring the evil they suffer upon themselves.

## Righteousness and oppression (verses 10–15)

Whether the nation is prosperous or suffering, God's final principle remains unchanged:

**It will be well with the righteous and ill with the wicked.**

The godly therefore have abundant reason to trust him, and sinners have every reason to repent and return.

The time had come for the Lord to demonstrate his power and call leaders to account for their use of **the wealth and authority entrusted to them**.

Neglecting the needs of poor people is sinful.

How much more hateful is the conduct of those who **first reduce people to poverty and then exploit them**!

## The pride and luxury of Zion (verses 16–26)

Isaiah warned the women of Zion that suffering would follow their pride and extravagance.

God notices outward vanity, even in dress.

The threatened judgments corresponded to the sins being condemned.

Henry describes repulsive illness as one form such judgment could take.

The precise kind of ornaments worn is not the essential point. Fashions change, and what seems impressive in one age might look absurd in another.

**Human nature, however, remains the same.**

Wasting time and money on self-display while neglecting **faith, generosity and even justice** displeases the Lord.

Even people who profess religion sometimes treat excessive luxury and ornamentation as morally harmless. But Henry asks why the Holy Spirit would have directed the prophet to expose it so carefully if it were not a serious evil.

Jerusalem would be conquered and reduced to ruins.

The prophet pictures it as **a grieving woman sitting on the ground**.

Henry notes a later Roman medal celebrating Jerusalem's destruction with a similar image of a seated, mourning woman.

Whenever sin is deliberately sheltered **inside a city's walls**, grief and mourning are likely to arrive **at its gates**.
