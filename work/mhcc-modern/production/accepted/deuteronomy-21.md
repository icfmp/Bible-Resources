# Deuteronomy 21 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The expiation of uncertain murder. | 1–9 |
| Respecting a captive taken to wife. | 10–14 |
| The first-born not to be disinherited for private affection. | 15–17 |
| A stubborn son to be stoned. | 18–21 |
| Malefactors not to be left hanging all night. | 22, 23 |

## A murder whose perpetrator is unknown (verses 1–9)

When a murderer could not be identified, Israel was commanded to carry out a solemn ceremony acknowledging and removing the guilt from the land.

This demonstrated the deep horror with which God regarded murder.

His providence has often brought such hidden works of darkness to light.

The guilty person's sin has sometimes been revealed in unexpected ways.

Everyone should recognize the seriousness of murder and help prevent, investigate and punish it lawfully.

The elders solemnly declared that they had neither committed the murder nor helped it take place.

The priests prayed for the country and its people, asking God to show mercy.

Our prayers should seek to counter the evil with which others are filling the world.

We should learn to prevent, detect and oppose murder diligently.

We must also avoid becoming involved in other people's sins.

If we refuse to speak against fruitless works of darkness, we risk sharing in them.

## A soldier and a captive woman (verses 10–14)

The law permitted an Israelite soldier, in certain circumstances, to marry a captive woman if he wished.

Henry emphasizes that **allowing such a marriage did not mean approving of it**.

The rule also revealed how strongly justice and honor should govern marriage, which is a sacred commitment.

## The rights of the firstborn (verses 15–17)

A father was forbidden to disinherit his eldest son without just cause merely because he preferred another child.

This principle concerning children remains binding on parents.

They must give each child the rights that belong to them, without favoritism.

## The stubborn and rebellious son (verses 18–21)

Notice how the offender is described.

He is **stubborn and rebellious**.

A child was not to be punished for limited understanding, slow learning or lack of intelligence.

The problem was deliberate refusal to obey.

Nothing draws people toward sin and makes them more obstinate than drunkenness.

People ruled by drinking forget their duty to honor their parents.

Both the father and mother were required to bring the complaint before the city's elders.

Children who deliberately reject their responsibilities should not blame their parents if affection eventually grows strained.

Under Israel's ancient law, the offender was to be publicly stoned.

Henry sees the punishment as showing the seriousness of persistent contempt for parental authority.

Though this offense does not receive that civil punishment in his own day, it remains offensive to God.

When young people become enslaved early to bodily appetite, the heart hardens and conscience loses its sensitivity.

Such a course leads toward rebellion and ruin.

## The body of someone executed must be removed (verses 22 and 23)

Under the law of Moses, contact with a dead body made a person ceremonially unclean.

An executed person's body therefore could not be left hanging overnight, defiling the land.

This rule has a special connection with Christ.

Someone left hanging in such disgrace was described as **cursed by God**.

Henry explains that this language expressed the deepest humiliation: the person appeared rejected by both heaven and earth.

Moses used those words under the Spirit's guidance so that they could later be applied to Christ.

Through his death on the cross, Jesus endured the law's curse **for us**.

That reveals his love and invites us to trust him.
