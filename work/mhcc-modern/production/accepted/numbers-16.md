## Korah's rebellion against the priesthood (verses 1–11)

Pride and ambition cause much harm both within churches and in nations.

Korah's company challenged God's appointment of Aaron and his family to the priesthood.

They claimed that the whole congregation was holy and favored by God.

But Israel had repeatedly defiled itself through sin and was still living under signs of God's displeasure.

The rebels falsely accused Moses and Aaron of claiming special honor for themselves, when in fact God had called them to their offices.

Henry identifies their attitude as the spirit of people who refuse to accept the authority God has established over them.

He also reflects on the harsh treatment that faithful servants may receive after serving others.

Moses did not immediately retaliate.

He sought guidance from God, as wise people should do when considering how to answer difficult accusations.

He reminded the rebels of the privileges they already enjoyed as Levites.

They were despising gifts from God because they wanted the office he had given someone else.

One way to resist envy of those above us is to consider how many people have fewer opportunities than we do.

## Dathan and Abiram refuse to obey (verses 12–15)

Moses summoned Dathan and Abiram to hear their complaints.

They refused to come.

They made thoroughly false accusations against him.

The people most deserving of honor are sometimes condemned most bitterly.

Moses was a remarkably gentle man, but he became deeply angry when God's honor was attacked through him.

He could not bear to see the people driving themselves toward ruin.

He appealed to God to bear witness to his integrity.

The rebels were ordered to appear with Aaron the following morning, when the morning incense would be offered.

Korah agreed.

Proud and ambitious people often arrange their own humiliation while imagining they are securing advancement.

## God's glory appears; Moses and Aaron plead for the people (verses 16–22)

The same divine glory that had appeared when Aaron was first installed in his office (Leviticus 9:23) now appeared to confirm his calling and defeat his opponents.

Few things are more terrifying to a guilty conscience than the visible glory of God.

It is dangerous to associate closely with people engaged in serious rebellion and to join in their wrongdoing.

Although the Israelites had betrayed Moses and Aaron, the two men continued acting as faithful shepherds of the nation.

Someone else's failure to do right by us never cancels our obligation to seek that person's good.

Their earnest prayer was heard.

## The earth opens beneath the rebels (verses 23–34)

The seventy elders accompanied Moses.

We should do what is right to support lawful authority when it is under unjust attack.

Anyone who wants to avoid sharing a sinner's destruction must separate from that person's evil conduct.

In response to Moses' prayer, God moved the congregation to withdraw from the rebels for safety.

The grace that leads people to separate from evil accompanies salvation.

God left the rebels to their own stubbornness and hard hearts.

Under divine instruction, Moses declared that if these men died ordinarily, he would accept being judged a fraud.

Immediately after he spoke, God opened the ground and swallowed up the rebels.

Their children also perished.

Henry admits that we cannot know all that might justify God's judgment of them, or all the mercy God might otherwise show them; but he maintains that infinite justice cannot do wrong.

The event was wholly miraculous.

God can appoint extraordinary judgments for people who persist in evil.

The earth is still burdened with human wrongdoing; Henry marvels that it does not sink beneath the weight.

The destruction of others should warn us.

If we could hear by faith the cries of those lost under judgment, we would be more diligent about escaping the same condemnation.

## Fire consumes those offering unauthorized incense (verses 35–40)

Fire came from the Lord and consumed the 250 men who were offering incense.

Aaron remained alive among them.

God guards the honor of the worship he has appointed.

He will not allow people to seize offices and religious privileges he has not given them.

The sacrifice of the wicked is offensive to him.

The censers used by the rebels were set apart and then turned into a covering for the altar, so that even those objects would serve God's glory.

The covering reminded future Israelites of this judgment, warning them not to rebel in the same presumptuous way.

The rebels had brought destruction on themselves.

Likewise, those who break God's law and neglect his gospel choose the path of death.

## A new complaint and Aaron's intercession (verses 41–50)

The earth had scarcely closed over the rebels when the people began complaining again.

They had ignored all the warnings.

They even called the condemned rebels God's people and accused divine justice of wrongdoing.

Their stubbornness, after both Sinai's awe-inspiring law and severe judgments, showed how necessary God's grace is to change human hearts.

Love can accomplish what fear alone cannot.

Moses and Aaron again pleaded for mercy.

They understood the seriousness of the people's rebellion.

Aaron hurried with incense and stood between the living and the dead.

He did not burn incense to purify the air.

He was pleading that God's offended justice would be satisfied and the plague stopped.

Because he cared about every Israelite's life, he went as quickly as possible.

We are called to repay evil with good.

Aaron here pictured Christ.

The world suffers from the deadly infection of sin.

Only Jesus Christ's cross and intercession can remove it.

He entered the defiled and dying camp of humanity.

He stood between the Judge and those condemned by sin.

Through his blood we receive redemption and forgiveness.

If we admire Aaron's willingness, how much more should we praise the Savior's love in taking our place and purchasing us with his life!

God showed his love while we were still sinners: **Christ died for us** (Romans 5:8).
