# Revelation 18 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Another angel from heaven proclaims the fall of mystical Babylon. | 1–3 |
| A voice from heaven admonishes the people of God, lest they partake of her plagues. | 4–8 |
| The lamentations over her. | 9–19 |
| The church called upon to rejoice in her utter ruin. | 20–24 |

## Babylon's fall and the call to come out (verses 1–8)

The downfall of **mystical Babylon** has been settled within God's purposes.

John sees another angel from heaven.

Henry suggests that the figure **seems to be Christ himself**, coming to defeat his enemies and spread the light of his gospel among the nations.

Babylon's wickedness is great.

It has abandoned true worship for idols, drawn people of all classes into spiritual unfaithfulness, and kept them loyal through **wealth and luxury**.

Henry understands the image as referring especially to **spiritual commerce**: people gaining riches by exploiting the sins, ignorance and foolishness of others.

God solemnly warns those who hope to receive mercy that they must **come out of Babylon**.

Henry also urges them to support its destruction, meaning the overthrow of the evil system depicted here.

There may be people who genuinely belong to God even within Babylon.

But his people must answer his call and separate from its wrongdoing.

Those who knowingly participate in the sins of the wicked expose themselves to **the same judgments**.

## The kings and merchants mourn (verses 9–19)

Those mourning over Babylon have shared both **its sensual pleasures and its commercial gains**.

The kings of the earth were flattered into idolatry.

Henry accuses Babylon of permitting them to oppress their subjects so long as they remained obedient to her power.

The merchants profited, in his interpretation, by trading in **indulgences, pardons and honors**.

These observations express Henry's particular historical critique of what he regarded as **the Roman Antichrist**, not an identity the passage establishes beyond dispute.

Babylon's allies were willing to share her sinful pleasure and wealth, but **not her punishment**.

Their sorrow was purely worldly.

They did not grieve over **God's anger against sin**, only over their own lost conveniences.

This reveals the worldly spirit Henry associates with Antichrist.

The splendor and riches of wicked people will not protect them when judgment comes; they may even make the loss harder to bear.

The passage speaks of **enslaved people and human souls as commodities**.

Henry connects these images with destructive spiritual commerce that brought ruin to countless souls.

He explicitly observes that such wrongdoing **is not limited to the system he identifies with Rome**.

Successful merchants everywhere should therefore learn to seek **the unsearchable riches of Jesus Christ** alongside their earthly work.

Otherwise, riches may fly away even before death, leaving the desired pleasures gone.

In any case, **death will soon end all their trade**.

Without reconciliation to God, worldly wealth will be exchanged for the grave and unending judgment.

## Heaven rejoices at the end of oppression (verses 20–24)

The same deliverance that makes God's servants rejoice on earth also brings rejoicing to the angels in heaven.

Henry expects even the apostles—whose memory he says was honored at Rome in ways he judged idolatrous—to rejoice in the fall of Babylon.

This is part of his historically situated Protestant reading of the passage.

Babylon's destruction reveals **God's justice**.

Because her fall is final, the persecuting power will never again trouble the faithful.

The vision supplies a sign assuring them of this complete end.

Let us learn from the errors and sins that brought others to ruin.

Recognizing how unstable all earthly possessions and institutions are, **set your affections on things above**.
