## The serpent deceives Eve (verses 1–5)

Satan attacked our first parents to draw them into sin, and the temptation proved disastrous.

The tempter was the devil, appearing in the form of a serpent. His aim was to lead Adam and Eve into sin and separate them from God.

From the beginning, then, the devil was a murderer and a great troublemaker.

He approached the woman when she was alone. That was part of his strategy.

Being alone gives many temptations a special advantage. Fellowship with other believers, on the other hand, greatly strengthens and protects us.

Satan also took advantage of finding Eve near the forbidden tree.

Those who do not want to eat the forbidden fruit should stay away from the forbidden tree.

His purpose in tempting Eve was to use her to tempt Adam. He still sends temptations through people we do not suspect, especially those with the greatest influence over us.

He began by raising a question about whether eating from the tree was really wrong.

He did not reveal his intention at once. His question seemed harmless.

Anyone who wishes to remain safe should be reluctant to engage in conversation with the tempter.

Satan misquoted God's command and spoke mockingly. He has been both a liar and a scoffer from the beginning, and those who mock in the same way show themselves to be his children.

His method is to make God's law seem uncertain or unreasonable, and then lead people into sin.

Our wisdom is to hold firmly to God's commands and honor them highly.

Has God really forbidden lying, misusing his name, drunkenness, and other sins?

Yes, I am sure he has. His commands are right, and by his grace I will keep them.

Eve showed weakness by continuing the conversation. The question itself should have warned her that the serpent meant no good, and she should have turned away.

Satan teaches people first to doubt, and then to deny.

He promised that eating the fruit would benefit them. He wanted them to become dissatisfied with their present condition, as though it were not as good as it could or ought to be.

No outward circumstances will make us content unless our minds learn contentment.

He also tempted them with advancement, as if they were fit to become gods.

Satan had ruined himself by wanting to be like the Most High. Now he wanted to infect our first parents with that same desire and bring them to ruin as well.

He continues to win people over by planting harsh thoughts about God and false hopes of what sin can gain them.

Let us always think of God as the greatest good, and of sin as the greatest evil.

In that way, let us resist the devil, and he will flee from us.

## Adam and Eve fall into sin and misery (verses 6–8)

Notice the steps of their transgression. They were not steps upward, but downward toward destruction.

First, **Eve saw.** Much sin enters through the eyes.

We must not deliberately look at what we are in danger of desiring sinfully (Matthew 5:28).

Second, **she took.** This was her own action.

Satan can tempt us, but he cannot force us. He may persuade us to throw ourselves down, but he cannot throw us down himself (Matthew 4:6).

Third, **she ate.** When she first looked, she may not have intended to take the fruit. When she took it, she may not have intended to eat. But that is where it ended.

Wisdom stops sinful desires at their first stirring, before we begin to act on them.

Fourth, **she gave the fruit to her husband.** Those who do wrong are often willing to draw others into the same wrongdoing.

Fifth, **Adam ate.** He passed over the tree of life, from which he was free to eat, and chose the forbidden tree of knowledge instead.

In doing so, he showed contempt for what God had given him and a desire for what God had chosen not to give.

He wanted to have whatever pleased him and do whatever pleased him.

In one word, his sin was **disobedience** (Romans 5:19)—disobedience to a clear, simple, express command.

Adam had no corrupt nature within him to betray him. His freedom of will was still strong, neither weakened nor impaired.

Yet he turned aside so quickly, and brought all his descendants into sin and ruin.

Who can say, then, that Adam's sin did little harm?

Only when it was too late did Adam and Eve see how foolish they had been to eat the forbidden fruit.

They saw the happiness they had lost, the misery into which they had fallen, the loving God they had offended, and the grace and favor they had forfeited.

See what dishonor and trouble sin brings! Wherever it enters, it causes harm and destroys comfort.

Sooner or later, sin brings shame. It will either be the shame of genuine repentance, which ends in glory, or the shame and everlasting contempt to which the wicked will rise on the last day.

People who have sinned often care more about preserving their reputation before others than about receiving pardon from God.

Their excuses are worthless. Like Adam and Eve's fig-leaf coverings, they cannot make the situation any better.

Yet we are all inclined to hide our sins as Adam did.

Before the fall, Adam and Eve would have welcomed God's gracious presence with humble joy. Now his coming terrified them.

No wonder they also became frightened of themselves and overwhelmed with confusion.

The tempter's promise was exposed as a lie. Satan had said they would be safe, but now they could not even feel safe.

Adam and Eve had become miserable comforters to one another.

## God calls Adam and Eve to account (verses 9–13)

Listen to God's startling question: “Adam, where are you?”

Those who wander away from God through sin should seriously consider where they are.

They are far from everything good, surrounded by enemies, enslaved to Satan, and on the road to complete ruin.

This lost sheep would have wandered endlessly if the good Shepherd had not searched for him and shown him that he could never be happy or at peace on that path.

If sinners truly considered where they stood, they would not rest until they returned to God.

When people are questioned about wrongdoing, they often admit only what is so obvious that they cannot deny it.

Like Adam, we have good reason to fear approaching God unless we are covered and clothed with the righteousness of Christ.

Sin appears most clearly in the mirror of God's commandment. That is why God placed his command before Adam, and why we should look into it ourselves.

Instead of confessing their sin fully and accepting their shame, Adam and Eve excused themselves and blamed others.

People who give in to temptation have a strange tendency to blame God for tempting them—as though misusing his gifts could excuse breaking his laws.

Those willing to enjoy the pleasure and profit of sin are often unwilling to accept its blame and shame.

Satan's temptations are deceptions. His arguments are lies, and his attractions are traps.

Do not believe him when he speaks pleasantly.

The deceitfulness of sin hardens the heart (Romans 7:11; Hebrews 3:13).

But Satan's cunning cannot excuse our sin. Though he is the tempter, **we are the sinners**.

Being deceived should not lessen our sorrow for sin. It should deepen our anger at ourselves for allowing a known deceiver and sworn enemy of our souls to mislead us.

## The serpent is cursed and the promised Seed is revealed (verses 14–15)

God began his sentence where sin had begun—with the serpent.

The devil's instruments must share in his punishment.

Under the figure of the serpent, the devil was sentenced to be brought low and cursed by God, hated and rejected by humanity, and finally destroyed by the great Redeemer.

The crushing of the serpent's head points to that final defeat.

War was declared between the Seed of the woman and the seed of the serpent.

Because of this hostility, grace and corruption remain in constant conflict within the hearts of God's people.

Satan uses their remaining corruption to strike, sift, and seek to devour them.

Heaven cannot be reconciled with hell, nor light with darkness. Neither can Satan be reconciled with a soul made holy by God.

There is also an ongoing struggle in the world between the wicked and the godly.

Yet here God graciously promised **Christ**, who would deliver fallen humanity from Satan's power.

The dawn of the gospel had begun. As soon as the wound was inflicted, the remedy was provided and revealed.

This promise of a Savior came without anyone asking or expecting it.

Without a revelation of mercy to give some hope of pardon, a sinner convinced of guilt would sink into despair and become hardened.

Through faith in this promise, our first parents and the patriarchs who lived before the flood were justified and saved.

Three things are revealed here about Christ.

First, **his incarnation**—his coming in human flesh.

What encouragement it gives sinners to know that their Savior is the woman's Seed, bone of our bone (Hebrews 2:11, 14)!

Second, **his sufferings and death**, pictured by the serpent bruising his heel—that is, his human nature.

Christ's sufferings also continue in the sufferings of the saints for his name. The devil tempts, persecutes, and kills them, bruising Christ's heel, for Christ is afflicted in their afflictions.

Yet while his heel is bruised on earth, the Head is in heaven.

Third, **his victory over Satan through those sufferings**.

Christ defeated Satan's temptations and rescued souls from his grasp.

Through his death he struck a fatal blow against the devil's kingdom—a wound to the serpent's head that can never be healed.

As the gospel advances, Satan falls.

## The punishment of humanity (verses 16–19)

Because of her sin, the woman was sentenced to sorrow and subjection.

These punishments corresponded to her sin, in which she had sought to satisfy what pleased her eyes and flesh, and to gratify her pride.

Sin brought sorrow into the world and turned it into a valley of tears.

No wonder our sorrows multiply when our sins do too.

The words “He shall rule over you” express, in Henry's understanding, the same duty found in the command for wives to submit to their husbands.

If the man had not sinned, he would always have ruled with wisdom and love. If the woman had not sinned, she would always have obeyed with humility and gentleness.

Adam blamed his wife. Yet although she was wrong to persuade him to eat the forbidden fruit, he was wrong to listen.

At God's judgment, such empty excuses will turn against those who make them.

God also marked Adam with his displeasure in three ways.

First, **his dwelling place was cursed**.

God had given the earth to humanity as a pleasant home. Now the ground was cursed because of human sin.

Yet Adam himself was not cursed as the serpent was; the curse fell on the ground for his sake.

Second, **his work and its benefits became bitter**.

Labor remains our duty, and we must perform it faithfully. It is also part of the sentence placed on humanity, a sentence that idleness defiantly refuses.

The discomfort and exhaustion that accompany work are just punishments. We must bear them patiently, for they are less than our sin deserves.

Even the food produced through our labor becomes less pleasant.

Yet humanity was not sentenced to eat dust like the serpent, but to eat the plants of the field.

Third, **human life became short**.

Since our days contain so much trouble, their shortness is in one sense a mercy.

Yet death remains dreadful to our nature, even when life itself is unpleasant. So death completes the sentence.

Sin brought death into the world. Had Adam not sinned, he would not have died.

Adam yielded to temptation, but the Savior resisted it.

How wonderfully the satisfaction made by our Lord Jesus through his sufferings and death answers the sentence passed on our first parents!

Did pain in childbirth come through sin? We read of the travail of Christ's soul (Isaiah 53:11), and the pains of death that held him (Acts 2:24).

Did subjection come through sin? Christ was made subject to the law (Galatians 4:4).

Did the curse come through sin? Christ became a curse for us and died a cursed death (Galatians 3:13).

Did thorns come through sin? He was crowned with thorns for us.

Did sweat come through sin? He sweated for us, as though great drops of blood were falling.

Did sorrow come through sin? He was a man of sorrows. In his agony, his soul was overwhelmed with sorrow.

Did death come through sin? He became obedient even to death.

The remedy is as wide as the wound.

Blessed be God for his Son, our Lord Jesus Christ!

## God clothes Adam and Eve (verses 20–21)

God named the man *Adam*, meaning “red earth.” Adam named the woman *Eve*, meaning “life.”

Adam's name points to the dying body; Eve's name points to the living soul.

Adam probably had the promised Redeemer in mind when he called his wife Eve, or life.

That Redeemer would be the life of all believers, and in him all the families of the earth would be blessed.

See also how God still cared for our first parents despite their sin.

Clothing came into the world with sin.

We have little reason to be proud of our clothes. They are reminders of our shame.

When God made clothes for Adam and Eve, he made them warm, strong, coarse, and plain—not splendid scarlet robes, but garments of skin.

Those who have little to wear should learn not to complain. If they have food and clothing, they can be content; they are as well provided for as Adam and Eve were.

Those who dress richly should learn not to make outward clothing their chief adornment.

It is supposed that the animals whose skins became their clothing were killed not for food, but for sacrifice, as a picture of Christ, the great Sacrifice.

Adam and Eve had made fig-leaf coverings, too narrow to wrap themselves in properly (Isaiah 28:20).

All our own attempts at righteousness are like those inadequate rags.

But God made them garments of skin—large, strong, durable, and suitable.

So it is with the righteousness of Christ.

Therefore, put on the Lord Jesus Christ.

## Adam and Eve are driven from Paradise (verses 22–24)

God told the man to leave the garden: he could no longer live there and enjoy it.

But Adam loved the place and was unwilling to go. So God made him leave.

This signified that Adam and all his guilty descendants had been shut out from the fellowship with God that had been the happiness and glory of Paradise.

Yet Adam was sent only to work the ground from which he had been taken.

He was sent to a place of labor, not a place of torment.

Our first parents lost the privileges of their innocent condition, but they were not abandoned to despair.

The way to the tree of life was closed.

From then on, it was useless for Adam or his descendants to expect righteousness, life, and happiness through the covenant of works.

That covenant's command had been broken, and its curse remained fully in force.

If we are judged by that covenant, we are all ruined.

God revealed this to Adam not to drive him to despair, but to stir him to seek life and happiness in the promised Seed.

Through that Seed, a new and living way into God's most holy presence has been opened for us.
