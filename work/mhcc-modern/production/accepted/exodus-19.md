## God proposes his covenant at Sinai (verses 1–8)

Moses was called up the mountain to serve as the messenger of the covenant.

But the One who originated it and brought it into being was God himself.

He granted this blessed covenant entirely through his own free grace.

The covenant established at Sinai was a **national covenant**.

Through it, Israel became a people living under Jehovah's government.

It was a picture of the new covenant God would make with true believers in Jesus Christ.

But like other pictures and shadows, it was not the full reality of the good things to come.

Israel, as a nation, broke its covenant.

The Lord therefore promised a new covenant in which he would write his law not on stone tablets but on the people's hearts (Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:7–10).

The covenant that these passages describe as passing away was the **national covenant** that Israel forfeited through sin.

Unless we understand this distinction, we are likely to misunderstand the Old Testament.

We must not imagine that the Jewish nation was under a covenant of works.

Such a covenant knows nothing of repentance, faith in a Mediator, forgiveness of sins or grace.

But neither should we imagine that every Israelite possessed the character and privileges of a true believer, actually sharing in the covenant of grace.

**All** the people lived under a gracious administration and received outward privileges that could assist them toward salvation.

Yet most of them—like many who publicly profess Christianity—settled for the outward privileges and went no further.

Israel agreed to the covenant's terms.

With one voice the people declared that they would do everything the Lord had said.

How good it would have been if their hearts had truly matched their words!

Moses, serving as a mediator, reported their response to God.

Christ is the greater Mediator.

As our Prophet, he reveals God's will to us through his commands and promises.

Then, as our Priest, he brings our spiritual sacrifices before God.

These are not merely prayers and praises.

They also include sincere love for God and holy resolutions, all produced within us by Christ's own Spirit.

## Israel prepares to hear God's law (verses 9–15)

God delivered the law in a solemn and awe-inspiring way so that Israel would recognize the majesty of the One who spoke.

The manner of its giving also exposed the people's guilt and taught them that their own obedience could never make them stand righteous before God's judgment.

Through the law, sinners learn three things: **what they ought to be, what they actually are, and what they lack**.

They discover the nature, necessity and glory of redemption—and of being made holy.

When the law has taught a sinner to flee to Christ and love him, that same law becomes the rule by which the believer lives in obedience and faith.

## The Lord descends upon Sinai (verses 16–25)

No sermon like this had ever been preached before, nor has there been one like it since: God spoke to his church in the wilderness.

We might expect such fearsome signs to suppress human arrogance and curiosity.

Yet the hard heart of an unawakened sinner can treat even the most terrible warnings and judgments lightly.

As we draw near to God, we must remember his holiness and greatness.

We must also remember our own smallness and defilement.

We cannot stand before his judgment on the basis of perfect obedience to his righteous law.

When sinners become convinced of their guilt, they ask what they must do to be saved.

The answer is to believe in the Lord Jesus Christ and be saved.

The Holy Spirit first uses the law to convince us of sin.

Then he takes the things of Christ and makes them known to our souls.

The gospel tells us that Christ redeemed us from the law's curse by becoming a curse for us.

Through his blood we receive redemption, including the forgiveness of sins.

Through him we are justified from everything from which the law of Moses could not justify us.

Yet God's law remains binding as a rule of life.

The Son of God came down from heaven and endured poverty, shame, agony and death.

He did this not only to redeem us from the law's curse.

He also did it to bind us more closely to obey its commands.
