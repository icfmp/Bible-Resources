# Psalm 106 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The happiness of God's people. | 1–5 |
| Israel's sins. | 6–12 |
| Their provocations. | 13–33 |
| Their rebellions in Canaan. | 34–46 |
| Prayer for more complete deliverance. | 47, 48 |

## The joy of God's people (verses 1–5)

Neither our sins nor our sufferings should keep us from **giving God glory and praise**.

The less deserving we are, the more astonishing his kindness becomes.

Those who trust in **the Redeemer's righteousness** should try to follow his example, praising him by both word and action.

God's people have good reason for joy.

They have no need to envy the pleasures or pride of people who live only for this world.

## Israel's sins and God's faithfulness (verses 6–12)

The psalm now turns to confession.

We must acknowledge that **the Lord has acted rightly**, while **we have acted wickedly**.

Though correction may be fully deserved, there is still hope that God will not abandon his people completely.

Even when suffering, they admit their guilt before him.

We begin to distrust the Lord when **we forget his earlier mercies**.

If God did not save us **for his own name's sake**, demonstrating his power and grace, all of us would perish.

## Repeated rebellion and the hunger of the soul (verses 13–33)

Those who refuse to wait for God's counsel may justly be left to follow their own desires and schemes.

**Excessive desire can become sinful even when the thing desired is lawful**.

God showed displeasure toward that conduct by allowing unrest, an accusing conscience and painful self-reproach.

Some people eat lavishly every day and possess strong, healthy bodies, yet their souls are wasting away.

They have no love for God, no thankfulness and no appetite for **the Bread of life**.

Feeding the body while starving the soul is dreadful folly.

Even faithful believers have abundant reason to say that **only the Lord's mercy has kept them from destruction**.

We have often set up idols within our hearts, clinging to forbidden things.

Had **One greater than Moses** not stood to turn away the Lord's anger, we would have been destroyed.

God dealt severely even with Moses for **rash words**.

What, then, do people deserve when they frequently speak proudly and wickedly?

The Lord may justly remove people whose friendship is a blessing to us when we irritate them, provoke them and grieve them continually.

## Israel's failures in Canaan and the final appeal (verses 34–48)

Israel's conduct in Canaan and the Lord's dealings with the people teach that **the path of sin runs downhill**.

Neglecting a commanded duty opens the door to committing further sins.

The Israelites failed to drive out the pagan peoples and then learned their practices.

One sin led to many others and brought divine judgment.

In part, their sin became **its own punishment**.

Those who led sinners into evil may later become instruments of their ruin.

**Satan first tempts and then torments**.

Yet eventually God showed pity to his people for the sake of his covenant.

His unchanging compassion and faithful love led him to replace judgment with mercy.

Henry explains that when Scripture speaks of God's **repenting**, it does not mean a change in his merciful nature, but a change in how he deals with the people.

The condition of the visible church should sober us.

If Christian nations persist in grave sin, we should not be surprised when God humbles them.

Without widespread and deep repentance, they can expect further trouble.

The psalm closes with **a prayer that the Lord will complete his people's deliverance** and praise for what he has already begun to do.

May the entire world soon answer **Amen**.
