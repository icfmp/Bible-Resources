# Exodus 32 — Modern English

| --- | --- |
| The people cause Aaron to make a golden calf. | 1–6 |
| God's displeasure, The intercession of Moses. | 7–14 |
| Moses breaks the tables of the law, He destroys the golden calf. | 15–20 |

## Israel makes the golden calf (verses 1–6)

While Moses was on the mountain receiving God's law, the people gathered noisily around Aaron.

They had grown tired of waiting for Moses to return.

Impatience leaves people exposed to many temptations.

We must wait for the Lord until he comes, even when he seems to delay.

The people readily gave up their earrings to make an idol.

Their willingness should shame our reluctance to give generously in the service of the true God.

They did not complain about the cost of idolatry.

Why, then, should we begrudge what true religion requires?

Aaron made an image of an ox or calf, shaping it with an engraving tool.

Then the people offered sacrifices to it.

They had put an image before themselves and exchanged God's truth for a lie.

Their sacrifices therefore became detestable.

Only a few days earlier, in that very place, they had heard God command them not to make a carved image.

They had also solemnly promised to obey everything he had said (Exodus 24:7).

Yet they broke his explicit command and defied his clear warning **before even leaving the place where they had made the covenant**.

This shows that the law has no more power to make us holy than to justify us.

Through it we learn what sin is, but we do not receive sin's cure.

God had appointed Aaron to the priesthood.

Yet a man who had disgraced himself by making an altar for a golden calf could never claim to deserve the honor of serving at God's altar.

He had to acknowledge that he owed it entirely to free grace.

This leaves no room for pride or boasting.

## God's anger and Moses' intercession (verses 7–14)

God told Moses that the Israelites had corrupted themselves.

Sin corrupts the sinner, and people corrupt themselves by yielding to their own sinful desires.

They had left the right path.

That is what sin does: it turns us away from duty into a wrong way.

They had quickly forgotten God's mighty works.

God sees what human beings cannot detect.

No evil in the world is hidden from him.

We could not bear to witness even a thousandth part of the wickedness God sees every day.

God revealed the greatness of his just displeasure in a way that human beings could understand.

Moses prayed for the people's deliverance from ruin.

In this, he pictured Christ, through whose mediation alone God reconciles the world to himself.

Moses appealed to **God's glory**.

Just as honoring God's name ought to be our first request—as it is in the Lord's Prayer—it should also be our principal argument when we pray.

God's promises should likewise be the basis of our prayers.

He is fully able to do everything he has promised.

See how powerful prayer can be!

In response to Moses, God showed his purpose to spare the people, although earlier he had appeared determined to destroy them.

Scripture calls this change in the **outward disclosure** of God's purpose his relenting from the threatened judgment.

## Moses destroys the idol (verses 15–20)

What a sharp contrast Moses experienced!

He came down from a mountain where he had enjoyed fellowship with God and entered a world full of wickedness.

In God's presence he had seen only purity and what is pleasing to him.

Among the people he now found sin and conduct that provoked the Lord.

To show that the idol was nothing at all, Moses ground the golden calf into dust.

He mixed the powder with the people's drink.

This pictured the truth that those who turn away in heart will be made to taste the bitter consequences of their own ways.

## Aaron's excuse and the judgment on idolaters (verses 21–29)

No wise person ever offered a more foolish and worthless excuse than Aaron did.

We should never allow other people's words or actions to draw us into sin.

Others can tempt us, but they cannot force us to sin.

When Moses approached, the people's dancing turned to trembling.

Their sin had exposed them to shame.

Moses did not try to remove that disgrace by concealing what had happened or offering a misleading explanation.

He dealt with it by punishing the wrongdoing.

The Levites were commanded to kill the leaders of this rebellion.

Henry says that those executed were the people who openly persisted in standing against God.

Those who persist in sin are heading toward destruction.

In the morning some of these people had been shouting and dancing.

Before night they were dead.

How quickly God's judgments can overturn the careless pleasure of people secure in their sins.

## Moses prays again for the people (verses 30–35)

Moses called Israel's wrongdoing a **great sin**.

One task of ministers is to show people the seriousness of their sins.

The dreadful evil of sin is seen in the great price required for pardon.

Moses pleaded with God for mercy.

He did not come to invent excuses for the people; he came to seek atonement.

We should not understand Moses to mean that he would willingly suffer **eternal destruction** in the people's place.

We must love our neighbors as ourselves, not more than ourselves.

But with the mind of Christ, Moses was willing to lay down his earthly life, however painfully, if it could preserve his people.

Even Moses could not completely turn aside God's anger.

That showed that the law given through Moses could not reconcile sinners to God or give them perfect peace with him.

**Only in Christ** does God forgive sin so completely that he remembers it no more.

This history also shows that a proud and worldly heart cannot long endure God's holy commands, humbling truths and spiritual worship.

But a god, a priest, a form of worship, a doctrine and a sacrifice adapted to worldly desires will always find plenty of followers.

Even the gospel itself can be distorted to suit worldly tastes.

How good it is for us that the Prophet greater than Moses, incomparably stronger and more merciful, has made atonement for our souls and continues to intercede for us.

Let us rejoice in his grace!

## Editorial verification — Exodus 32

**Secondary source reading:** [CCEL, Exodus 32](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc.iii.xxxii.html), corroborated for the defective clause against [a separate public transcript](https://www.christianity.com/bible/web/exodus/32-12). Baseline production source: exact **MHCC 0.1.1**, still pending native XML verification.

- **1–6:** impatient waiting, generous gifts for false worship versus reluctance in true worship, carved calf and polluted sacrifices, contradiction of the recent command and covenant **Exodus 24:7**, inability of law to sanctify/justify, Aaron depending on free grace — retained.
- **7–14:** sin as **self-corruption** and deviation from duty, God seeing all evil, humanly expressed just displeasure, Moses' intercession as a figure of Christ, **God's glory and promises as prayer's grounds**, and divine “repenting” explained as a change in the **outward revelation of his purpose**, not a change in God's being — retained.
- **15–20:** contrast of God's pure presence and sinful world, idol ground to dust, sinners made to drink their own ways — retained.
- **21–29:** Aaron's invalid excuse, human temptation not compulsion, shame met by punishment **rather than concealment**, leaders openly persisting, sudden judgment after careless celebration — retained as Henry's account.
- **30–35:** great sin and price of pardon, Moses pleading for atonement, explicit denial that he would accept **eternal ruin**, willingness to die bodily, limits of Mosaic mediation, **Christ alone** fully reconciling, worldly corruption of worship and gospel, Christ's stronger mediation — retained.

**Textual caution, 7–14:** The secondary witnesses share a syntactically defective clause immediately after “the greatness of his just displeasure”: the words corresponding to “after the manner of men who would have prayer of Moses could save them” do not form a complete sentence. The modern manuscript records the **independently intelligible adjacent assertions** (displeasure expressed in human terms; Moses pleading; Christ's mediation) but does **not** claim to have recovered the exact missing connection.
