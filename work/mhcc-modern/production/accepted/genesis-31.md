# Genesis 31 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Jacob departs secretly. | 1–21 |
| Laban pursues Jacob. | 23–35 |
| Jacob's complaint of Laban's conduct. | 36–42 |
| Their covenant at Galeed. | 43–55 |

## Jacob leaves Laban's household (verses 1–21)

Scripture gives us a detailed account of the affairs of these families, while saying nothing about what people would call the great political events of that age.

The Bible is meant to teach ordinary people the duties of daily life: how to serve God, enjoy the blessings he gives, and do good wherever he has placed them.

Selfish people imagine that anything which passes them by has been taken from them.

Greed can become so powerful that it swallows up even natural family affection.

The mistake of placing too high a value on worldly wealth lies at the root of covetousness, envy, and every kind of evil.

People whose hearts are set on this world get in one another's way. Each seems to be taking something from everyone else.

That is how discontent, jealousy, and quarrels grow.

Yet there are other riches, more than enough for everyone. Happy are those who seek them first.

Whenever we move from one place to another, we must pay attention to God's command and trust his promise.

If he is with us, we need not be afraid.

We face so many dangers that nothing else can give our hearts lasting courage.

When difficulties come, remembering earlier times when we enjoyed God's special favor and fellowship is deeply refreshing.

We should also often remember the vows we have made to him, so that we do not fail to keep them.

## Laban pursues Jacob, and Rachel's idols are exposed (verses 22–35)

God can put a bridle in the mouths of wicked people and restrain their malice without changing their hearts.

Even when they have no love for God's people, they may pretend otherwise and try to make a virtue of doing what they cannot avoid.

How foolish of Laban to call things his gods when someone could steal them!

Enemies can steal what belongs to us, but they cannot steal our God.

Laban accused Jacob of things Jacob knew nothing about.

Those who entrust their cause to God are still free to defend themselves, provided they do so with gentleness and reverence.

When we read how Rachel stole her father's household images, what a sad picture of wrongdoing appears!

Nahor's family had left the idolatrous Chaldeans. Had that very family now become idolatrous itself?

Yes, it had.

They seem to have been like people in later times who swore both by the Lord and by Malcham (Zephaniah 1:5).

They were also like people in our own day who want to serve both God and wealth.

Many acknowledge the true God with their lips, while their hearts and homes remain full of spiritual idols.

A person who gives himself over to covetousness, as Laban did, has already made the world his god.

He need only live among openly idolatrous people to become an idolater himself—or at least someone who approves of their practices.

## Jacob answers Laban (verses 36–42)

Jacob willingly endured the burning heat of day and the freezing cold of night to become Laban's son-in-law.

What, then, should we refuse to endure in order to become children of God?

Jacob spoke of God as his father's God.

He considered himself unworthy of God's attention, yet he was loved for his father's sake.

He called him both the God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac.

Abraham had died and entered the world where perfect love casts out fear.

Isaac, however, was still alive, honoring the Lord in his heart as the one to be feared and revered.

## Jacob and Laban make a covenant at Galeed (verses 43–55)

Laban could neither justify his own conduct nor prove Jacob guilty. So he wanted to hear no more about their dispute.

He would not admit his fault, as he should have done.

Instead, he proposed a covenant of friendship, and Jacob readily agreed.

They raised a heap of stones to preserve the memory of the occasion.

Henry explains this custom by saying that writing was either unknown or little used at that time.

They offered a sacrifice of peace offerings.

Peace with God gives true comfort to the peace we enjoy with our friends.

The two men then ate together, sharing the meal connected with the sacrifice.

In ancient times, people often confirmed covenants of friendship by eating and drinking together.

God is the judge between people who are in dispute, and his judgment is always right.

Whoever does wrong does so at his own peril.

They gave the place a new name meaning “heap of witness.”

After their angry exchange, they parted as friends.

God is often kinder to us than our fears lead us to expect.

He can direct the hearts of others in our favor far beyond anything we anticipated.

Trusting him is never in vain.

## Editorial verification — Genesis 31

The four original exposition sections were collated in a separate post-drafting pass against [the CrossWire MHCC reading in STEP](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version%3DMHCC@reference%3DGen.31), with the [distinct CCEL Concise reading](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc.ii.xxxi.html) as a comparative witness. The published `MHCC 0.1.1` source includes no recorded Genesis 31 wording correction. This is a chapter-level editorial review, **not** full original-native SWORD reconstruction.

- **1–21:** the detailed family narrative versus unrecorded state affairs; the Bible's concern for ordinary duties, God's service and blessings; selfishness, greed even overcoming natural affection, wealth as root of envy and discord; spiritual possessions sufficient for all; God's command and promise in relocations; courage through his presence, remembered communion and kept vows — all retained.
- **22–35:** the bridle/restraint image **without heart renewal**, hypocritical love, Laban's stealable gods versus the unstealable God, Jacob's innocence and gentle self-defense, Rachel's theft, the Nahor-family/Chaldean contrast, **Zephaniah 1:5** and divided worship, heart-and-home idolatry, worldliness as idolatry and complicity — all retained.
- **36–42:** the heat/frost comparison with becoming God's children, Jacob's felt unworthiness and favor for his father's sake, the titles **God of Abraham** and **Fear of Isaac**, and the difference between dead Abraham's perfect love and living Isaac's reverence — all retained.
- **43–55:** Laban's refusal to own his fault, freely agreed friendship covenant, stone memorial, the author's historically situated statement about limited writing (attributed rather than asserted as independently established fact), peace offering, sacrificial meal and covenant feast, divine judgment and moral accountability, the name **heap of witness**, peaceful departure, and God overcoming our fears — all retained.

**Original editorial irregularity preserved:** the source's *chapter outline* labels Laban's pursuit **23–35**, while the actual exposition section begins at **22–35**. This manuscript faithfully reproduces the outline as printed and labels the exposition by its actual original section scope.
