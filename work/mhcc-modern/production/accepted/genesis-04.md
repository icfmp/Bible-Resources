## Cain and Abel: their work and worship (verses 1–7)

When Cain was born, Eve said, “I have received a man from the Lord.”

Perhaps she believed he was the promised Seed. If so, she was bitterly disappointed.

*Abel* means “vanity.” If Eve thought Cain—the name means “possession”—was the promised Seed, she may have been so taken up with him that her second son seemed like nothing to her.

Notice that both sons had work to do.

God intends everyone to have some useful occupation in this world. Parents should teach their children to work.

“Give them a Bible and a calling,” said the good Mr. Dod, “and God be with them.”

We may believe that, after the fall, God instructed Adam to shed the blood of innocent animals and burn part or all of their bodies.

This would picture the punishment sinners deserve—bodily death and the wrath of God, often represented by fire—as well as the sufferings of Christ.

The worship of God is no recent invention. It began at the beginning. It is the good, ancient way (Jeremiah 6:16).

Cain's offering and Abel's were different.

Cain showed a proud, unbelieving heart. That is why God rejected both him and his offering.

Abel came before God as a sinner, following God's appointed way.

His sacrifice expressed humility, sincerity, and believing obedience.

Through the promised Seed, Abel sought the blessings of the new covenant of mercy. God gave a sign that his sacrifice was accepted.

Abel offered by faith; Cain did not (Hebrews 11:4).

In every age there have been two kinds of worshippers, like Cain and Abel.

Some proudly and stubbornly reject the gospel way of salvation and try to please God through methods of their own invention.

Others come to him humbly, believing, by the way he has revealed.

Cain nurtured bitter anger toward Abel. He allowed discontent and rebellion against God to grow in his heart.

But God sees all our sinful feelings and complaints.

Not one angry, envious, or resentful look escapes his attention.

The Lord reasoned with Cain. If he approached God in the right way, he would be accepted.

Some understand God's words as another offer of mercy: if Cain did not do well, a sin offering lay at the door and he could take advantage of it.

The same word can mean both *sin* and *a sacrifice for sin*.

The meaning would then be: although you have not done well, do not despair; the remedy is close at hand.

Christ, the great Sin Offering, is said to stand at the door (Revelation 3:20).

Those who refuse to come to the door and seek the benefit of that offering deserve to perish in their sins.

God's acceptance of Abel did not take away Cain's birthright or give it to Abel.

Why, then, was Cain so angry?

When we examine the cause of our sinful anger fairly and carefully, much of its heat and agitation disappears.

## Cain murders Abel and receives his sentence (verses 8–15)

Malice in the heart can end in murder by the hands.

Cain killed Abel, his own brother, born of the same mother.

Abel was a brother Cain should have loved, a younger brother he should have protected, and a good brother who had never wronged him.

What terrible consequences followed our first parents' sin! How deeply this must have grieved them.

Notice Cain's pride, unbelief, and refusal to repent.

He denied what he had done, as if he could hide his crime from God.

He tried to cover a deliberate murder with a deliberate lie.

Murder is a sin that cries out for judgment. Blood calls for blood—the blood of the murdered person calls for the blood of the murderer.

Who can measure the reach or weight of God's curse? How far does it spread? How deeply does it strike?

Only in Christ are believers rescued from that curse and made heirs of blessing.

Cain was cursed from the earth. His punishment came in the very place where he had chosen to set his heart and find his happiness.

Every created thing becomes for us whatever God makes it: a comfort or a burden, a blessing or a curse.

The wicked person's sin brings a curse on what he does and on what he possesses.

Cain complained about his punishment, not his sin.

A heart is terribly hardened when it cares more about suffering than about wrongdoing.

God has wise and holy reasons for allowing even very wicked people to live longer.

It is useless to speculate about what mark God put on Cain.

The mark was certainly recognizable, both as a sign of Cain's disgrace and as God's warning that others must not kill him.

Though Abel died, he still speaks.

He tells us how dreadful murder is. He warns us to suppress anger at its earliest stirrings and teaches that the righteous must expect persecution.

He also points us toward life beyond death and an eternal reward enjoyed through faith in Christ and his atoning sacrifice.

He shows us the excellence of faith in the atoning sacrifice and blood of the Lamb of God.

Cain killed Abel because Cain's deeds were evil and his brother's were righteous (1 John 3:12).

The hostility God announced between the woman's Seed and the serpent's seed had now broken out into open conflict. That struggle has continued ever since.

We are all involved in it. No one can remain neutral.

Our Captain has declared, “Whoever is not with me is against me.”

Let us stand firmly, but with gentleness, for truth and righteousness against Satan.

## Cain leaves God's presence (verses 16–18)

Cain cast off all fear of God and no longer attended to his ordinances.

People who pretend to be religious while playing games with God are justly left to themselves until they fall into openly scandalous wrongdoing.

Then they abandon the outward appearance of godliness which their conduct had disgraced, and whose power they had always denied.

Cain went out from the Lord's presence. We never read that he returned to it and found comfort there.

The region where he lived was called *Nod*. The name can suggest “shaking” or “trembling,” reflecting his restless and unsettled spirit. It can also suggest a land of wandering.

Those who leave God cannot find true rest anywhere else.

People on earth who looked forward to the heavenly city were content to live in tents.

Cain, by contrast, gave no thought to that city and built one here on earth.

So those who remain under God's curse seek their permanent home and satisfaction in this world.

## Lamech and the abilities of Cain's descendants (verses 19–24)

The first person recorded as breaking the law of marriage belonged to Cain's wicked family.

Until then, a man had only one wife at a time. Lamech took two.

Worldly people set their hearts on worldly things. They are often very skilled and hardworking in pursuing them.

That was true of Cain's descendants.

Among them was a father of shepherds and a father of musicians—but not a father of the faithful.

There was someone who taught the working of bronze and iron, but no one who taught the good knowledge of the Lord.

They learned how to become rich, powerful, and entertained, but we hear nothing of reverence for God or service to him.

For most people, present things fill their minds.

Lamech had enemies whom he had provoked.

He compared himself with Cain, his ancestor, and flattered himself that his own guilt was much less serious.

He seems to have turned God's patience in sparing Cain into an excuse for expecting to sin without punishment.

## Seth and the renewed worship of God (verses 25–26)

The birth of another son comforted Adam and Eve in their sorrow.

They named him *Seth*, meaning “set,” “settled,” or “placed.”

Through his descendants humanity would continue to the end of time, and from his line the Messiah would come.

Cain, the leader of the departure from God, became a wanderer.

Seth, from whom the true church would come, was established in one place.

Only in Christ and his church can we find a truly lasting home.

Seth followed the example of his murdered brother Abel.

He shared the same precious faith in the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ, and became another witness to the grace and power of the Holy Spirit.

God allowed Adam and Eve to see true religion revived within their family.

Those who worshipped God began to do more openly in their faith.

Some publicly professed the true religion, taking a stand against the wickedness of the world around them.

The worse others become, the better and more zealous we should seek to be.

Here began the visible distinction between those who profess God's truth and those who live without reverence for him.

That distinction has continued ever since and will remain as long as the world lasts.
