# Judges 11 — Modern English

## Jephthah and the elders of Gilead (verses 1–11)

People should not be blamed for their parentage when their own conduct and character remove any disgrace associated with their birth.

God had forgiven Israel, and Jephthah was therefore willing to forgive the people who had rejected him.

Jephthah did not presume that victory was certain.

He knew God could justly allow the Ammonites to succeed as a further punishment for Israel's sins.

Nor did Jephthah place confidence in himself.

If he succeeded, the Lord would have given the enemy into his hands.

In saying so, he reminded the people to look to God as the Giver of victory.

Henry applies the conversation to those who desire salvation through Christ.

If Christ saves you, will you accept him as your **Ruler**?

There is no salvation on terms that exclude his authority.

If he makes you happy, will you also let him make you holy?

If he becomes your Helper, will you acknowledge him as your Head?

Jephthah was willing to risk his life for a little earthly honor.

How could Christians turn away from spiritual conflict merely because it is difficult, when Christ promises the **crown of life** to the one who overcomes?

## Jephthah seeks peace with Ammon (verses 12–28)

One way to honor God is to use our possessions rightly.

We should **receive them from him, use them for him and surrender them when he calls for them**.

Jephthah's message shows that he knew the books of Moses well.

His argument was clear, and his demand was reasonable.

People whose faith gives them the greatest courage are often also the most willing to pursue peace and make the first efforts to secure it.

But greed and ambition may disguise their purposes under claims of justice, making sincere attempts at peace ineffective.

## Jephthah's victory and difficult vow (verses 29–40)

Henry draws **five lessons** from Jephthah's vow.

**First**, traces of distrust and uncertainty may remain even in the hearts of genuine and remarkable believers.

**Second**, our vows should express gratitude to God, not attempt to purchase the favor we desire.

**Third**, we must consider vows carefully before making them, lest they trap us.

**Fourth**, what we have solemnly promised to God should be done when it is **possible and lawful**, even if fulfilling it is painful or difficult.

**Fifth**, children should submit willingly and cheerfully to their parents in the Lord.

What exactly happened when Jephthah fulfilled his vow is difficult to determine.

Henry says it is thought that **Jephthah did not offer his daughter as a burnt offering**, because such a sacrifice would have been abhorrent to the Lord.

The proposed alternative is that she remained unmarried and lived apart from her family.

But he does **not** present this explanation as certain.

Learned interpreters disagree over this and several other difficult passages.

We do not need to be overwhelmed by questions whose answers are uncertain.

Thanks be to God, what is necessary for our salvation is plain.

Those who remember Christ's promise about the teaching of the Holy Spirit and submit to that heavenly Teacher will receive the truth they need to understand.
