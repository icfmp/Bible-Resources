# Genesis 43 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Jacob is persuaded to send Benjamin into Egypt. | 1–14 |
| Joseph's reception of his brethren, their fears. | 15–25 |
| Joseph makes a feast for his brethren. | 26–34 |

## Jacob agrees to send Benjamin (verses 1–14)

Jacob urged his sons to go and buy a little more food. In a time of shortage, even a little must be enough.

Judah insisted that Benjamin must go with them.

Children do not dishonor their parents or neglect their duty when they humbly offer advice and, when necessary, reason with them.

Jacob saw that there was no alternative and agreed.

His prudence and fairness appeared in three decisions.

First, he sent back the money found in their sacks.

Honesty requires us to return not only what we obtained through our own fault but also what reached us through someone else's mistake.

Even when something comes to us by an oversight, keeping it after discovering the error is dishonest.

Second, Jacob sent twice as much money as before. Grain prices might have risen, or they might have to pay a ransom for Simeon.

Third, he sent a gift of products available in Canaan but scarce in Egypt, including balm and honey.

Providence does not distribute the same gifts everywhere.

Yet honey and spices cannot replace grain for bread.

Canaan was suffering from severe famine, but it still had balm, myrrh, and similar goods.

We can live adequately on plain food without delicacies, but we cannot live on delicacies without ordinary food.

Let us thank God that what we most need and find most useful is generally the most common and affordable.

People prize gold, silver, and the luxury products of each country.

But in a famine they readily exchange them for bread.

How little use will earthly treasures be on the day of wrath!

How readily we should count them all as loss for the surpassing worth of knowing Jesus Christ.

If we wish to prevail with people, we should first prevail with God in earnest prayer.

Yet every request for the blessings of this life, and every request to be spared its sufferings, should end with “Your will be done.”

## Joseph receives his brothers; they are afraid (verses 15–25)

Jacob's sons returned to Egypt for a second time to buy grain.

If we ever experience a famine of God's word, we should not complain about having to travel as far for spiritual food as these men did for physical food.

Joseph had instructed his steward to bring them into his house.

Even this frightened them. Those who are burdened by guilt tend to interpret everything in the worst possible light.

But the steward reassured them.

His words suggest that Joseph, a good master, had introduced him to the knowledge of the true God, the God of the Hebrews.

Faithful servants should take suitable opportunities to speak seriously and reverently about God and his providence.

## Joseph prepares a feast for his brothers (verses 26–34)

Notice the deep respect Joseph's brothers showed him. In this way his dreams were increasingly fulfilled.

Joseph treated them with great kindness and generosity.

But notice also how early the distinction between Jews and Gentiles appears in this account.

In a famine, receiving enough food would have been a great blessing. Instead, they were given a feast.

Their worries and fears were now gone. They ate joyfully, believing that they were at peace with the ruler of the land.

If God accepts our work and what we bring before him, we have reason to rejoice.

Joseph showed special favor to Benjamin, testing whether his brothers would envy him.

We should be content with what we have and not resent what others possess.

Likewise, Jesus increasingly shows those he loves how much they need him.

He teaches them that he is their only refuge from destruction, overcomes their reluctance, and brings them to himself.

Then, when he sees fit, he lets them taste his love and welcomes them to the provision of his house as a foretaste of the greater blessings he intends for them.
