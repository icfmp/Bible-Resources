# Job 3 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Job complains that he was born. | 1–10 |
| Job complaining. | 11–19 |
| He complains of his life. | 20–26 |

## Job laments his birth (verses 1–10)

Job's friends had sat beside him for **seven days** without offering comfort.

Meanwhile Satan attacked Job's mind, attempting to undermine his confidence and fill him with harsh thoughts about God.

The divine permission seems to have extended to such inward attacks as well as to physical pain.

Job was in a special way a figure of **Christ**.

Our Savior's inward suffering, in Gethsemane and upon the cross, was especially dreadful. Henry relates much of it to Satan's attacks in that hour of darkness.

These inward trials help explain why Job's response changed from complete submission to the impatience heard here and later in the book.

Believers who have tasted the bitterness of inward spiritual trials know they can be more dreadful than even severe outward trouble, especially in comparison with the sweetness of enjoying God's love and presence.

We should therefore not be astonished that Job shared ordinary human weaknesses.

Yet we should rejoice that Satan failed to prove him a hypocrite.

**Job cursed the day of his birth, but he did not curse his God**.

Henry believes Job afterward regretted these wishes, and supposes he would judge them even more clearly now in everlasting happiness.

## Job asks why he did not die at birth (verses 11–19)

Job complained about the tender care he had received from those present at his birth.

No creature enters the world more helpless than a human baby.

God's power and Providence sustained our fragile lives.

His mercy and patience spared lives already forfeited through sin.

God himself placed natural affection for children in parents' hearts.

There is an important difference between **desiring to depart and be with Christ, free from sin** and **desiring death simply as an escape from life's suffering**.

The first desire can grow from grace; the second betrays remaining sin.

Wisdom and duty require us to make the best of our present condition, whether living or dying.

We should live for the Lord and die for the Lord, belonging to him in either case (**Romans 14:8**).

Job described the grave as a place where the wicked no longer trouble anyone and the weary rest.

When persecutors die, they can no longer persecute.

Those who are exhausted cease from their labors.

But an even better rest—free from **sin, temptation, conflict, grief and labor**—awaits believers in the enjoyment of God's presence.

There they rest in Jesus.

Even now, insofar as we trust and obey Christ, we find rest for our souls amid the troubles of this world.

## Job finds life unbearable (verses 20–26)

Job felt like someone who had lost the way and could see no hope of escape or better days.

Yet this was a poor state of mind in which to approach death: **he was unwilling to go on living**.

Our constant concern should be to prepare for the life to come and leave to God the timing of our departure.

Grace teaches us to be willing to die even amid life's greatest comforts, and willing to live even amid its greatest sorrows.

Job's path was hidden from him. He did not understand why God was contending with him.

A suffering and tempted believer may know something of that heaviness.

When we have concentrated too much on things we can see, a heavenly Father's discipline may expose the misery of life apart from renewed assurance of his presence.

Only God can restore **the joy of salvation**.

Blessed be God: though the earth contains much human wickedness, it is also filled with his goodness.

We can live through hardship by attending to the duties he gives us.

Those willing to receive **Christ as Savior** may look ahead to everlasting mercy.
