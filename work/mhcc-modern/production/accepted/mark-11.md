# Mark 11 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Christ's triumphant entry into Jerusalem. | 1–11 |
| The barren fig-tree cursed, The temple cleansed. | 12–18 |
| Prayer in faith. | 19–26 |
| The priests and elders questioned concerning John the Baptist. | 27–33 |

## Jesus enters Jerusalem (verses 1–11)

Jesus' remarkable entrance into the city showed that **he did not fear the strength or malice of his enemies**.

The disciples were frightened, so this would encourage them.

Christ was not overwhelmed by the suffering he knew awaited him.

Yet everything about the entry displayed his **humility**.

We should avoid seeking personal grandeur and willingly associate with people of modest circumstances.

How inappropriate for Christians to demand splendor when Christ deliberately rejected it!

The crowd welcomed him as **the One who was to come**, long promised and expected.

He came in the Lord's name.

Let us give him our best affections.

He is the blessed Savior who brings blessing, and the Father who sent him deserves our praise.

Glory to God in the highest heavens, blessed forever!

## The fruitless fig tree and the temple (verses 12–18)

Jesus examined the fig tree seeking fruit.

The ordinary time for figs had not yet fully come, though it was approaching.

He found none.

The tree was made a warning **not to other trees but to the people of that generation**.

Henry sees it as a figure of judgment on the **Jewish religious community of Jesus' day**, in which Christ sought fruit but found none.

That is his historical interpretation rather than an assertion about every Jewish person.

Jesus then entered the temple and corrected the abuses taking place in its courts.

It showed that when the Redeemer came to Zion he would **turn ungodliness away from Jacob**.

The chief priests and scribes responded by looking for a way to kill Jesus rather than seeking reconciliation with him.

They must have feared that they were, in effect, **fighting against God**.

## Prayer, faith and forgiveness (verses 19–26)

The disciples were astonished that the fig tree had withered so quickly.

Henry applies it again to people who reject Christ and to his historical reading of the Jewish church's condition.

We should never be satisfied with religion that does **not produce good works**.

Jesus taught his disciples to pray with faith.

Genuine Christian faith works great **spiritual miracles**.

It justifies us and thereby removes **mountains of guilt**, never to rise again in judgment against us.

It purifies the heart and removes **mountains of sinful corruption**, clearing the way for God's grace to transform our lives.

One principal reason to approach the throne of grace is to seek **forgiveness of our sins**.

That concern should occupy us every day.

## Jesus challenges the religious authorities (verses 27–33)

Jesus showed the close relationship between **his own teaching and baptism** and **John the Baptist's** mission.

Both pointed toward establishing the gospel kingdom.

The elders who questioned Jesus were not sincerely seeking truth.

They wanted to **win an argument**.

He therefore did not owe them the explanation they pretended to desire.

Even without his direct reply, his works clearly demonstrated divine authority.

No human being could have performed those miracles unless **God was with him**.
