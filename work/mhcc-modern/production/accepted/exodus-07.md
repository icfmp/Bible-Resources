# Exodus 7 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Moses and Aaron encouraged. | 1–7 |
| The rods turned into serpents, Pharaoh's heart is hardened. | 8–13 |
| The river is turned into blood, The distress of the Egyptians. | 14–25 |

## Moses and Aaron receive courage (verses 1–7)

God glorifies himself by making people know that he is Jehovah.

Israel would learn this through the fulfillment of his promises.

The Egyptians would learn it when his judgments were poured out upon them.

Moses came as Jehovah's ambassador, speaking in God's name.

He gave Pharaoh commands, announced judgments against him, and called those judgments down upon him.

Pharaoh, however proud and powerful he was, could not resist.

Moses no longer stood trembling before Pharaoh. Instead, Pharaoh trembled before him.

This seems to be what God meant when he said Moses would be “a god to Pharaoh.”

At last Moses was freed from his fears.

He raised no more objections but, strengthened in faith, began his task courageously and continued it faithfully.

## The signs and Pharaoh's hardened heart (verses 8–13)

When people dislike something because it stands against their pride and sinful desires, they refuse to be convinced by it.

Yet they are easily persuaded to believe whatever they want to be true.

God always sends his word with sufficient evidence of its divine authority.

But when people are determined to disobey and eager to find objections, he may allow them to be caught by a trap they have chosen.

Henry describes Pharaoh's magicians as deceivers.

They attempted to imitate Moses' real miracles by hidden tricks and sleight of hand.

For a time they succeeded enough to mislead the people watching.

But eventually they had to admit that they could no longer reproduce what God's power was doing.

Few people contribute more to the ruin of sinners than those who resist the truth by entertaining others with a counterfeit imitation of it.

Satan is most dangerous when he disguises himself as an angel of light.

## The waters of Egypt turn to blood (verses 14–25)

The first of the ten plagues turned water into blood.

What a dreadful judgment it was!

The sight of vast flowing streams of blood must have filled people with horror.

Nothing is more ordinary than water.

God has wisely and kindly arranged that something so necessary and useful for human life should usually be inexpensive and widely available.

But now the Egyptians had to drink blood or die of thirst.

Egypt had been a pleasant land.

The dead fish and blood made it a miserable place.

This plague was justly sent upon the Egyptians.

The Nile was treated as their god.

When we idolize some created thing, God may rightly take it away from us or make it bitter to us.

The Egyptians had stained the river with the blood of Hebrew infants.

Now God made that same river blood.

Those who thirst for blood will sooner or later receive more of it than they desire.

The plague also carried a warning.

Egypt depended heavily on its river (Zechariah 14:18).

By striking the Nile, God warned that the produce of the whole land could be destroyed.

Christ's love turns his disciples' ordinary blessings into spiritual benefits.

But God's anger turns the things his enemies most prize into misery and a curse.

Aaron was to bring the plague by striking the river with his staff.

He did so in front of Pharaoh and his attendants.

God's genuine miracles were not performed like Satan's lying wonders. Truth has no need to hide in corners.

Consider God's almighty power.

Every created thing becomes for us what he makes it—water or blood.

And see how quickly the conditions of worldly life may change.

What is always fleeting may soon become painful as well.

Notice the harm sin does.

If what once comforted us becomes a burden, we must often recognize our own responsibility.

Sin turns our water into blood.

The plague lasted seven days.

Yet throughout that time Pharaoh's proud heart would not allow him to ask Moses to pray for its removal.

In this way hypocritical hearts store up more and more judgment.

It is no wonder that God's anger had not passed away, but his hand was still raised against Egypt.

## Editorial verification — Exodus 7

The three original units were compared in a separate pass against [the complete CCEL Concise reading of Exodus 7](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc.iii.vii.html). The source of record remains exact published `MHCC 0.1.1`; its full SWORD native/XML links are not yet audited here.

- **1–7:** God's glory in both promises to Israel and judgments upon Egypt, Moses as ambassador commanding and warning, Pharaoh's inability to resist, “a god to Pharaoh” explained as representation of divine authority, and Moses' growing courage — retained.
- **8–13:** willful disbelief driven by pride/lust, easy acceptance of desired falsehoods, divine evidence alongside permitted snares, **Henry's historical reading of the magicians as using deceptive tricks**, initial imitation followed by failure, counterfeit truth and Satan appearing as an angel of light — retained.
- **14–25:** first plague's horror, ordinary water as common mercy, Nile idol and infants' blood, reciprocal judgment, dependence on river and **Zechariah 14:18**, Christ blessing ordinary mercies contrasted with judgment on God's enemies, public sign rather than hidden trick, water/blood under divine power, sins turning comforts into burdens, seven-day duration and Pharaoh's stubborn refusal to seek prayer — retained.
