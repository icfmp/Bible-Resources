## The wickedness that provoked God's judgment (verses 1–7)

The most remarkable event in the history of the ancient world was its destruction by the flood.

Here we see how widespread wickedness had become, why God's anger was just, and why he resolved in holiness to punish that world.

Throughout history, God's particular displeasure has rested on marriages between those who profess true faith and those who openly oppose it.

The ungodly spouse's example corrupts the other or seriously weakens that person's faith.

Faith disappears from family life, and children are raised according to the worldly values of the parent who has no fear of God.

If we profess to be sons and daughters of the Lord Almighty, we must not marry without his approval.

He will not bless us when we choose beauty, cleverness, wealth, or worldly honors over faith and holiness.

God's Spirit had been contending with human beings.

He had sent Enoch and Noah, and perhaps others, to preach to them.

He had waited patiently, ready to show grace despite their rebellion. He had also stirred fear and conviction in their consciences.

But the Lord declared that his Spirit would not contend with humanity forever.

He would leave people to become hardened in sin and ready for destruction.

This was because humanity had become *flesh*: not merely weak and frail, but carnal and corrupt.

People had misused the noble powers of their souls to satisfy sinful desires.

God sees all the wickedness among human beings.

Nothing can be hidden from him now. And unless people repent, he will soon bring what they have done into the open.

A nation's wickedness is especially great when notorious sinners are celebrated as famous people.

Sin was being committed everywhere, by every kind of person.

Anyone could see that humanity's wickedness was great.

But God saw something deeper: every thought and intention of the human heart was continually evil.

Here lay the bitter root and the polluted spring.

The heart was deceitful and desperately wicked. Its principles were corrupt, its habits and desires evil.

People planned evil and deliberately worked out ways to cause harm.

No goodness remained among them.

God looked upon their wickedness as one who had himself been injured by it.

He saw it as a loving father sees the folly and stubborn rebellion of a disobedient child—a grief so deep that the father might wish he had never had that child.

Scripture speaks here in human terms.

It does not mean that God can change or become unhappy.

If God hates our sin so deeply, should we not grieve over it ourselves?

May we look upon the One we have grieved and mourn!

God regretted having made humanity. Yet Scripture never says that he regretted redeeming humanity.

When God resolved to destroy human beings, the original expression was especially striking: “I will wipe humanity from the earth.”

The image is of dirt being wiped from a place that should be clean and thrown onto a refuse heap.

Even as God pronounced judgment, he spoke of humanity as his own creation.

Those who fail to fulfill the purpose for which life was given forfeit their lives.

God made this decision only after his Spirit had contended with people for so long without success.

God's justice punishes only those who refuse to be reformed by his grace.

## Noah finds grace (verses 8–11)

Noah did not find favor with other people.

They hated and persecuted him, because his life and preaching condemned the world around him.

But he found grace in the Lord's eyes.

That made him far more honorable than the celebrated people of his age.

Let this be our deepest desire: to live in a way that is pleasing to God.

While the rest of the world lived wickedly, Noah kept his integrity.

It was God's favor toward Noah that produced this good life in him.

Noah was a righteous man, justified before God through faith in the promised Seed.

Through that faith he was made holy, received right principles, and lived uprightly among others.

He was not simply honest. He was devoted to God, continually seeking to do his will.

God looks with favor upon those who sincerely look up to him in faith.

It is easy to seem religious when religion is fashionable.

But it takes strong faith and determination to go against the current and stand for God when no one else will.

That is what Noah did.

Every kind of sin was found among the people. They corrupted God's worship.

Sin had filled the earth with violence, fully justifying God's decision to destroy the world.

The corruption spread everywhere.

When wickedness becomes widespread, ruin is not far away.

As long as a nation has a remnant of people who pray, their intercession may keep judgment back even as the nation's guilt continues to grow.

But when everyone is breaking down the barriers through sin, and no one stands in the breach to repair them, what can we expect but a flood of wrath?

## The coming flood and the building of the ark (verses 12–21)

God told Noah that he intended to destroy the wicked world with water.

“The Lord confides in those who fear him” (Psalm 25:14).

His secret is shared with all believers, enabling them to understand and apply the warnings and promises of his written word.

God chose a flood to drown the world.

Just as he chooses the rod with which to correct his children, he chooses the sword with which to destroy his enemies.

God established his covenant with Noah.

This is the first place in the Bible where the word *covenant* appears.

Here it seems to refer to two things.

First, **the covenant of providence**: the ordinary course of nature would continue to the end of time.

Second, **the covenant of grace**: God would be Noah's God and would take a people for himself from Noah's descendants.

God then directed Noah to build an ark.

It was something like the hull of a ship, built to float on the water.

The ark was enormous. Henry compares its size to half that of St. Paul's Cathedral and says it could hold more than eighteen of the largest ships of his day.

God could have kept Noah safe without making him work or endure any expense or difficulty.

Instead, he gave Noah the task of making the very means by which he would be preserved.

This tested Noah's faith and obedience.

Both God's providence and his grace recognize and reward obedience and diligence.

God gave Noah detailed instructions for the ark. Built according to those instructions, it was perfectly suited to its purpose.

God promised to preserve Noah and his family in it.

When we obey God, both we and our families are likely to benefit.

The godliness of parents benefits their children in this life and helps them toward eternal life, provided they make proper use of those blessings.

## Noah's faith and obedience (verse 22)

Noah's faith overcame every objection raised by unbelieving reasoning.

He had to build an enormous structure unlike anything he had ever seen, and provide food for all the living creatures it would shelter.

That demanded great care, labor, and expense.

His neighbors would surely laugh at him.

Yet by faith Noah overcame every objection. He obeyed promptly and firmly.

Once he began building, he did not stop until the ark was finished.

That is what he did, and what we must do.

He feared the coming flood, and so he prepared the ark.

Noah's warning also contains an even more solemn warning for us: we must flee from the wrath to come.

That judgment will sweep the unbelieving world into the pit of destruction.

Christ is the true Noah, the One who brings us comfort.

Through his sufferings he has already prepared the ark of salvation, and he graciously invites us to enter it by faith.

While the day of his patience continues, let us hear his voice and obey him.
