# Genesis 39 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |

| Joseph is falsely accused by his mistress. | 13–18 |

## God is with Joseph in Potiphar's house (verses 1–6)

Our enemies may take away our outward signs of honor, but they cannot take away wisdom or grace.

They can separate us from friends, family, and country, but they cannot take away the Lord's presence.

They can deprive us of earthly blessings, rob us of freedom, and lock us in prison, but they cannot shut us out from fellowship with God, from the throne of grace, or from the blessings of salvation.

Joseph was blessed—wonderfully blessed—even in the house where he served as a slave.

When God is present with us, he makes what we do prosper.

Godly people are a blessing wherever they live. Faithful servants can be a blessing even if their position is low and they are little respected.

Sometimes the prosperity of wicked people comes, in one way or another, for the sake of the godly among them.

Here a wicked household was blessed because one good servant lived in it.

## Joseph resists temptation (verses 7–12)

Physical beauty, in men or women, often becomes a trap both to the person who possesses it and to others.

We should therefore never become proud of beauty, and we must always watch carefully against the temptations that come with it.

We greatly need to make a covenant with our eyes, so that what we look at does not corrupt our hearts.

When lust gains control, people sacrifice decency, reputation, and conscience.

Potiphar's wife showed that her heart was entirely set on doing evil.

Satan had failed to overcome Joseph through the world's troubles and disapproval. Through them all, Joseph held firmly to his principles.

So Satan attacked him with pleasures—a temptation that has ruined more people than suffering has.

But God's grace enabled Joseph to resist and overcome. His escape was as striking a demonstration of divine power as the rescue of the three young men from the fiery furnace.

This particular sin could easily have entangled him.

The woman tempting him was his master's wife. Her favor might help him advance, while refusing her could turn her into a dangerous enemy.

The place and the time suited the temptation. And she urged him constantly and repeatedly.

Yet the almighty grace of God enabled Joseph to withstand this assault.

He remembered what he owed both to God and to his master.

Honor, justice, and gratitude all require us not to betray those who trust us, even if the betrayal could remain hidden.

Above all, Joseph would not offend his God.

He considered three things.

First, **who was being tempted?** He was a man in covenant with God, someone who openly professed faith and belonged to him.

Second, **what was he being tempted to do?** Others might call the sin a small matter, but Joseph refused to think of it that way.

Call sin by its proper name; never make it seem less serious than it is. Sins of this kind are great wickedness, exceedingly sinful.

Third, **against whom would the sin be committed?** Against God—against his nature, his rule, his love, and his purposes.

Those who love God therefore hate sin.

By God's grace, Joseph overcame the temptation by getting away from it. He did not remain to negotiate with it; he fled as though escaping for his life.

If we truly intend not to do evil, we must flee like a bird escaping a trap or a deer escaping a hunter.

## Joseph is falsely accused (verses 13–18)

Joseph's mistress failed to make him guilty, so she sought revenge instead.

Those who have broken the restraints of decency may also disregard the restraints of truth.

It is nothing new for the best of people to be falsely accused of the worst crimes by people who are themselves deeply guilty.

How good it is to know that a day of disclosure is coming, when everyone will appear as they truly are.

## God is with Joseph in prison (verses 19–23)

Joseph's master believed the accusation.

Henry considers it likely that Potiphar chose this prison because it was the worst available. But God intended it as the path toward Joseph's future honor.

God acknowledged Joseph and vindicated him.

Far from friends and relatives, he had no one to help or comfort him. Yet the Lord was with him and showed him mercy.

A person who has a clear conscience in prison has a good God there as well.

God caused the keeper of the prison to look favorably on Joseph and entrust him with responsibility for its affairs.

A good person will do good wherever he is, becoming a blessing even in imprisonment or exile.

But as we look at Joseph, let us not forget Jesus.

He suffered temptation without sin. He was slandered, persecuted, and imprisoned without cause; yet through the cross he ascended to the throne.

May God enable us to follow him in submission and suffering, and finally to the same place of glory.

## Editorial verification — Genesis 39

The four original exposition units were compared in a distinct post-drafting pass against the [CCEL complete-text reading of the Concise Commentary, Genesis 39](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc.ii.xxxix.html). The authoritative base remains the pinned published `MHCC 0.1.1`, whose exact SWORD-native XML has not yet been independently collated to this manuscript.

- **1–6:** the fourfold contrast between removable external blessings and irremovable wisdom/grace, presence, divine communion and salvation; Joseph's prosperity as a slave, blessing through a humble godly servant, and wicked prosperity for a believer's sake — retained.
- **7–12:** beauty as danger to both sexes, the covenant with the eyes, lust's assault on conscience, Satan's failed trials and subsequent temptations of pleasure, rescue compared to **the fiery furnace**, the woman's powerful position and constant pressure, duty to both master and God, **all three reasons** Joseph gives himself (his covenant identity, the seriousness of the sin, and its offense against God), flight rather than bargaining, bird/deer imagery — retained.
- **13–18:** failed seduction followed by revenge, disregard for both decency and truth, unjust accusations against the innocent and the coming day of discovery — retained.
- **19–23:** Potiphar's belief, Henry's **qualified** conjecture about the prison, God's distinct purpose, comfort of his presence, the prison keeper's favor, goodness in exile or chains, and the full Christological conclusion from innocent suffering through the cross to the throne — retained.

All four original section boundaries and headings are preserved. The text's printed “avoiding the temper” at the end of 7–12 is not reproduced as a nonsensical modern phrase: the following explicit “not stay to parley ... fled” establishes that Henry is describing escaping temptation.
