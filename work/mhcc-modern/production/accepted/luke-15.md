# Luke 15 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Parables of the lost sheep, and the piece of silver. | 1–10 |
| The prodigal son, his wickedness and distress. | 11–16 |
| His repentance and pardon. | 17–24 |
| The elder brother offended. | 25–32 |

## The lost sheep and lost coin (verses 1–10)

The lost sheep illustrates the great work of **human redemption**.

The sinner has wandered away from God and is exposed to certain destruction unless brought home.

Yet the sinner does not naturally want to return.

Christ earnestly seeks and brings lost people back.

In the second parable, the missing coin was just **one piece of comparatively small value**.

Yet its owner searched carefully until she found it.

These pictures show the different ways God works to bring lost souls back to himself and **the Savior's joy when they return**.

Let us make sure our own repentance is real and leads to salvation.

## The younger son's sin and misery (verses 11–16)

The parable of the prodigal son teaches **what repentance is** and how ready the Lord is to welcome and bless those who return.

It displays the riches of gospel grace and has helped countless sinners find encouragement to repent.

It is already wrong, and the beginning of still worse evil, when people regard **God's gifts as debts owed to them**.

Sinners are foolish to content themselves with receiving their good things only during this life.

Our first parents tried to become independent of God, destroying themselves and their descendants.

The same desire for independence lies beneath persistent sin.

We can recognize aspects of ourselves in the prodigal son.

A sinful condition means **distance from God**.

It means **squandering** thoughts, mental powers, time and opportunities.

It is a condition of **spiritual poverty**, lacking necessary food and clothing for the soul or provision for the world to come.

It is a condition of **degrading bondage**: serving sinful desires is like being reduced to feeding pigs.

It brings continual dissatisfaction.

Earthly wealth and bodily pleasures cannot even permanently satisfy our bodies, much less the soul.

The world and flesh cannot supply what a soul needs; they offer what poisons it instead of nourishing it.

A sinful condition is **spiritual death**, because the sinner lacks the life of God.

It is also a **lost condition**.

Unless God's mercy intervenes, those separated from him will perish eternally.

The prodigal son's misery is only a faint picture of the ruin brought by human sin.

Yet how few recognize their true condition!

## The younger son's repentance and welcome (verses 17–24)

The prodigal's recovery began when he **came to himself**.

This is a turning point in conversion.

The Lord opens the sinner's eyes and convinces the heart of sin.

The person now sees everything—including self and God—in a different light.

The sinner recognizes that even the humblest servant of God is happier than a person still in rebellion.

It is helpful to think of God as **a Father and our Father** when returning to him.

The prodigal did not merely decide to return; he rose and continued until he reached home.

In the same way, the penitent sinner resolutely leaves slavery to Satan and sinful desires and returns to God **through prayer**, overcoming fear and discouragement.

The Lord meets such a person with unexpected signs of forgiving love.

The humble sinner is clothed in **the Redeemer's righteousness**.

Through **the Spirit of adoption**, peace of conscience and the grace of the gospel, the believer receives strength to walk in holiness.

The Savior welcomes such people with divine consolation, and grace forms within them the desire and strength **both to will and to do** what is right.

## The elder brother resents mercy (verses 25–32)

The elder brother illustrates the spirit of **the Pharisees**, though Henry says the warning goes beyond them.

It also exposes the proud response that may arise when people see God's mercy extended to others.

Henry compares this resentment with the attitude of many Jews toward Gentiles who had turned to Christ.

People in every age can object to the gospel and its messengers in the same way.

How terrible to hate or despise people **for whom Christ shed his precious blood**, whom the Father loves and in whom the Holy Spirit dwells!

Such an attitude springs from pride, excessive confidence in one's own righteousness and ignorance of one's heart.

The mercy and grace of God shine not only when he receives a repenting prodigal.

They also appear in his **gentle patience toward irritable and resentful saints**.

Those children of God who remain near their Father's house enjoy an incomparable blessing: **they are with him and will continue with him**.

Happy are those who receive Christ's invitation with gratitude.
