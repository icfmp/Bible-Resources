# Isaiah 13 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The armies of God's wrath. | 1–5 |
| The conquest of Babylon. | 6–18 |
| Its final desolation. | 19–22 |

## The appointed armies of judgment (verses 1–5)

Warnings from God's word become a heavy burden to those determined to remain wicked.

The forces gathered against Babylon are called **God's consecrated or appointed ones** because they are designated for this particular work and given the ability to accomplish it.

They are also called **his mighty ones**, for even their strength comes from him.

They would arrive from distant lands.

God can bring judgment through people who seem so far away that no one imagines they might ever become dangerous.

## Babylon's defeat by the Medes and Persians (verses 6–18)

Isaiah describes Babylon's terrible overthrow by **the Medes and Persians**.

People who were arrogant, cruel and frightening during prosperity can become overwhelmed by terror when calamity arrives.

Every confidence and comfort would fail.

The sun and stars are described as losing their light.

Such language often appears in the prophets to picture **the upheaval of governments and kingdoms**.

God would confront Babylon's wrongdoing, especially **pride**, which brings people low.

The destruction is pictured as a general scene of horror.

Those who join themselves to Babylon should expect to share her judgments (**Revelation 18:4**).

People are willing to surrender all their possessions to preserve life, yet their wealth cannot ransom them from death.

The cruelty described here should make us grieve and wonder how deeply **human nature has become corrupted**.

Henry also addresses the suffering of infants. He connects the reality of death from life's very beginning with his doctrine of **humanity's inherited guilt through the fall**.

That is **his theological explanation** of the tragedy, not an endorsement of brutality.

The final day of the Lord will be more terrible still than the judgments described in this chapter.

The unrepentant will find no refuge.

Yet few people behave as though they believe these warnings.

## Babylon's complete desolation (verses 19–22)

Babylon had been a magnificent and powerful city.

Yet Isaiah announced its total destruction, leaving it uninhabited and occupied by wild animals.

Henry sees the fulfillment of this prediction as evidence of Scripture's truth.

He also understands Babylon's ruin as a picture of the coming destruction of **New Testament Babylon**, and as a warning to all sinners to flee God's wrath.

For believers, it is encouragement to expect eventual victory over the enemies of their souls and of Christ's church.

Everything in the world can change and decay.

Let us therefore seek **the kingdom that cannot be shaken** and hold firmly to the grace through which we may serve God acceptably, with reverence and holy fear.
