# Judges 1 — Modern English

## Judah and Simeon begin the conquest (verses 1–8)

The Israelites knew that the war against the Canaanites had to continue after Joshua's death. What they did not know was how best to proceed.

They therefore inquired of the Lord.

God assigns work according to the strength he has given. Those who are most able should be prepared to do the most.

Judah held the first place in dignity and therefore should also stand first in duty.

Judah's effort would achieve nothing without God's blessing. Yet God would not give success while Judah refused to act.

Judah was the largest and most important tribe, while Simeon was the smallest.

Even so, Judah asked for Simeon's friendship and help.

Israelites should help one another against the Canaanites. Likewise, Christians from different groups should support and strengthen one another.

People who help each other in love have reason to hope that God will graciously assist them both.

Adoni-bezek was captured.

He had been a cruel tyrant.

The Israelites, Henry believes, acted under God's direction in making him suffer the kind of cruelty he had inflicted on others.

His own conscience acknowledged the justice of what happened to him.

God's righteousness is sometimes displayed when a sinner's punishment corresponds to the sin committed.

## Hebron and other cities are taken (verses 9–20)

The Canaanites had chariots made with iron, but Israel had the Lord on its side.

His chariots are thousands of angels (Psalm 68:17).

Nevertheless, the Israelites allowed fear to overcome faith.

Caleb's actions have already been described in Joshua 15:16–19.

The Kenites also settled in the land.

Israel allowed them to live where they wished because they were a peaceful and contented people.

They troubled no one, and no one troubled them.

Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth.

## Other tribes leave enemies unconquered (verses 21–36)

Israel grew careless in both duty and its own long-term interests.

Out of laziness and fear, the people refused to finish conquering the land.

Greed contributed as well: they hoped to profit from allowing Canaanites to remain among them.

They did not hate or fear idolatry as they should have done.

The same unbelief that had kept their ancestors wandering for forty years now prevented them from possessing Canaan fully.

Distrusting God's power and promises deprived them of blessings and brought new troubles.

The same can happen to believers who begin their Christian lives well.

Their spiritual graces become weak, sinful desires revive, Satan attacks them through temptations suited to their character, and the world regains its influence.

Their consciences become burdened with guilt, their hearts with sorrow, their reputations with disgrace, and the gospel itself suffers dishonor.

A believer may be corrected severely and eventually restored without being lost.

Even so, the person may mourn those foolish decisions throughout life and, when approaching death, regret opportunities lost to glorify God and serve the church.

Fellowship with God's enemies, whether around us or in our own hearts, can only harm us.

Our wisdom is to continue the struggle against them without making peace with sin.
