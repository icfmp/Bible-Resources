# Genesis 9 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God blesses Noah and grants animals for food. | 1–3 |
| Blood and murder are forbidden. | 4–7 |
| God's covenant is marked by the rainbow. | 8–17 |
| Noah plants a vineyard, becomes drunk, and is mocked by Ham. | 18–23 |
| Noah speaks concerning Canaan, Shem, and Japheth; his death. | 24–29 |

## God's blessing and the gift of food (verses 1–3)

God's blessing is the reason things go well for us. We depend on him, and we should thank him.

Let us not forget how much we benefit from the work animals do, or from their flesh as food.

Nor should we be any less thankful for the safety we enjoy from savage and dangerous animals, because God has placed a fear of human beings deep within them.

Every day, on every side, we see this promise fulfilled.

God's permission to eat animals fully justifies their proper use. But it does not justify gluttony, and still less does it justify cruelty.

We must never cause animals needless pain, either while they live or when we take their lives.

## The prohibition of bloodshed (verses 4–7)

The main reason for forbidding the eating of blood was surely to remind worshippers of the great atonement represented by the shedding of blood in sacrifices.

But the command also seems intended to restrain cruelty.

If people became accustomed to shedding and consuming the blood of animals, they might grow insensitive to their suffering and less horrified by the thought of shedding human blood.

No one has the right to take their own life. Our lives belong to God, and we must surrender them only when he decides.

If we do anything to hasten our own death, we are accountable to him.

When God demands an account of a life unjustly taken, the murderer cannot give that life back and must answer with his own.

Sooner or later, in this world or the next, God will expose murders and punish those that are beyond human justice.

Yet God has appointed authorities to protect the innocent and restrain wrongdoers. They must not carry the sword in vain (Romans 13:4).

Henry insists that deliberate murder should be punished by death.

The reason given for the seriousness of murder is that even fallen humanity still bears traces of God's image. Unjustly killing a human being therefore defaces that image and dishonors God.

## God's covenant and the rainbow (verses 8–17)

The old world was destroyed as a testimony to divine justice. The continued existence of this world is a testimony to divine mercy.

Yet the sin that drowned the old world will one day bring this one to destruction by fire.

People seal agreements to make their promises solemn and give one another assurance that they will be kept.

God appointed the rainbow as the seal of his covenant.

It seems likely that rainbows had appeared in clouds before the flood. But they had never been a sign of this covenant until God made them so.

A rainbow appears when we have the greatest reason to fear that the rain will overwhelm us.

At that very moment, God displays the sign of his promise that it will not prevail.

The darker the cloud, the brighter the bow shining within it.

In the same way, when threatening troubles multiply, God's encouragements can abound even more.

A rainbow is formed when sunlight shines upon or through drops of rain.

Likewise, all the glory belonging to the seals of God's covenant comes from Christ, the Sun of righteousness. He can shine with glory even upon the tears of his people.

An ordinary bow can inspire fear. But this bow has neither string nor arrow.

It cannot hurt us. It points upward rather than toward the earth.

God meant the signs of his covenant to comfort us, not terrify us.

As God looks upon the rainbow and remembers his covenant, we should look upon it with faith and thankfulness and remember that same covenant.

Without God's revelation, we could never know this gracious assurance. Without faith, that assurance would bring us no benefit.

The same is true of the greater dangers facing humanity and the blessings promised under the new covenant.

## Noah's drunkenness and his sons' conduct (verses 18–23)

Scripture records Noah's drunkenness with a fairness and honesty found only in the Bible.

It shows the weakness and imperfection of even a good man—though he may have fallen into this sin unexpectedly.

The best people cannot stand upright unless they depend on God's grace and are upheld by it.

Ham appears to have been a bad man. He probably took pleasure in seeing his father in such a shameful condition.

Noah had been described as blameless among the people of his generation (Genesis 6:9). That meant sincere faithfulness, not sinless perfection.

He had remained sober while surrounded by drunken people. Now he became drunk in a setting where the people around him were sober.

Whoever thinks he stands should be careful not to fall.

When we enjoy God's good gifts in abundance, we must be particularly careful not to misuse them through excess (Luke 21:34).

Noah's sin brought shame. Drunkenness is a serious evil.

It exposes people's weaknesses and reveals secrets they would otherwise keep hidden. A drunken gatekeeper leaves the gates open.

It also disgraces people and exposes them to contempt. Those who are drunk say and do things they would be ashamed even to think of when sober.

Notice the care Shem and Japheth took to cover their father's shame.

Love should cover the faults of others (1 Peter 4:8).

And parents and others deserving our respect should be treated with an additional covering of reverence.

God's blessing rests upon those who honor their parents, while his curse especially falls upon those who dishonor them.

## Noah speaks about Canaan, Shem, and Japheth (verses 24–29)

Noah pronounced a curse upon Canaan, Ham's son. Perhaps Canaan had been more guilty than the others.

He was to become a servant of servants—the lowest and most despised of servants—even to his brothers.

Henry connects this prophecy with Israel's later victories over the Canaanites, when they were killed in battle or compelled to pay tribute.

He also describes Africa as having been populated largely by Ham's descendants and interprets the continent's history through that framework.

He points to periods of Roman, Saracen, and Turkish domination, and describes the ignorance, violence, enslavement, and misery he believed were widespread in parts of Africa during his own period.

He grieves that so many Black Africans were bought and sold like animals in the marketplace, transported far from their homes, and forced to perform exhausting labor.

But he expressly refuses to use these events as an excuse for those who profit from such cruelty.

God has never commanded people to enslave Black Africans. Henry declares that God will certainly punish those who commit such terrible wrongs.

Henry regarded the course of history as a fulfillment of Noah's prophecy. He argued that it showed Noah was not simply speaking out of personal anger, but that the Holy Spirit had used Ham's offense to reveal God's hidden purposes.

“Blessed be the LORD, the God of Shem.”

Henry understands the church as being preserved among Shem's descendants. From them came the Jewish people, who for a long time were the only people publicly professing faith in the true God.

Christ, who is the Lord God, would also descend from Shem in his human nature.

Noah blessed Japheth too, and with him the nations descended from his family across the islands and distant lands.

This points to the conversion of the Gentiles and their entrance into the church.

Henry explains that the blessing may be read as saying that God would *persuade* Japheth, who would then dwell in Shem's tents.

Jew and Gentile would be united in the gospel fold. Both would become one in Christ.

Noah lived long enough to see two worlds: the world before the flood and the world after it.

Yet as an heir of the righteousness that comes by faith, he now rests in hope, waiting to see a world better than either.

## Chapter-level editorial audit and historical-context note

Compared in a separate reading pass against [CCEL's digital MHCC Genesis 9](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc/mhcc.ii.ix.html) and the publicly available original CrossWire MHCC text. The derivative's pinned original is `MHCC 0.1.1`, which documents no Genesis 9 text corrections.

- **1–3:** blessing/dependence, work and meat from animals, protective fear of humans, lawful use vs gluttony and cruelty—all retained.
- **4–7:** atoning blood, restraint of cruelty, suicide and accountability, God judging concealed murder, public authorities (Romans 13:4), author's explicit death-penalty position and residual imago Dei—all retained.
- **8–17:** old world destroyed/new world preserved, future fire, rainbow's likely earlier existence, cloud/bow, Sun of righteousness shining on tears, unstrung upward bow, divine revelation and faith, new covenant—all retained.
- **18–23:** *possible* surprise of Noah's sin, *probable* Ham's pleasure, blamelessness not sinless perfection, drunken gatekeeper image, Luke 21:34, 1 Peter 4:8, duty of filial respect—all retained.
- **24–29:** *possible* greater guilt of Canaan, covenant line through Shem, the author's historical interpretation of Africa, **his explicit condemnation of enslavers**, Semitic ancestry of Christ, conversion of Gentiles via Japheth, and better world in faith—all retained. No historically consequential passage was removed simply because its wording is uncomfortable.

**Explicit, non-authorial editorial clarification:** Henry's historical claims that most African populations descend from Ham and that Africa's history should be read as a fulfillment of Canaan's curse are **his interpretations**, not additional claims of the biblical text, established genealogical facts, or doctrines endorsed by the modern editor. They are retained here as attributed interpretations rather than silently updated, erased, or repeated as newly verified facts. The original term used for Black Africans has been replaced with its contemporary neutral equivalent, without weakening Henry's vigorous condemnation of cruel enslavement. Distinguishing the historical author from the modern editorial voice is necessary for readers to understand what the text actually asserts. This clarification is editorial and is not presented as a sentence of Matthew Henry.
