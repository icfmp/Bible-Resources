# Romans 10 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The apostle's earnest desire for the salvation of the Jews. | 1–4 |
| The difference between the righteousness of the law, and the righteousness of faith. | 5–11 |

## Paul's desire for Israel's salvation (verses 1–4)

Many Jews built their hope on **a false foundation**, refusing Christ's free salvation through faith.

People in every age make the same mistake in different forms.

The law's exacting demands were meant to reveal our need of salvation **by grace through faith**.

Its ceremonies pictured **Christ fulfilling its righteousness and bearing its curse**.

Even under the Mosaic law, everyone truly justified before God received that blessing **through faith in the perfect righteousness of the promised Redeemer**.

The law is not destroyed, nor are God's intentions disappointed.

Through Christ's death, satisfaction has been made for our breaking of the law, and its purpose has been accomplished.

**Christ fulfilled the whole law**.

Therefore, whoever believes in him is counted righteous before God **as though they themselves had fulfilled it**.

People who understood either God's justice as Governor or his righteousness as Savior would not continue imagining they could justify themselves.

## The righteousness of faith is available through Christ (verses 5–11)

Sinners who recognize their guilt do not need to trouble themselves about where this righteousness can be found.

When we speak of seeing, receiving or feeding upon Christ, we mean **Christ as he is offered and promised in God's word**, not someone we must physically retrieve from heaven or from the depths.

Justification through faith in Jesus Christ is **a plain teaching**.

God brings it before people's minds and hearts, so unbelief has no excuse.

If a person confesses faith in Jesus as Lord and Savior of lost sinners and sincerely believes in the heart that **God raised him from the dead**, that resurrection reveals the Father's acceptance of his atonement.

Such a person is saved through **Christ's righteousness imputed by faith**.

Yet no faith truly justifies unless it also has power **to sanctify the heart and direct its affections through love for Christ**.

We must give our whole selves to God—**the soul by believing in the heart and the body by confessing with the mouth**.

Those who trust Jesus will never regret that confidence.

Such faith will never leave a sinner ashamed before God, and believers should acknowledge it openly before others.

## The same salvation is offered to Jews and Gentiles (verses 12–17)

There is not one God generous to Jews and another less generous to Gentiles.

**The Lord is one Father who gives the same promise to all who call on Jesus' name**.

Jesus is the Son of God, **God manifested in human flesh**.

Genuine believers call upon him humbly and sincerely.

But how can anyone call upon the divine Savior without first **hearing about him**?

What is the Christian life if not a life of prayer?

Prayer acknowledges our dependence upon Christ and expresses our desire to entrust ourselves to him and expect every blessing from him.

The gospel therefore had to be preached to the Gentiles.

Someone had to tell them **what to believe**.

How welcome this good news should be to those hearing it!

The gospel is given not merely to be **known and believed**, but also **obeyed**.

It is not simply a system of opinions; it is **a guide for living**.

Faith begins, grows and becomes strong **through hearing**.

But faith grows through hearing the word **as God's own word**, not merely as human information.

## The prophets already foretold Gentile inclusion (verses 18–21)

Could the Jews have known that God would call Gentiles?

Yes. **Moses and Isaiah** had already announced it.

Isaiah clearly presented God's grace as taking the initiative in receiving Gentiles.

Was this not true of us?

Did God not love us first and reveal himself when we were not seeking him?

His patience toward people who continually provoke him is astonishing.

The time of divine patience is called **a day**.

Like daylight, it gives people light and opportunities to work.

But, like a day, it is **limited and followed by night**.

God's long patience makes persistent disobedience more blameworthy.

We can wonder that God's mercy is not overcome by human wickedness.

We can also wonder that human wickedness continues resisting God's mercy.

And we can rejoice that through the spreading gospel, **millions have heard the message of divine grace**.
