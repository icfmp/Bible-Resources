## Joseph is loved by Jacob and hated by his brothers (verses 1–4)

Joseph's history points us to Christ, who was humbled before being exalted. It also reflects the Christian's path: we must pass through many troubles before entering the kingdom.

Few accounts show so clearly the good and evil movements of the human heart, or God's remarkable providence in using both to fulfill his purposes.

Although Joseph was his father's favorite, he was not raised to be idle. Parents do not truly love their children when they spare them every task, effort, and hardship.

There is good reason why excessive pampering is called spoiling a child. Those who grow up doing nothing may end up good for nothing.

Yet Jacob made his preference obvious by dressing Joseph more finely than the rest. Parents should not favor one child over another without serious reason arising from their different conduct.

Children soon notice unequal treatment, and quarrels follow. Away from Jacob's supervision, his older sons behaved in ways they would never have dared at home.

Joseph reported their misconduct so that their father could restrain them. He was not spreading gossip to stir up discord; he acted as a faithful brother.

## Joseph's dreams (verses 5–11)

God gave Joseph an early glimpse of his future promotion, to support and comfort him through the long and painful troubles that lay ahead.

Joseph dreamed of advancement, but not of imprisonment.

Many young people beginning life are much the same: they imagine only success and pleasure, never the suffering they may encounter.

Joseph's brothers understood the dreams correctly, but hated their meaning. They committed crimes to prevent the prediction from coming true, yet became the very instruments of its fulfillment.

Something similar happened with Christ. The Jews understood what he said about his kingdom. Determined that he should not reign over them, they plotted his death. By crucifying him, they opened the way to the very exaltation they wanted to prevent.

## Joseph obeys his father; his brothers plot to kill him (verses 12–22)

How readily Joseph obeyed his father's instructions! Children who receive particular affection from their parents should be particularly ready to obey them.

See how deliberately his brothers plotted against him. They intended to kill him with calculated malice, in cold blood.

“Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer” (1 John 3:15).

The sons of Jacob hated Joseph because their father loved him. New events, including his dreams, fed the resentment already festering in their hearts until they resolved to kill him.

God holds every heart in his hand. Reuben, the firstborn, had more reason than any of the others to be jealous of Joseph, yet he proved Joseph's best friend.

God overruled their actions to accomplish his own purpose: Joseph would save many people from death.

Joseph was also a picture of Christ, the beloved Son of his Father, hated by a wicked world. The Father sent him from his presence to visit us in great humility and love.

Christ came from heaven to earth to seek and save us. People plotted maliciously against him; his own people did not receive him, but crucified him.

He submitted to it willingly as part of his purpose to redeem and save us.

## Joseph is sold (verses 23–30)

Joseph's brothers threw him into a pit to die of hunger and cold. How cruel were the supposed mercies of those who should have loved him!

They dismissed his distress and were not grieved by Joseph's suffering (Amos 6:6). While he languished in the pit, they sat down to eat.

They felt no remorse. Yet human wrath will praise God, and he will restrain whatever remains of it (Psalm 76:10).

Remarkably, the brothers were restrained from murdering Joseph. Just as remarkably, their decision to sell him was turned toward God's praise.

## Jacob is deceived; Joseph is sold to Potiphar (verses 31–36)

Once Satan has taught people to commit one sin, he teaches them to hide it with another: theft and murder are covered with lies and false oaths.

But whoever conceals sin will not prosper for long. Joseph's brothers kept their dreadful secret for a time, but their wickedness eventually came to light and has now been made known to the world.

They sent their father Joseph's richly colored coat to cause him grief. Seeing it covered in blood, Jacob quickly assumed that a wild animal had torn Joseph apart.

Anyone who knows a parent's heart can imagine his agony.

His sons pretended to comfort him. What miserable, hypocritical comforters! If they had truly wanted to help, they could have told him the truth at once.

Sin has a strange power to harden the heart. Jacob refused to be comforted.

The more strongly we attach ourselves to any created thing, the more deeply we may suffer if it is taken away or becomes bitter to us. Excessive love often leads to excessive sorrow.

Wise parents do not raise their children in excessive luxury and ease. They cannot know what hardships their children may face before death.

The whole chapter leaves us marveling at God's providence.

The malicious brothers seem to have achieved their purpose. The merchants, interested only in profit and indifferent to what they trade, seem to have achieved theirs. Potiphar has acquired a fine young slave and has also obtained what he wanted.

Yet through all of this, God's plan is moving forward. Joseph's sale leads to Israel's descent into Egypt, then to Israel's deliverance through Moses, then to the establishment of true religion in the world, and ultimately to its spread among all nations through the gospel.

Thus human wrath will praise the Lord, and he will restrain the remainder.
