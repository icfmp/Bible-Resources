# Ezekiel 32 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The fall of Egypt. | 1–16 |
| It is like that of other nations. | 17–32 |

## The fall of Egypt is lamented (verses 1–16)

It is fitting for us to **weep and tremble on behalf of people who will not weep or tremble for themselves**.

In God's judgment, great oppressors are no better than **beasts of prey**.

People who admire the world's splendor will be astonished when that splendor collapses.

Those who already know how empty earthly things are will be less surprised.

When other people are destroyed by their sins, **we too have reason to fear**, since we also are guilty.

The instruments of Egypt's destruction were powerful, and the examples of devastation frightening.

Egypt's waters would flow **like oil**.

Henry understands this as a sign that **deep sadness and heaviness would cover the nation**.

God can quickly empty people who possess a great abundance of worldly goods.

The more things we depend on for happiness, the more occasions we create for grief when they disappear.

Even the strongest human beings are **weak and helpless before God**.

The destruction of Egypt pictured **the destruction of Christ's enemies**.

## Egypt joins other nations in the grave (verses 17–32)

Many nations had been devastated and carried down to the grave before Egypt.

In the prophetic picture, they wait to receive Egypt with contempt.

Yet **Judah and Jerusalem**, although devastated around the same time by the same power, are **not included in this list**.

The difference lay in **God's merciful purpose in correcting his own people** and the salvation he had reserved for them.

For Judah, the suffering was not the same final descent into the pit.

Pharaoh would see the other nations and find a kind of consolation.

But the comfort wicked people imagine enjoying **after death** is only **fantasy**, not real comfort.

This picture of ruined kingdoms shows how sorrowful the present world is and how strong the rule of death appears in it.

Human beings already die quickly enough, yet they devise **ingenious ways to destroy one another**.

The passage also makes us look toward the next world.

Although it chiefly concerns the destruction of nations, Henry understands it to contain an unmistakable allusion to **the eternal ruin of those who remain unrepentant**.

How terribly Satan deceives people!

Why do they commit such evil and shed so much blood in pursuit of **wealth, fame, power or pleasure**?

Their anxious striving is empty.

The day will come when everyone in the grave hears **the voice of Christ** and comes out.

Those who have done good will rise **to life**; those who have done evil will rise **to condemnation**.
