# Acts 16 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Paul takes Timothy to be his assistant. | 1–5 |
| Paul proceeds to Macedonia, The conversion of Lydia. | 6–15 |
| An evil spirit cast out, Paul and Silas scourged and imprisoned. | 16–24 |
| The conversion of the jailer at Philippi. | 25–34 |
| Paul and Silas released. | 35–40 |

## Paul chooses Timothy for the mission (verses 1–5)

The church has good reason to expect useful service from **young ministers** who begin with Timothy's spirit.

But when people refuse to yield or accommodate others in anything, they appear to lack even the first elements of **a Christian spirit**.

There is little reason to expect such people to teach the gospel's doctrines and commandments successfully.

The council's decree had the purpose of setting aside the burdensome **ceremonial law and outward regulations**.

It established a spiritual way of serving God, suited to the nature of both God and humanity.

Through this, believers were strengthened in faith, and the church grew daily.

## Paul reaches Macedonia and Lydia believes (verses 6–15)

Ministers' movements and their opportunities to bring others the means of grace are directed in a special way by **divine Providence**.

We must follow that direction.

If God prevents us from carrying out what we planned, we ought to submit and trust that his decision is best.

People need spiritual help greatly.

They should therefore seek it, invite those able to instruct them and respond promptly when God calls.

God's worshipers should assemble solemnly on the Sabbath whenever possible.

If a synagogue is unavailable, be thankful for a smaller, more private place to gather.

Do not abandon fellowship when an opportunity exists.

**Lydia** was among Paul's hearers.

She had an honorable occupation, which Luke mentions to her credit.

Although responsible for a business, she found time to attend to her soul.

We cannot excuse neglecting religion by saying we have a trade to run.

We have both **work in the world** and **a God to serve and souls to care for**.

Christianity does not necessarily take us away from ordinary business; **it teaches us how to conduct it**.

Pride, prejudice and sin keep divine truth outside the understanding and affections until grace opens a way into them.

**The Lord alone opens a heart** to receive and believe his word.

We must trust Jesus Christ. No one comes to God as Father except **through the Son as Mediator**.

## Paul and Silas are beaten and imprisoned (verses 16–24)

Satan is the father of lies.

Yet he sometimes speaks important truths when doing so can advance his purposes.

False and ungodly people who claim to proclaim the gospel can do great harm by causing careless observers to confuse them with Christ's genuine servants.

People doing good by leading sinners out of wrongdoing may nevertheless be slandered as **troublemakers**.

They teach others to fear God, trust Jesus, abandon sin and live holy lives, but are accused of introducing dangerous customs.

## The Philippian jailer turns to Christ (verses 25–34)

God's consolations for servants suffering in his cause are neither few nor insignificant.

How much happier sincere Christians can be than the enemies who enjoy outward prosperity!

Even in deep darkness, we may cry to God.

There is no place and no hour when sincere prayer is inappropriate.

No suffering, however intense, should completely prevent us from **praising him**.

Christianity shows its divine origin in part by obliging us to respect and protect **our own lives**.

Paul shouted so that the jailer could hear and heed him: **“Do yourself no harm!”**

The warnings of Scripture against sin and all approaches to it have the same merciful purpose.

Do not ruin yourself.

Do not bring harm upon your own life through sin, for nothing can ultimately harm you as sin can.

Even regarding bodily health, Scripture warns us against practices that injure us.

Conversion changes how people speak **about and to** faithful believers and ministers.

The jailer's question became intensely serious.

His own salvation—formerly farthest from his thoughts—became **his deepest concern**.

He was anxious about his precious soul.

People convinced of sin and genuinely concerned about salvation will surrender themselves to Christ.

Here is the gospel, the covenant of grace, in a few words:

**“Believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved, you and your household.”**

The Lord so blessed that word that the jailer was immediately softened and humbled.

He now treated Paul and Silas with kindness and compassion.

Professing faith in Christ, he was **baptized together with his household**.

The Spirit worked such strong faith in them that their doubts gave way.

Paul and Silas knew through the Spirit that God had worked within them.

Once sinners are converted, they begin to love and honor people they once despised or hated, seeking to relieve the very suffering they once wanted to increase.

As faith's fruits become visible, terror gives way to **trust and joy in God**.

## Paul and Silas are honorably released (verses 35–40)

Paul was willing to endure suffering for Christ.

He did not seek revenge or personal satisfaction.

Yet he refused to depart as though he had deserved the **unlawful punishment** imposed upon him.

He therefore required a public and honorable release.

He was not insisting on a small point of wounded pride.

It was a matter of **justice**, important not merely for himself but **for the cause he represented**.

When an appropriate apology is offered, Christians should not continue expressing personal anger or demand excessive compensation for wounded feelings.

The Lord makes his faithful people more than conquerors through every conflict.

Instead of being destroyed by sufferings, they can become **comforters of fellow believers**.
