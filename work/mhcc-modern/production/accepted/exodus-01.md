# Exodus 1 — Modern English

## The children of Israel multiply (verses 1–7)

For more than two hundred years, while Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob lived in freedom, the Hebrew people increased only slowly.

Only about seventy people went down to Egypt.

Yet in roughly the same length of time, even under cruel slavery, they became a great nation.

This remarkable growth fulfilled the promise God had made long before to their fathers.

God may sometimes take a long time to fulfill his promises, but their fulfillment is always certain.

## Israel is oppressed in Egypt (verses 8–14)

Egypt became a place of slavery for Israel.

A place where we once found happiness may soon become the place where we suffer.

What we once expected to bring us the greatest comfort may become our heaviest burden.

We must not put our trust in people or say of any place short of heaven, “Here I will rest.”

Everyone who knew Joseph loved him and treated his brothers kindly for his sake.

But even the best and most useful service someone renders to others is quickly forgotten after that person dies.

Our chief concern must be to serve and please God.

Unlike human beings, he is not unjust. He will never forget our work or our labor of love.

Israel's supposed offense was its prosperity.

Nothing is more hateful to a wicked person than to see the righteous flourish.

The Egyptians feared that the Israelites would join Egypt's enemies and escape from the land.

Wickedness is cowardly as well as unjust. It fears where there is nothing to fear and flees when no one is pursuing.

Human wisdom is often foolish and deeply sinful.

Taskmasters were placed over God's people not merely to make them work hard, but deliberately to make them suffer under their burdens.

Their labor served Pharaoh's interests, yet it also made their lives bitter.

Still, Israel continued to grow remarkably.

Christianity has often spread most when it was persecuted: the blood of the martyrs became the seed of the church.

Those who plot against the Lord and his people achieve nothing lasting. Their plans are vain, and they only increase their own distress.

## Pharaoh orders the killing of Hebrew sons (verses 15–22)

The Egyptians tried to destroy Israel by murdering its children.

The hostility of the serpent's offspring toward the woman's Seed can make people abandon every feeling of compassion.

It was clear that the Hebrew people were under an extraordinary blessing.

We also see how acts of service toward God's Israel are often rewarded in kind.

Pharaoh ordered that all the Hebrew baby boys be drowned.

The same enemy who used Pharaoh to try to destroy the church in its infancy is still busy trying to suppress the first serious thoughts of repentance in the human heart.

Those who want to escape this enemy must fear committing sin and cry out immediately and earnestly to the Lord for help.
