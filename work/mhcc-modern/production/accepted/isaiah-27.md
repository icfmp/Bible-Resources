# Isaiah 27 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God's care over his people. | 1–5 |
| A promise of their recall to Divine favour. | 6–13 |

## God's fruitful and protected vineyard (verses 1–5)

Through the power of **his death**, and by the preaching of his gospel, **Jesus Christ** overcomes and will finally destroy the one who has the power of death—**the devil, the ancient serpent**.

The unbelieving world resembles a fruitless wilderness.

But **the church is God's vineyard**, tended carefully so that it will bear valuable fruit.

The Lord protects it during the night of suffering and persecution.

He also protects it during times of peace and prosperity, **when temptations can be no less dangerous**.

He cares not only for the vineyard's survival but also for its fruitfulness.

We need **the continual watering of divine grace**. If withdrawn, we wither and become unable to accomplish anything spiritually good.

God sometimes contends with his people, yet graciously invites them to reconciliation.

Where he finds only **briers and thorns** instead of useful vines, and those thorns stand in rebellion against him, he will tread them down and burn them.

This passage summarizes the gospel by which God's church should continually be watered.

Since sin entered the world, God has had a **righteous charge against humanity**; humanity has maintained an unrighteous quarrel against him.

Nevertheless, God graciously invites sinners to make peace.

**Pardoning mercy is the strength of the Lord**.

Cling to Christ crucified, the power of God, as a drowning person grasps a rope, branch or board within reach.

There is **no other name by which we can be saved**.

This is both the only way and a sure way to obtain salvation.

God is willing to be reconciled to us.

## The remnant restored and the gospel trumpet (verses 6–13)

In the latter days, under the gospel, the church would become **more securely established and more widely spread** than the ancient Jewish church.

May our souls be constantly watered and guarded so that we produce the Spirit's fruits in **goodness, righteousness and truth**.

Henry observes that the Jewish people continued to exist as **a numerous and distinct people**, although those who persecuted them had not survived in the same way.

He regards their endurance through the centuries as an important sign supporting the Scriptures' divine origin and as a solemn warning concerning sin.

This is his stated historical and theological interpretation.

However violent the storms, God can still say, **“Peace, be still.”**

He permits afflictions for his people's ultimate spiritual good.

Henry says that after the Babylonian captivity the Jews showed remarkable opposition to **idols and idolatry**.

For all believers, discipline fulfills its purpose when it separates them from sin, its occasions and temptations.

Jerusalem flourished under divine protection; when God withdrew that protection, the city became desolate.

This also pictures the distress of a church producing **wild grapes instead of good fruit**.

Sinners sometimes presume that God will spare them because he is merciful and because they are his creatures. Such claims are **not grounds for continuing in rebellion**.

Henry understands **verses 12–13** as pointing both toward the Jews' recovery after Babylon and **their eventual return from later dispersion**.

The passage further portrays sinners gathered to God through the **preaching of the gospel**, which proclaims the Lord's acceptable year.

Those hearing the gospel trumpet join the church and learn to worship God.

At the last trumpet, **all the saints will be gathered together**.
