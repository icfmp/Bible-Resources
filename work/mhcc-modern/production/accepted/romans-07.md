# Romans 7 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Believers are united to Christ, that they may bring forth fruit unto God. | 1–6 |
| The use and excellence of the law. | 7–13 |
| The spiritual conflicts between corruption and grace in a believer. | 14–25 |

## Believers are released from law as covenant and joined to Christ (verses 1–6)

As long as people remain under the law **as a covenant by which they seek justification through personal obedience**, they remain enslaved to sin in one form or another.

Only **the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus** can free sinners from the law of sin and death.

Believers are delivered from the law's **condemning power** for their past sins.

They are also delivered from the way the law, through their fallen nature, **provokes indwelling sin**.

This is not freedom from God's law **as a rule for living**.

It is freedom from the law **as a covenant of works**.

In profession and privilege Christians live under **a covenant of grace**, the gospel of Christ, not under the Mosaic law as a covenant of works.

Paul compares this change to **marriage to a new husband**.

Believers are joined to **Christ**.

Death ends the binding relationship to the former husband, just as death frees an enslaved worker from the former master's yoke.

Through living and effective faith, believers have **died to law as covenant** and become united to Jesus Christ.

They now live in dependence upon him and in obedience to him.

Good works come **from union with Christ**, as a vine bears fruit because it remains joined to its roots.

**No fruit acceptable to God is produced before union with Christ**.

The law and the best efforts of people still in fallen flesh cannot restore love for God, overcome sinful desires or create inward sincerity.

Those blessings come through **the Holy Spirit's special sanctifying work**.

Without the renewing, recreating grace of the new covenant, we can achieve no more than **outward conformity to the letter of a rule**.

## God's holy law exposes the sin in us (verses 7–13)

To understand sin as we must for repentance, peace and pardon, we need to examine our hearts and lives **against God's law**.

Paul did not recognize the sinfulness of his thoughts, motives and conduct until the law revealed it.

That perfect standard exposed faults far more numerous than he had imagined.

But **the law provided no mercy or grace to rescue him** from his guilt.

Anyone who understands fallen human nature recognizes a tendency to want forbidden things merely because they are forbidden.

We readily notice this in children while ignoring it in ourselves.

The more humble and spiritually mature a Christian becomes, the more clearly that believer recognizes Paul's description of spiritual struggle—from the first conviction of sin to later growth in grace.

Paul once lived as a Pharisee, apparently respectable but ignorant of the law's spiritual depth and of his own inward corruption.

When the Holy Spirit brought God's command to his conscience, Paul understood its demands.

His sinful nature then rose in resistance.

He recognized sin's evil, his own helplessness to keep God's law and his condition as **a condemned criminal**.

The commandment was not the cause of sin.

Rather, fallen corruption used the commandment as an opportunity to stir up sinful impulses.

**The law remains holy, just and good**.

It does not tolerate sin, but exposes and condemns it even within our hearts.

Corrupt nature can twist even the best things.

**The same heat softens wax and hardens clay**.

Good food or medicine can become harmful if used wrongly, though its proper purpose is to nourish or heal.

Similarly, the law exposes deadly corruption, but **sin is the poison that kills**.

The ruin comes from sin, not from the law itself.

## Remaining sin troubles the mature believer (verses 14–17)

Measured against God's holy rule, Paul recognized that he fell far short of perfection.

He described himself as **carnal**, like a person sold unwillingly to a cruel master he hates but cannot escape without help.

The genuine Christian is unwillingly troubled by **remaining sin** and cannot remove its last chains until a gracious and powerful Friend delivers them.

This remaining corruption painfully hinders service to God like that rendered by angels and perfected believers.

Henry interprets Paul's strong language as reflecting **his great progress in holiness**, profound self-abasement and hatred of sin.

If we cannot understand the depth of this struggle, perhaps we have far less holiness and insight into God's law and our own hearts.

Many believers have recognized their own condition in Paul's words.

Paul describes the daily conflict with remaining original corruption.

At times he fell into thoughts, words or actions that his **renewed judgment and affections rejected**.

When he distinguished his spiritual, renewed self from the flesh in which sin remained, he did **not** suggest people are no longer accountable for their sinful deeds.

He emphasized their evil: sin opposes both right reason and renewed conscience.

**Sin dwelling in someone does not prove that it rules them**.

A person may live in a country or city without governing it.

## The believer delights in holiness while resisting the flesh (verses 18–22)

The purer and holier a person's heart becomes, the more sensitive that person is to the sin remaining within.

Believers increasingly recognize the beauty of holiness and the excellence of God's law.

Their desire to obey grows through grace.

Yet they do not completely accomplish the good they desire.

Remaining sin rises within them, so they sometimes act wrongly **despite their settled intention to obey**.

This troubled Paul deeply.

Just as the flesh fights the Spirit and prevents the believer from doing all the good desired, so **the Spirit also resists the flesh** and prevents the believer from doing everything sinful desire suggests.

How different this is from those who willingly satisfy sinful impulses, ignore conscience and proceed deliberately toward ruin!

True believers, under grace, sincerely delight in God's law and the holiness it demands according to **the inward person renewed in true holiness**.

## Christ alone delivers from the continuing struggle (verses 23–25)

Paul is not describing a person who willingly follows the flesh.

He describes someone deeply concerned **not** to follow it.

Some abuse these verses to excuse their sin, as they abuse other Scriptures.

Yet serious Christians should thank God for the comfort he gives through this passage.

Abuse by sinners is no reason to reject Scripture or a well-grounded interpretation of it.

Only someone engaged in this struggle can deeply understand Paul's cry: **“What a wretched man I am!”**

Paul could not deliver himself.

This made him thank God all the more earnestly for the salvation revealed through **Jesus Christ**, who would finally free him from this enemy.

According to his renewed mind, will, affections and purposes, Paul served God's law.

Yet within his flesh the remaining corrupt nature continued fighting against his mind.

He did not serve sin by **approving or freely living in it**.

He remained unable to remove all its influence, even at his best, and therefore had to seek help from beyond himself.

Paul thanked God for **Christ as his deliverer, atonement and righteousness**, not for some holiness produced within himself as the ground of justification.

He did not trust in personal righteousness.

He wanted to obey God's law in every respect, but sin prevented perfect obedience.

For sinners who remain imperfect, the only final rescue is **God's free grace in Jesus Christ**.

God could, by divine power and the Holy Spirit, remove all remaining sin from Christians even during this life if he had so chosen.

But in his wisdom he allows this struggle to continue.

It reminds believers of the miserable condition from which grace rescued them.

It prevents **self-confidence**.

And it keeps all their consolation and hope fixed upon **the rich, free grace of God in Christ**.
