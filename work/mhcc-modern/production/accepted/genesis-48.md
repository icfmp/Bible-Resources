# Genesis 48 — Modern English

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Joseph visits his dying father. | 1–7 |

## Joseph visits Jacob on his deathbed (verses 1–7)

The deathbeds of believers, together with their final prayers and counsel, can make a lasting impression on young people, those who live lightly, and those enjoying prosperity.

When it is appropriate, it is good to bring children to witness such moments.

If the Lord allows, it is a precious thing to bear witness at the end of life to his truth, his faithfulness, and the goodness of his ways.

We should aim to live in such a way that our dying words carry weight.

All true believers are blessed in death, though not everyone dies with the same abundance of spiritual comfort.

Jacob adopted Joseph's two sons.

He did not want them chiefly to inherit their father's wealth and influence in Egypt, but to share in the promised inheritance given to Abraham.

Through this act, the dying patriarch taught the young men to choose their place among God's people.

He appointed each to become the head of a tribe.

Those who, by God's grace, resist the temptations of worldly riches and advancement in order to embrace true faith—even in poverty or disgrace—are worthy of special honor.

Jacob wanted Ephraim and Manasseh to understand that it is better to occupy a humble place within God's people than a high place outside them.

## Jacob blesses Ephraim and Manasseh (verses 8–22)

These two godly men recognized God's hand in their blessings.

Joseph said, “These are my sons, whom God has given me.”

Jacob said, “God has allowed me to see your children.”

Our blessings become doubly sweet when we recognize that they come from God's hand.

He does more than prevent what we fear; he exceeds what we hope for.

Jacob spoke of the care God had shown him all his life.

He had endured many hardships, but God had protected him from the evil in them.

As he approached death, Jacob looked forward to being redeemed forever from sin and sorrow.

Christ, the Angel of the covenant, redeems his people from every evil.

In Scripture, deliverance from misery and danger by God's power, coming through the ransom of Christ's blood, is often called redemption.

When Jacob blessed Joseph's sons, he deliberately crossed his hands.

Joseph wanted his firstborn to receive the leading blessing and would have rearranged his father's hands.

But Jacob was not confused, nor did he simply prefer one boy out of personal affection.

He acted by the Spirit of prophecy and according to God's direction.

God distributes blessings differently among his people.

He gives some more gifts, graces, comforts, and earthly blessings than others.

Often those we least expect to receive the most are the very ones God chooses.

He chooses the weak things of this world and lifts the poor out of the dust.

Grace does not follow the natural order, and God does not always give preference to the people we think most deserving.

He acts according to his own good pleasure.

How poor are people whose only riches belong to this world!

And how terrible it is to approach death with no solid hope of good—only fearful expectations of unending evil.
