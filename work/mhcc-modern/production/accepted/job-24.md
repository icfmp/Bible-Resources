# Job 24 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Wickedness often unpunished. | 1–12 |
| The wicked shun the light. | 13–17 |
| Judgements for the wicked. | 18–25 |

## Wickedness may go unpunished for a time (verses 1–12)

Job continued discussing the prosperity of wicked people.

He had already shown in **chapter 21** that people who disregard God can live comfortably.

Here he observed that some who openly defy justice succeed in their wicked actions without receiving visible punishment in this life.

He considered both those who act unjustly **under the cover of law and authority** and **robbers who use force**.

Job said that God did not immediately call them to account for their folly.

He did not mean God failed to see their deeds.

He meant that judgment did not come at once, exposing them publicly as foolish.

Yet the person who becomes rich through injustice will finally be exposed as a fool (**Jeremiah 17:11**).

## Wicked people avoid the light (verses 13–17)

Consider the energy and effort sinners invest in carrying out harmful plans.

Their determination should shame our laziness about doing what is good.

They work hard to prepare opportunities for sinful desires, then labor again to conceal acts that lead to death and final judgment.

Less effort devoted to **putting sinful desires to death** would bring life and heaven instead.

Shame entered the world through sin, and lasting shame awaits those who persist in it.

Sinners live with constant fear of being discovered.

How foolish!

They are afraid **of human eyes**, yet they do not fear **the eye of God**, which always sees them.

They are not frightened of committing deeds they are terrified others may discover.

## Job reflects on the judgments of the wicked (verses 18–25)

Some wicked people decline gradually and die quietly.

They may be honored in life and forgotten after death, along with their violence and oppression.

Death gathers people as a harvest worker gathers ears of grain.

Some of Job's observations may appear to match what we see in Providence.

But Henry warns that Job's **conclusions about God's purposes were distorted by partial knowledge**.

The Lord governs human affairs with complete justice and wisdom.

We must remember this whenever he tests us.

**God cannot do wrong.**

The unequaled suffering of God's Son would seem puzzling if viewed apart from this truth.

But when we see **Christ as the sinner's Surety**, bearing the curse we deserved, we understand how divine justice could be satisfied and his people saved through such suffering.
