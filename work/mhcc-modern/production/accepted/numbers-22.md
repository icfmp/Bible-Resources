## Balak asks Balaam to curse Israel (verses 1–14)

The king of Moab formed a plan to have Israel cursed.

He wanted God, who had been fighting for Israel, to turn against the people.

He mistakenly imagined that a prophet's curse against Israel—and a blessing upon his own army—would enable him to defeat them.

Balaam had a remarkable reputation, so Balak sent far away to hire him.

Henry says we do not know whether God had spoken to Balaam or through him before these events.

It seems possible, but afterward God certainly did speak through him.

Yet Scripture gives convincing evidence that Balaam lived and died as a wicked person and an enemy of God and his people.

A curse uttered against someone cannot harm that person without just cause.

Balak's messengers brought payment meant to tempt Balaam into wrongdoing.

But God restrained Balaam and forbade him to curse Israel.

Balaam was not ignorant of Israel's situation.

He should have answered immediately that he could never curse a people whom God had blessed.

Instead he delayed overnight to consider the proposal.

When we bargain with temptation, we place ourselves in danger.

Balaam did not faithfully communicate the full force of God's command to the messengers.

People who soften divine prohibitions are particularly vulnerable to temptation, treating disobedience as though it merely required additional permission.

The messengers also misrepresented Balaam's reply when reporting it to Balak.

Flattering advisers can keep rulers and other people from recognizing their own folly.

## A second delegation persuades Balaam (verses 15–21)

Balak sent a second group of messengers.

How good it would be if we persevered so firmly in doing right even after disappointments!

Balak appealed both to Balaam's greed and to his pride and ambition.

We should pray continually for God to mortify such desires within us.

Sinners spare neither time, cost nor humiliation when pursuing luxury or malice.

Should believers be less willing to do what is good?

Balaam knew that he was obligated to obey God.

His public words sounded excellent.

But speaking religiously proves nothing about the actual condition of the heart.

Many call God their Lord without truly belonging to him.

Balaam seemed to reject the offer, but he never expressed genuine hatred for the proposed sin.

He wanted the reward and hoped to receive God's permission to do what was already forbidden.

A heart is ruled by corruption when it asks God for permission to sin.

God left Balaam to pursue his own desires.

Just as God sometimes denies his children's requests in love, he may grant sinful desires in judgment.

## The angel opposes Balaam on the road (verses 22–35)

God sometimes permits people to proceed in sin.

We must never interpret that permission as approval.

Holy angels oppose wrongdoing and may prevent more of it than we realize.

The angel was Balaam's **adversary** only because Balaam was pursuing the wrong road.

People who block our progress toward sin are often our truest friends.

God exposed his displeasure to Balaam through the donkey.

A person determined to do evil may force a way forward despite every warning placed in the path by Providence.

The Lord made the animal speak.

This was a real miracle of God's power.

The One who made humans able to speak could give a donkey a human voice whenever he chose.

The donkey protested Balaam's cruelty.

God's righteousness protects even the weakest creatures from abuse; he can provide them with a voice or defend them himself.

At last Balaam's eyes were opened.

God has many ways of humbling a stubborn heart.

When our spiritual eyes are opened, we recognize how dangerous our sinful choices are and how much mercy there was in being stopped.

Balaam said, “I have sinned.”

Yet he did not seem to understand or admit the corruption of his own heart.

He was prepared to turn back only because he could not continue.

Some people abandon their sins only because their sins can no longer be carried out.

The angel made clear that Balaam would not merely fail to curse Israel: he would be compelled to bless the people.

That would bring God greater honor, and Balaam greater shame, than if he had returned home.

## Balaam meets Balak (verses 36–41)

Balak complained that Balaam had not come sooner.

Balaam warned the king that he could not say whatever he wanted.

He sounded annoyed.

Yet he remained just as eager to please Balak as he had previously claimed to be eager to please God.

We desperately need to pray, “Our Father in heaven, do not lead us into temptation.”

We should watch over our own hearts.

Balaam shows how much knowledge a person may possess about God while still remaining without the saving grace of God.
