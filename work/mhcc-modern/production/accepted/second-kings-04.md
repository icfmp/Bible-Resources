## Elisha multiplies the widow's oil (verses 1–7)

Elisha's miracles were works of real charity.

So were the miracles of Christ.

They were not merely extraordinary displays of power but acts of great kindness to the people who received them.

God shows both his greatness and his goodness.

Elisha listened willingly to a poor widow's trouble.

People who leave their families burdened with debt often have little idea how much suffering they cause.

Everyone who professes faith in the Lord should trust him for daily provision without testing him through carelessness or wastefulness.

We should avoid running into debt.

Few practices bring more disgrace upon the gospel or leave more hardship for surviving family members.

Elisha helped the widow find a way to pay what she owed and support herself and her children.

The provision was miraculous.

But it also illustrated a wise ordinary way of helping distressed people: **assist them in using what little they possess through their own diligent work**.

The oil continued flowing while empty containers remained.

There is no shortage in God or in the riches of his grace.

The limitations are in ourselves: our faith fails, not his promise.

He gives more than we ask.

If more containers had been provided, there was sufficient oil to fill them.

God has enough for all and enough for each.

The Redeemer is sufficient to provide salvation for every sinner who comes to him, and his saving resources will not be exhausted.

The widow was to **pay her debt first**, before using the remainder to provide for the children.

Though her creditors had been harsh, the debt remained due.

One of the chief duties of Christian living is to pay every just debt and give each person what rightly belongs to them, even if doing so leaves little for ourselves.

We should act from conscience, not merely compulsion.

Honest people cannot comfortably enjoy their daily bread unless it is truly theirs.

Then the widow and her family could live on what remained, using the proceeds from the oil to support an honest livelihood.

We should not expect miracles of this kind today.

But we can expect God's mercy as we seek him and wait upon him.

Widows in particular may trust the Lord who rules human hearts and can provide just as effectively without an outward miracle.

## The Shunammite woman is given a son (verses 8–17)

Elisha had earned the king's regard through his past service.

Yet a good person can find just as much happiness in serving others as in gaining influence or raising personal status.

The Shunammite woman did not require a favor from the king.

She was content to live among her own people, who loved and respected her and whom she could help.

Many people would be happier if they could recognize when their situation is already good.

God sees even a secret desire that we have held back in submission to his will.

He may hear the prayers of his servants on behalf of those who have helped them and send blessings that were **neither requested nor expected**.

The promises made by God's true servants must not be treated as mere attempts to flatter or deceive, like worldly promises.

## The Shunammite's son is restored to life (verses 18–37)

The child died suddenly.

All a mother's tenderness could not preserve the life of a child who had come through promise and prayer and been received as a gift of love.

Yet the mother's restraint under this terrible suffering was remarkable.

She did not utter a bitter or irritable word.

She trusted God's goodness so deeply that she was ready to hope he would restore what he had taken away.

What great faith!

The One who gave that faith would not disappoint it.

She asked her husband for permission to go immediately to the prophet.

Although she was a woman of rank, she did not rely solely on occasional visits from Elisha to her home.

She also participated in public worship.

God's ministers should ask sincerely about the welfare of their friends and families.

The woman's answer was, **“It is well.”**

How could everything be well when her child was dead at home?

Everything God does is well ordered.

All is well for those who have died **if they have gone to heaven**, and all may be well for those who remain **if suffering helps them continue toward heaven**.

Whenever an earthly comfort is removed, it is well if by grace we can say that our hearts had not made an idol of it.

If we had loved it too much, we have reason to fear it may have been given and removed as discipline.

Elisha prayed to God in faith.

The child was brought back to life and returned to his mother.

People who seek to bring spiritual life to those dead in sin must feel deep concern for them and pray with earnest effort.

No minister can give another person divine life.

Nevertheless, ministers should use every means and work as diligently as though they could.

## Harmful food purified and a small meal multiplied (verses 38–44)

There was a famine of bread, but **not a famine of hearing God's word**.

The sons of the prophets were gathered around Elisha, listening to his teaching.

Elisha made dangerous food wholesome.

If our meal consists of nothing more than a pot of stew, remember that even this great prophet and his guests had no more.

The table can become a trap.

Food that should nourish us may instead bring danger, which is reason to eat with a healthy awareness of our dependence on God.

As we receive life's provisions and comforts, we must remember death's approach and the danger of sin.

We should thank the Lord for making our food healthy and nourishing.

He is the Lord who heals us.

Elisha also made a little food sufficient for many.

What he had freely received, he freely gave.

God has promised his church that he will bless her provisions abundantly and satisfy her poor with bread (**Psalm 132:15**).

When he provides, his gift is enough.

What he blesses can accomplish a great deal.

Christ's later miracle of feeding the crowds was greater still.

Yet both teach that those who wait upon God while following their duties may trust his providential care.
