# Jeremiah 16 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Prohibitions given to the prophet. | 1–9 |
| The justice of God in these judgments. | 10–13 |
| Future restoration of the Jews, and the conversion of the Gentiles. | 14–21 |

## Jeremiah lives in expectation of the nation's ruin (verses 1–9)

The prophet was to behave as a person expecting **the destruction of his country very soon**.

Because distressing days lay ahead, God told him to refrain **from marriage, customary mourning for the dead and celebration**.

Those wanting others to believe God's word must show through **personal self-denial** that they believe it themselves.

All peace—**inward and outward, within the family and among the public**—comes from the Lord's kindness and mercy.

When he removes peace, distress follows.

In certain extraordinary circumstances it may be right to abstain from acts that are **normally our duty**.

We should never cling tightly to the pleasures and concerns of this passing life.

## The judgment is just (verses 10–13)

The people's response seems to have been a complaint against God.

Rather than humbling themselves and admitting sin, they **justified themselves**, as though the Lord had treated them unjustly.

But God answered plainly.

They were **more stubborn than their ancestors**.

Each one walked according to **the plans of his or her own heart**.

Since they refused to listen, they would be taken to an unfamiliar country.

If they had enjoyed **God's favor**, even the land of captivity could have been pleasant.

## Restoration will foreshadow salvation through Christ (verses 14–21)

The rescue from Babylon would be remembered instead of **the original exodus from Egypt**.

It would also picture **spiritual redemption** and what Henry describes as a later deliverance of the church from **antichristian oppression**.

No sin can be hidden from God or overlooked by him.

He can send instruments of judgment **like fishermen who use deception** or **hunters who pursue with force**.

But Jeremiah, rejoicing in the promised mercy, addressed the Lord as **his strength and refuge**.

The return from captivity would be a picture of **the far greater salvation accomplished by the Messiah**.

The nations had often seen God's power in his anger.

They would also recognize him as **the strength of his people and their refuge in times of trouble**.
