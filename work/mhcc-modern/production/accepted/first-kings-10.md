## The queen of Sheba visits Solomon (verses 1–13)

The queen of Sheba came to hear Solomon's wisdom so that she might increase her own understanding.

Our Savior refers to her search for knowledge of God through Solomon in order to rebuke those who fail to seek God through Jesus Christ himself.

We, too, can find our way through difficulties by waiting upon God in prayer, diligently searching the Scriptures, seeking advice from wise and experienced Christians, and putting what we learn into practice.

Solomon's wisdom impressed the queen more deeply than his riches, power and magnificence.

The excellence of heavenly things, and of Christians whose conduct agrees with their faith, is greater than any report about them can adequately express.

In the queen's experience the truth exceeded the reports.

All who, by grace, enjoy fellowship with God will likewise discover that not even half the pleasure and benefit of walking in wisdom was told them beforehand.

How much more will the glorified saints say, when they enter heaven, that not even a thousandth part of its happiness had been described to them (1 Corinthians 2:9)!

The queen called those who continually attended Solomon happy.

We have still greater reason to call the servants of Christ blessed: those who dwell in his house will continue praising him.

She brought Solomon a generous gift.

Christ does not need anything we give him, but he calls us to give as an expression of gratitude.

A believer who has enjoyed fellowship with Jesus will return to ordinary responsibilities ready to fulfill them with better motives, while looking forward to the day when being absent from the body will mean being present with the Lord.

## Solomon's riches (verses 14–29)

Solomon's wealth increased until silver was scarcely valued.

This is the nature of worldly riches: when they become plentiful, people tend to value them less.

How much more should the enjoyment of spiritual wealth diminish our attachment to earthly possessions!

If abundant gold makes silver seem insignificant, should not wisdom, grace and foretastes of heaven—which are worth far more than gold—make gold itself seem small to us?

Solomon's greatness fulfilled God's promise.

Let that encourage us to seek first God's kingdom and his righteousness.

Yet Solomon was also the man who, after experiencing every kind of earthly pleasure, wrote a book declaring that worldly things are vanity, accompanied by vexation of spirit, and that setting our hearts on them is foolish.

He recommended sincere godliness as bringing far greater happiness than all the riches and power he possessed.

Through God's grace, that true happiness is available to us.
