# Genesis 47 — Modern English

## Joseph presents his brothers to Pharaoh (verses 1–6)

Joseph had become a powerful man, especially in Egypt, but he was not ashamed to acknowledge his brothers.

The rich and influential should never overlook or despise their poorer relatives.

Our Lord Jesus is not ashamed to call us his brothers and sisters.

Pharaoh asked the brothers what work they did.

They answered that they were shepherds and had come to stay only for a time, while the famine continued in Canaan.

Pharaoh offered to employ them as shepherds if they were capable and hardworking.

Whatever our occupation, we should aim to do it well and show ourselves skillful and diligent.

## Jacob blesses Pharaoh (verses 7–12)

Jacob prayed that the Lord would bless Pharaoh.

He did so with the dignity of old age, the devotion of a true believer, and the authority of a patriarch and prophet.

He was not ashamed of his faith. He also wanted to show gratitude to the ruler who had helped him and his family.

Jacob then gave a remarkable answer to an ordinary question.

He called his life a pilgrimage: the journey of a stranger living in a foreign land on the way to his true home.

Earth was not his permanent home. His dwelling, inheritance, and treasure were in heaven.

He counted his life in days.

Even measured that way, life is quickly numbered, and none of us can be sure of having even one more day.

Let us therefore learn to number our days.

Jacob said his days had been few. Although he was 130 years old, that was very little compared with eternity.

He said his days had also been difficult.

Human life is short and full of trouble. Since our days are marked by evil, it is well that they are few.

Jacob had lived through much suffering and had aged more quickly than some of his ancestors.

Young people should not be proud of their strength or beauty.

Neither should older people be proud of their age or gray hair, though others rightly show them respect.

Even those thought very old today do not reach the ages of the patriarchs.

Gray hair is a crown of glory only when it is found along the path of righteousness.

Jacob's answer must have reminded Pharaoh that earthly success and happiness cannot last or finally satisfy us.

After a life of emptiness and distress, everyone enters the grave, whether from a throne or a cottage.

Only the prospect of a lasting home in heaven can give us true happiness after our brief and weary journey on earth.

## Joseph provides for the Egyptians during the famine (verses 13–26)

Once Jacob and his family had been provided for—the particular mercy that God intended in raising Joseph to power—the account turns to the preservation of Egypt itself.

There was no bread, and the people were close to death.

See how completely we depend on God's providence.

All our wealth could not save us from starvation if rain failed for two or three years.

We are utterly dependent on God's mercy. Let us remain in his love.

See also how much suffering is caused by our own lack of care.

If the Egyptians had stored grain during the seven years of plenty, they would not have been so desperate.

But they ignored the warning.

Silver and gold could not feed them; they needed grain.

People will give everything they possess to preserve their lives.

Henry cautions that Joseph's actions here should not be judged by the standards of his own later age. He notes that the Egyptians regarded Joseph as a public benefactor.

In Henry's assessment, Joseph acted consistently with his character, dealing between Pharaoh and the people in the fear of God.

The Egyptians themselves said to Joseph, “You have saved our lives.”

How many people will gratefully say to Jesus on the last day, “You saved our souls from the most dreadful destruction, when our need was greatest!”

The Egyptians surrendered their property, and even their freedom, to stay alive.

Should we then think it too much to count everything as loss and surrender all at Christ's command?

For his sake we can do so, because he saves our souls and promises to give us a hundredfold, even in this present world.

If Christ has saved us, we will gladly become his servants.

## Jacob prepares to die (verses 27–31)

At last the time approached when Israel would die.

He had been a prince with God, had wrestled with the Angel, and prevailed. Yet he still had to die.

Joseph's provision of bread had saved him from death by famine, but could not protect him from death through old age or illness.

Jacob died by degrees, like a candle slowly burning down to its socket. He could see death approaching.

It is a mercy to recognize death's approach before feeling its full force, so that we are moved to do with all our strength whatever work remains.

But death is never very far from any of us.

As Jacob approached it, his concern was where he would be buried—not how grand his funeral would be.

He wanted to be buried in Canaan because it was the promised land.

Canaan pointed toward heaven, the better country he was waiting for (Hebrews 11:14).

Nothing eases a dying believer's heart more than the sure prospect of rest in the heavenly Canaan.

When this request was settled, Jacob bowed at the head of his bed and worshiped God, as explained in Hebrews 11:21.

He thanked God for all his kindnesses, supported himself despite his weakness, and showed that he was willing to leave this world.

Even those who lived on Joseph's food—and even Jacob, whom Joseph loved so dearly—had to die.

But Jesus Christ offers the true bread, which we may eat and live forever.

Let us come to him and surrender ourselves to him.

As death draws near, the One who sustained us in life will meet us and assure us of everlasting salvation.
