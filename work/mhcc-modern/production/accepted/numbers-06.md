## The Nazirite vow (verses 1–21)

The word *Nazirite* means someone **set apart**.

Some people, including Samson and John the Baptist, were appointed by God before their birth for lifelong consecration.

Ordinarily, however, a Nazirite vow was taken voluntarily for a limited period.

It involved separation from ordinary worldly pleasures and special dedication to God's service.

Scripture already mentions Nazirites as a known class, but this chapter gives definite rules for their obligations.

God supplied those rules so that superstition would not endlessly add further restrictions.

Nazirites were forbidden wine, strong drink and even grapes.

Those who dedicate themselves to God must not let physical appetite rule them.

Christians should practice moderation in alcoholic drinks; once someone becomes ruled by them, Satan finds that person easier to trap.

Nazirites were to avoid everything produced by the vine.

This teaches us to keep away not only from sin itself but also from things near it that draw us into temptation.

They were forbidden to cut their hair or shave during the vow.

This marked their willingness to forgo bodily ease and outward adornment.

It was also the outward sign of Samson's lifelong separation.

They must not touch ritually unclean things.

Likewise, people devoted to God should keep their consciences pure from dead works.

Through every day of the vow they were to be holy to the Lord.

Without inward holiness, all their outward abstinence was worthless.

For deliberate, defiant violation of the vow, Henry notes that no specific penalty or sacrifice was appointed: the guilty would have to answer to God.

But the law made provision for those whose vows were disrupted without willful sin.

Henry contrasts these vows with the religious orders of the Roman Catholic Church as he understood them.

**He argues that the rules are fundamentally different**, not that Nazirites directly authorize those later orders.

Unlike those orders, Nazirites were not forbidden marriage, nor were they subject to a general ban on permitted foods.

They abstained completely from wine, whereas the orders Henry criticizes did not.

Ordinary Nazirites chose a temporary period rather than making a lifelong vow, and in certain circumstances a parent or husband had authority concerning the vow.

This is Henry's historical and confessional criticism; it should not be treated as an editorial claim independently established for the modern world.

Christ is not only our **Surety**, but also our **example**.

For his sake we should renounce sinful pleasures, resist fleshly desires, separate ourselves from sin, openly confess our faith, moderate our natural affections, cultivate spiritual thinking and devote ourselves to God's service.

Our lives should encourage others to follow him.

## The priestly blessing (verses 22–27)

The priests were appointed to bless the people solemnly in the Lord's name.

What makes up this blessing?

God's almighty protection as our Savior.

His favor, like the smile of a loving Father or the warmth of sunlight.

His forgiveness of sins, provision for our needs, comfort in sorrow and grace preparing us for eternal glory.

Together these are the greatest blessings anyone can receive.

Compared with them, worldly pleasures scarcely deserve mention.

This blessing was also a form of prayer.

The name **Jehovah** appears three times.

Henry notes that Jewish interpreters saw a mystery in the repetition.

He believes the New Testament explains it: we are to look for grace from the Lord Jesus Christ, love from God the Father and fellowship from the Holy Spirit (2 Corinthians 13:14).

Each Person is Jehovah, yet there are not three Lords but **one Lord**.
