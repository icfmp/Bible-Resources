# Matthew 21 — Modern English

## Jesus enters Jerusalem (verses 1–11)

The prophet Zechariah foretold Christ's coming as king (**Zechariah 9:9**).

When Jesus displayed royal glory, he did so through **humility and gentleness**, bringing salvation through mercy rather than earthly splendor.

The poverty and meekness of Zion's King show how unsuitable greed, ambition and pride are in those who belong to his kingdom.

The disciples obtained the donkey, but Jesus did not use it **without its owner's agreement**.

The equipment for his ride was whatever could be provided.

We should not consider even the clothing we wear too valuable to be offered for Christ's service.

The religious rulers who later joined the crowd mocking Jesus on the cross had not joined the crowd honoring him at his entrance.

Those who truly accept Christ as king must be ready to **lay everything at his feet**.

**Hosanna** means **Save now, we pray!**

How little lasting value the applause of a crowd may have!

The same people can shout **Hosanna** one day and **Crucify him** another.

Many outwardly welcome the gospel without becoming steadfast disciples.

The whole city was stirred by Jesus' arrival.

Some who awaited Israel's consolation may have rejoiced.

Others, including Pharisees, were moved by envy.

Christ's kingdom produces very different responses in human hearts.

## Jesus cleanses the temple (verses 12–17)

Parts of the temple courts had become a market for animals and materials used for sacrifices, and a place for money-changing.

Christ expelled those responsible, as he had done earlier in his ministry (**John 2:13–17**).

His works witnessed to his true identity even more powerfully than the cries of **Hosanna**.

His healings in the temple fulfilled the promise that the glory of the later house would exceed that of the earlier one.

If Jesus examined the visible church today, how many hidden wrongs would he discover and cleanse?

How many things are performed under the appearance of religion that belong more properly to **a den of thieves** than to **a house of prayer**?

## Jesus curses the fruitless fig tree (verses 18–22)

The barren fig tree represents **hypocrites in general**.

Christ seeks the reality and power of faith where he sees outward profession.

Many appear spiritually flourishing but bear **leaves without fruit**.

Empty profession frequently withers even in this life under Christ's judgment.

The fig tree lost its leaves because it had produced no fruit.

Henry also applies this warning **to the Jewish nation of Jesus' day**, interpreting its rejection of Christ, growing hardness and eventual national ruin through the fig tree.

The Lord is just in his judgments.

Let us fear becoming people who look spiritually alive while remaining fruitless.

## Jesus answers those challenging his authority (verses 23–27)

Jesus now appeared publicly as Messiah.

The chief priests and scribes were especially angry because he exposed abuses they had allowed or encouraged.

He questioned them about **John's ministry and baptism**.

Many people fear being exposed as liars more than they fear actually lying.

They therefore say things contrary to their true thoughts or intentions, or pretend to remember and forget what suits their purpose.

Jesus declined to answer opponents determined to use any reply against him.

It is often best to avoid **pointless disputes with malicious opponents**.

## The two sons (verses 28–32)

Parables of rebuke invite offenders to judge their own conduct by their own answers.

The two sons illustrate the contrast between people who gave an outward appearance of obedience but rejected John's mission, and people who initially resisted yet afterward acknowledged its authority and responded.

All humanity resembles children whom the Lord has raised and who have rebelled against him.

Some appear more polite and respectable in their disobedience than others.

Often an openly defiant sinner is brought to repentance and becomes God's servant, while a religious formalist becomes harder in pride and hostility.

## The wicked tenants of the vineyard (verses 33–46)

The parable exposes the sin and ruin of the **Jewish leaders and nation in the narrative**.

Its warning also applies to **everyone who possesses the outward church's privileges**.

People who mistreat God's servants reveal how they would treat Christ himself.

Those faithful to his cause should not expect an entirely favorable reception from a wicked world or from merely nominal Christians.

We should ask whether, as congregations, families and individuals, we yield the fruit that ought to follow the spiritual advantages God has given us.

The owner of the vineyard will come and judge wicked tenants.

The religious leaders were builders who rejected the stone God had chosen.

They rejected Christ's teaching and laws and treated him as worthless.

Henry applies the rejected stone's acceptance to **Gentiles who embraced the Savior**.

Christ knows who will produce gospel fruit through the means of grace.

Unbelief destroys sinners.

But God can restrain human anger and turn even its outbreaks toward his praise.

May Christ grow increasingly precious to us as **the secure Foundation and Cornerstone of the church**.

May we willingly follow him even when despised and hated for his sake.
