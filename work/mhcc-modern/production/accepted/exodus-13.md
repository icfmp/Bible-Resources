## The firstborn are dedicated to God; the deliverance is remembered (verses 1–10)

God commanded Israel to dedicate its firstborn sons to him. This memorial joined two great events: the deaths of Egypt's firstborn, both human and animal, and Israel's deliverance from slavery.

The people were to remember that their lives had been spared through a ransom. That ransom pointed ahead to the atonement that would be made for sin at the appointed time.

Lives rescued from death are to be devoted to God's service.

Parents were not to think of their firstborn as belonging to them without first presenting them solemnly to God and acknowledging his claim upon them.

Whatever God spares for us through special mercy should be used for his honor. At the very least, we should acknowledge his goodness through grateful acts of devotion and charity.

Israel was to remember its departure from Egypt every year.

We should also remember the day of Christ's resurrection. In him we were raised from death's house of slavery.

Scripture does not expressly tell us the day of the year on which Christ rose, but it does tell us the day of the week. Henry therefore urges us to commemorate this greater deliverance weekly.

Israel observed the feast of unleavened bread. Under the gospel, we must do more than remember Christ in thought: we must also observe his holy Supper, as he commanded, in remembrance of him.

Parents must teach their children to know God. This is an ancient command to instruct them in the faith.

It is especially valuable to acquaint children with Bible history while they are young.

People who have God's law in their hearts should also have it on their lips. Speaking about it often strengthens their own hearts and teaches others.

## The firstborn animals and the ransom (verses 11–16)

Firstborn animals that could not be offered as sacrifices had to be exchanged for animals that could, or else be destroyed.

Our own souls have been forfeited under God's justice. Unless Christ's sacrifice ransoms them, they will certainly perish.

These ordinances constantly reminded Israel of its duty to love and serve the Lord.

In a similar way, baptism and the Lord's Supper remind Christians of their profession and their duties—when these ordinances are explained and observed properly. They also give us opportunities to remind one another.

## Israel takes the longer road (verses 17–20)

There were two possible routes from Egypt to Canaan.

One would take only a few days. The other, through the wilderness, was much longer.

God chose the longer route for his people.

The Egyptians would be drowned in the Red Sea, while the Israelites would be humbled and tested in the wilderness.

God's way is the right way, even when it seems a roundabout way.

If he does not lead us along the shortest path, we can be sure he leads us along the best one. We will see that clearly when we reach our journey's end.

The Philistines were formidable enemies. The Israelites needed to be prepared for the wars of Canaan through the hardships of the wilderness.

God measures his people's trials according to their strength (1 Corinthians 10:13).

The people departed in orderly formation. Henry records differing explanations of the formation: five abreast or divided into five companies.

They also carried Joseph's bones. This testified to their faith and hope that God would bring them to Canaan.

## The Lord guides his people by cloud and fire (verses 21–22)

The Lord went ahead of Israel in a pillar—a visible sign of the divine presence.

Christ was with his church in the wilderness (1 Corinthians 10:9).

When God leads his people into a wilderness, he does not abandon or lose them there. He cares for them and leads them through it.

For Moses and the faithful Israelites, being sure of divine guidance was a great comfort.

We too may be sure that the Lord goes before us when we make his glory our goal, his word our rule, his Spirit the guide of our desires, and his providence the guide of our circumstances.

We cannot see him with our eyes. Now we must live by faith.

As Israel moved, the pillar went ahead and indicated the place where divine wisdom intended them to camp.

It sheltered them from the heat by day and gave them light at night.

God's word is likewise a lamp for our feet and a light for our path. Our Savior's love has provided it for us, and it testifies about Christ.

The Bible is to us what the pillar was to Israel.

Listen to the One who says that he is the light of the world: whoever follows him will not walk in darkness but will have the light of life (John 8:12).

Jesus Christ alone is the way, the truth, and the life (John 14:6).

He is made known to us in Scripture, and the Holy Spirit directs our souls to him in answer to prayer.
