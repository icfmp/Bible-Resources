# Job 16 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Job reproves his friends. | 1–5 |
| He represents his case as deplorable. | 6–16 |
| Job maintains his innocency. | 17–22 |

## Job calls his friends miserable comforters (verses 1–5)

Eliphaz had claimed Job's words were useless and irrelevant.

Job now made the same charge against Eliphaz's words.

People who condemn others' speech should expect their own to be condemned in turn.

This is easy, and the exchange can continue indefinitely.

But what good does it accomplish?

**Angry replies excite passions. They do not convince minds or make truth clearer.**

Job's judgment of his friends also reminds us of the limits of every created comfort compared with God.

Eventually we will all discover that without him, created things are poor comforters.

When sin weighs on the conscience, judgment terrifies or death approaches, **the Holy Spirit alone** can give truly effective comfort.

In times of suffering, all other comfort without his work accomplishes little.

We should sympathize deeply with the sorrows of our brothers and sisters, as if they were our own.

Soon enough, those troubles may become our own.

## Job describes his misery (verses 6–16)

Job gave a painful account of his suffering.

How much reason do we have to thank God if we are not experiencing troubles like his!

Even good people find it hard, during great distress, to prevent bitter thoughts about God.

Eliphaz had accused Job of refusing humility.

Job replied that he understood humility all too well: **dust was the right place for him**.

In this he reminds us of Christ, the **Man of Sorrows**, who pronounced mourners blessed because they would receive comfort.

## Job defends his innocence and seeks a witness (verses 17–22)

Job's position was terrible.

Yet his conscience testified that he had not willingly practiced any grave, deliberate sin.

He remained ready to acknowledge sins arising from human weakness.

Eliphaz had accused him of religious hypocrisy.

Job therefore pointed to **prayer**, a central act of true religion, and maintained that his prayers had been sincere, though not free from every imperfection.

He still had a God to whom he could turn, confident that the Lord fully knew his suffering.

People who pour out tears before God may have many faults and be unable to defend themselves.

Yet **they have a Friend who pleads for them—the Son of Man**.

Our hope of acceptance before God must rest upon him.

Death is a journey from which we do not return to earthly life.

Each of us must make that journey, certainly and soon.

Should the Savior not therefore be precious to us?

Should we not be prepared to obey and suffer for him?

If our consciences have been cleansed by his atoning blood and testify that we are not continuing deliberately in sin or hypocrisy, death will become **release from prison and entrance into everlasting joy**.
