# Matthew 20 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The parable of the labourers in the vineyard. | 1–16 |
| Jesus again foretells his sufferings. | 17–19 |
| The ambition of James and John. | 20–28 |
| Jesus gives sight to two blind men near Jericho. | 29–34 |

## Workers in the vineyard (verses 1–16)

The parable's first purpose appears to be showing how the **Gentiles** would be called after the Jews and receive **equal privileges** in God's kingdom.

It also teaches broader lessons.

**First, God owes no one a debt.**

**Second, some people who begin late** and seem unpromising may, by God's blessing, grow into great knowledge, holiness and usefulness.

**Third, the final reward of the saints is not measured by how long ago they were converted**.

The parable describes the visible church and explains different ways in which the last become first and the first last.

Until God calls us to his work, we remain idle all day.

A sinful life is actually hard service to Satan, yet it is **idleness with respect to God**.

The marketplace represents the world, from which the gospel summons us.

Come away from the marketplace and serve God.

His work does not allow carelessness.

A person may drift into destruction through idleness, but the path to heaven requires diligence.

The **Roman denarius**, the coin paid for the day's work, is explained by Henry using a value in the currency of his own time (**seven and a half old English pence**), as sufficient wages then for a day's support; that historical comparison is not a modern wage conversion.

This reward does not mean obedience creates a debt God must pay.

After all our service, we are still **unprofitable servants**.

Nevertheless, God sets an actual reward before his people.

The eleventh-hour call is **no excuse to postpone repentance until old age**.

The late workers had not earlier received an offer of work.

Similarly, the Gentiles were called when the gospel first reached them.

Those who have already heard repeated gospel invitations and refused them will not be able to claim later that **no one called them**.

Do not be discouraged, but do not delay.

**Now is the accepted time**.

Proud Pharisees and merely nominal Christians often complain when God's grace is generous to others.

We tend to imagine that God has favored others too much and us too little, or that we have worked far harder than others.

But God's kindness to another person is **no injustice to us**.

Worldly people choose their payment in this present life.

Believers must remember that they seek **heaven as their inheritance**.

Why then should they expect ultimate satisfaction from earthly goods?

God punishes no one beyond what is deserved and never neglects faithful service.

By showing extraordinary grace to some, he wrongs no one.

Envy is **an evil eye**: resenting someone else's happiness and desiring harm for them.

It pains the envious person, displeases God and hurts neighbors.

It brings neither real pleasure, profit nor honor.

Let us abandon claims of merit and seek salvation as a **free gift**.

Rather than begrudging divine mercy, rejoice when God shows it to others.

## Jesus predicts his suffering and resurrection (verses 17–19)

Jesus now spoke even more specifically about his approaching suffering.

As before, he also promised his **resurrection and glory**, encouraging and comforting the disciples.

Seeing by faith the Savior who was once crucified and is now glorified humbles proud and self-justifying hearts.

When we recognize that the Son of God needed to be humbled and suffer for the salvation of sinners, we understand more clearly the **freedom and abundance of grace**.

## Jesus answers the ambition of James and John (verses 20–28)

The sons of Zebedee misused Christ's promise of future comfort as an opportunity for personal ambition.

Some people take even spiritual encouragement and turn it toward selfish purposes.

Pride easily besets us.

Seeking to surpass others in rank and splendor is sinful ambition.

Jesus corrected their request by speaking of the **suffering** his followers would endure.

Their cup could be bitter and cause trembling, yet it was **not the cup of God's wrath against the wicked**.

It was a cup held by their **Father** (**John 18:11**).

Suffering may be severe but it is temporary—a cup that will eventually be emptied.

Henry likens suffering for Christ to **baptism**, which signifies joining the Lord in covenant and fellowship.

He describes baptism as **an outward and visible sign of inward and spiritual grace**, and sees sufferings in Christ's service as signs of that grace too (**Ezekiel 20:37; Isaiah 48:10; Philippians 1:29**).

James and John did not yet understand the full meaning of Christ's cup or baptism.

Often those who speak most confidently know least about the cross.

Ambition causes profound damage among brothers and sisters.

Wherever the disciples quarreled, the desire for greatness lay beneath it.

The one who serves most diligently, suffers most patiently, seeks the good of fellow believers and labors for the salvation of souls resembles Christ most closely.

Such a servant will receive the greatest honor from Christ in eternity.

Jesus described his own death using the language of **Old Testament sacrifices**.

His sacrifice for human sin is the **true and substantial offering**, of which the legal sacrifices were only faint and incomplete pictures.

He gave his life as **a ransom for many**: a sacrifice sufficient for all, effective in many.

A trembling believer may therefore ask, **“Why not for me?”**

## Jesus restores sight to two blind men (verses 29–34)

People sharing the same illness or trial do well to **pray together**, encouraging one another.

Christ has mercy enough for everyone who asks him.

The blind men prayed **earnestly**. They cried as people who genuinely desired help.

Cold, indifferent desires are unlikely to obtain what is needed.

They prayed **humbly**, placing themselves in the hands of the merciful Mediator.

They prayed **with faith**, acknowledging Jesus as Lord.

Henry attributes this confession to the Holy Spirit.

They also **persevered**, refusing to fall silent when others discouraged them.

Jesus received and encouraged them.

We usually recognize bodily suffering quickly and can explain it readily.

If only we recognized **spiritual sickness and blindness** with the same urgency!

Many spiritually blind people insist they can see.

Jesus gave the men sight, and then they followed him.

**No one follows Christ blindly in the spiritual sense.** First he opens the eyes through his grace; then the heart is drawn to him.

These miracles summon us to come to Jesus.

May our daily prayer be to **grow in grace and in the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ**.
