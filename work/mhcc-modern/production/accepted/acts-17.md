## Paul preaches in Thessalonica (verses 1–9)

The purpose of Paul's preaching and reasoning was to prove that **Jesus is the Christ**.

Christ **had to suffer for us**, because otherwise he could not purchase our redemption.

He also **had to rise again**, because otherwise he could not apply that redemption to us.

We proclaim that Jesus is the Messiah.

Therefore, we may hope for salvation through him, and we must submit to **his rule**.

Some unbelieving Jews were angry because the apostles preached salvation to Gentiles.

How strange that people begrudge others privileges that they themselves refuse to accept!

Neither rulers nor ordinary citizens should fear the spread of genuine Christian faith, although turbulent people sometimes use religion as a cover for evil schemes.

We should distance ourselves from those who misuse religion for wrongdoing.

Then we can show our desire to act uprightly in society while still claiming the right to worship God **according to conscience**.

## The Bereans search the Scriptures (verses 10–15)

The Jews at Berea gave serious attention to the message.

They did not merely hear Paul preach on the Sabbath.

**They examined Scripture every day**, comparing its teaching with what the apostles reported.

Christ's doctrine has nothing to fear from honest inquiry.

Those defending it ask only that people examine **fully and fairly** whether the message is true.

Genuinely noble people take Scripture as their rule and constantly consult it.

May all who hear the gospel resemble the Bereans, receiving the word eagerly but **checking daily in Scripture whether the teaching is true**.

## Paul encounters Athenian philosophers (verses 16–21)

Athens was famous for intellectual learning, philosophy and the fine arts.

Yet some people highly regarded for intelligence and knowledge are remarkably childish, superstitious, credulous and irreverent in spiritual matters.

Athens had become full of idols.

Anyone zealous for Christ should be ready to speak on his behalf in whatever company opportunities arise.

Most educated Athenians paid little attention to Paul.

Others, including representatives of philosophical schools whose principles especially opposed Christianity, commented on his teaching.

Paul continually proclaimed **two central subjects**: **Christ and the life to come**.

Christ is **the Way**; heaven is **the destination**.

The Athenians found these teachings unfamiliar after centuries of their own intellectual traditions.

They wanted to hear more, but not because they judged the message good.

**They were attracted to it because it seemed strange and new**.

They brought Paul to a place where such matters were examined.

People who constantly pursue conversation about something new often become meddlesome.

They spend their precious time talking without accomplishing good.

Our time deserves wiser use, because how we employ it has consequences reaching into eternity.

## Paul proclaims the Creator and coming judgment (verses 22–31)

This sermon was addressed to **Gentile idolaters** who did not know the true God.

Its starting point therefore differed from Paul's preaching to Jews.

When speaking to Jews, Paul appealed to **prophecy and miracles** to lead them to recognize and trust the Redeemer.

When speaking to Gentile idolaters, he began with **God's ordinary works of creation and providence**, leading them to recognize and worship their Creator.

Paul had seen an altar inscribed **“TO THE UNKNOWN GOD.”**

Henry notes that other writers recorded this feature of Athenian religion.

Even after multiplying idols, the Athenians thought there might be another god they did not know.

How many people called Christians likewise practice religious ceremonies earnestly while not truly knowing **the God they claim to worship**!

Paul proclaimed glorious truths about the Lord he served and urged his hearers to serve.

God had been patient with idolatry for a long time.

But the period of such ignorance was ending: through his messengers, God now **commanded all people everywhere to repent**.

Different philosophical schools would find Paul's teaching challenging because it exposed the emptiness or error of their cherished beliefs.

## Some Athenians mock, others believe (verses 32–34)

Athenians treated Paul more politely in outward manner than people in some other towns.

Yet their contempt for his message and indifference toward it were no less serious.

The subject deserving the greatest attention often receives the least.

Those who mock must answer for their mockery.

God's word, however, never accomplishes nothing.

Some people will respond, join themselves to the Lord and continue listening to his faithful servants.

The certainty of future **judgment**, with **Christ as our Judge**, should move everyone to abandon sin and come to him.

Whatever starting point a gospel message uses, it must lead ultimately **to Christ and his authority**, because salvation and resurrection come from him and through him.
