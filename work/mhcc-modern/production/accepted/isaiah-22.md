# Isaiah 22 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The siege and taking of Jerusalem. | 1–7 |
| The wicked conduct of its inhabitants. | 8–14 |
| The displacing of Shebna, and the promotion of Eliakim, applied to the Messiah. | 15–25 |

## Jerusalem's terror and suffering (verses 1–7)

Why was Jerusalem overwhelmed with fear?

Some of the dead were not killed by swords but by **famine**; others may have died from terror or become helpless through discouragement.

The leaders fled but were overtaken.

God's servants who see approaching judgments and warn sinners about them feel genuine grief at the prospect.

Yet even the most dreadful horrors of a city captured by force are only a faint picture of **the terrors of the final day of wrath**.

## Defensive preparations without faith (verses 8–14)

Judah's weakness became more obvious than ever.

The people showed both **confidence in earthly strength** and **careless disregard for divine warning**.

They strengthened fortifications and secured the city's water supply.

These were practical measures, but they acted **without seeking God**, honoring him or depending upon his blessing.

Every created resource becomes useful only as the Lord makes it useful.

We should thank him for each gift and use it to serve him.

The people were contemptuous of divine justice even as God corrected them.

His purpose was to humble them and call them to repentance.

Instead they pursued pleasure and acted as though judgment and eternity did not matter.

Henry sees practical unbelief in **life after death** as a root of the selfish security and sensuality that ruin so many people.

Such disbelief dishonors God and rejects his appointed remedy.

Whether it expresses itself through presumptuous confidence or through despair, it can lead people to **willful destruction**.

## Shebna removed, Eliakim appointed (verses 15–25)

Isaiah's warning to **Shebna** exposed his pride, vanity and imagined security.

How empty is worldly greatness when death can so soon bring it to nothing!

What lasting difference does it make whether a person's body rests in an elaborate tomb or beneath ordinary grass?

Someone who uses power to drive other people about may justly find himself driven from his own position.

**Eliakim** would replace Shebna.

Those entrusted with authority should ask God for the grace to perform their responsibilities properly.

Eliakim's appointment also points beyond itself.

Jesus describes his own authority as Mediator through **the key of David** (**Revelation 3:7**).

He possesses complete authority over the kingdom of heaven and every affair belonging to it.

Human rulers should act as **fathers to the people under their care**.

It is more honorable to bring goodness to one's family through faith and service than to inherit famous names or titles.

Worldly prestige adds no true excellence to a person. It hangs upon someone like a decoration that can soon fall away.

Eliakim was compared with **a nail fixed securely in a wall**, on which his family could depend.

In Eastern houses, great nails or pegs were built into walls to hold household possessions.

**Jesus Christ is the secure nail**.

Whatever we entrust to him in faith cannot be finally lost.

He opens a door that no one can close and brings faithful believers, **body and soul**, into eternal glory.

But those who reject his salvation will discover that when he closes the door, no one can open it—whether they are excluded from heaven or confined under final judgment.
