## Disease strikes the livestock (verses 1–7)

God demanded Israel's release, but Pharaoh resisted.

The contest would show whose word would stand.

The Lord's hand struck the Egyptian livestock, and many animals of every kind died from a deadly disease.

Their owners suffered a heavy loss.

They had made Israel poor. Now God would make them poor.

Even the illness and death of animals are within God's providence.

Not even a sparrow falls without our heavenly Father's knowledge.

None of Israel's livestock was to die.

The Lord would make a distinction between his people and the Egyptians.

The Egyptians worshiped their cattle.

When we turn something into an idol, God is just to take it away.

Pharaoh was a proud tyrant and cruel oppressor, and the righteous Judge of all the earth was just in making him an example.

No one who receives a punishment he deserves has a just reason to complain.

Hardness of heart is a condition in which neither threats nor promises, neither judgments nor mercies, leave any lasting impression.

Conscience becomes numb, while pride and presumption fill the heart.

Such people persist in unbelief and disobedience.

This is what Scripture also calls a heart of stone.

How different it is from a heart of flesh—a broken and repentant heart!

Sinners have only themselves to blame for the pride and ungodliness with which they misuse God's generosity and patience.

However God hardens a human heart, Henry says, he does so as a punishment for earlier sins.

## The plague of boils (verses 8–12)

When the death of the livestock had no effect, God sent a plague that struck the Egyptians' own bodies.

If smaller judgments do not move people, greater ones may follow.

Sometimes God makes the punishment itself expose the sin.

The Egyptians had oppressed Israel in furnaces.

Now ashes from those furnaces became a source of terror to them.

The boils were a painful and severe affliction.

Even the magicians were struck.

Their power had already been restrained, but they had continued opposing Moses and strengthening Pharaoh in unbelief until they were forced to retreat.

Still Pharaoh remained obstinate.

He had hardened his own heart.

Now, as a righteous judgment, God gave him over to his own sinful desires, allowing Satan to blind and harden him further.

If people deliberately close their eyes against the light, it is just for God to close them.

Short of hell itself, this is the most dreadful judgment a person can suffer.

## God warns Egypt about the hail (verses 13–21)

God commanded Moses to bring Pharaoh a fearful warning.

In his providence, God had placed before Moses a man as fiercely stubborn as Pharaoh.

Every circumstance would make the result an especially striking display of God's ability to humble his proudest enemies.

When divine justice threatens destruction, divine mercy also shows a way of escape.

God distinguished not only between Israel and Egypt, but also between Egyptians who listened and those who refused.

If Pharaoh would not yield and prevent the judgment, people who accepted the warning could at least seek shelter.

Some believed God's words, feared him, and brought their servants and livestock under cover.

They acted wisely.

Even among Pharaoh's servants, some trembled at God's word.

Should not Israel tremble at it too?

Others refused to believe and left their animals outside.

Stubborn unbelief ignores even the clearest warnings and wisest advice.

Those who perish after such warnings bear responsibility for their own destruction.

## The hail falls (verses 22–35)

The hail caused terrible devastation.

It killed both people and livestock.

Crops already growing above the ground were destroyed, while those that had not yet emerged were spared.

Goshen was protected.

God can send rain or hail on one city and not another, either in mercy or in judgment.

Pharaoh humbled himself before Moses.

No one could have spoken better than he did.

He admitted that he was wrong and acknowledged that the Lord was righteous.

God must be acknowledged as just when he speaks, even when his voice comes in thunder and lightning.

Yet Pharaoh's heart remained hard.

Moses pleaded with God for him.

He had good reason to think Pharaoh would soon turn back from his apparent repentance, and he told Pharaoh so.

Still, Moses promised to intercede for him.

Moses left the city even though the hail and lightning kept Pharaoh and his servants indoors.

Peace with God makes a person unafraid even of thunder.

Pharaoh was terrified by the judgment.

But when it ended, he forgot his fine promises.

People who are changed by neither God's judgments nor his mercies usually become worse.
