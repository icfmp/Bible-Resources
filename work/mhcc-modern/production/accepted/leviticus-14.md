# Leviticus 14 — Modern English

## Declaring a person cleansed (verses 1–9)

The priests could not heal leprosy.

But after the Lord removed the affliction, they were responsible for following the appointed ceremonies so that the person could again join the worship and fellowship of God's people.

The ceremonies illustrated the duties of people who truly repent and the responsibilities of ministers toward them.

Applied to the spiritual leprosy of sin, they teach us that when we withdraw from people who live disorderly lives, we must not treat them as enemies but warn them as brothers and sisters.

When God brings such people to repentance, we should welcome them back with tenderness, gladness and genuine love.

We must neither encourage people to remain in sin nor discourage those who repent.

When the priest found that a person had been healed, he formally declared that person clean through the prescribed ceremonies.

One bird was killed. A second was dipped in the blood of the first and then released.

Henry suggests this may picture Christ shedding his blood for sinners, then rising and ascending into heaven.

After being declared free from the affliction, the restored person still had to remove every trace of it.

Likewise, those who have the comfort of forgiveness should carefully turn away from their sins.

Everyone who possesses this hope seeks to become pure.

## Offerings for a restored person (verses 10–32)

The restored person was to appear before the Lord with the appointed offerings.

When God enables us to return to public worship after sickness, distance or other obstacles, we should show gratitude by using that opportunity diligently.

Both we and our offerings must be brought before God by the Priest who cleanses us—our Lord Jesus Christ.

Alongside the ordinary rites of the guilt offering, blood and oil were placed on the person being cleansed.

Where Christ's blood is applied to bring justification, the oil of the Holy Spirit is also applied for sanctification.

These two blessings cannot be separated.

The law made gracious provision for the poor.

Poor people were as welcome at God's altar as the rich.

They could bring less costly sacrifices, but the essential ceremony was the same for everyone.

Their souls were equally precious, and the same Christ and gospel were needed by both.

Even the poorest had to bring one lamb.

No sinner could have been saved without the Lamb who was slain and redeemed us to God through his blood.

## Suspected leprosy in a house (verses 33–53)

Leprosy in a house, like leprosy in a garment, is difficult for us to understand.

But where sin rules a household, it becomes a plague there just as surely as it does in an individual heart.

Heads of households should watch for the first signs of sin in their families and remove it, whatever form it takes.

If an affected section of a house was discovered, it had to be removed.

If the affliction remained, the entire building was to be pulled down.

It was better to lose a home than to continue living in one that remained contaminated.

The leprosy of sin can ruin both families and churches.

Sin is so deeply woven into our mortal bodies that those bodies must finally be brought down by death.

## Concluding instruction on the law of leprosy (verses 54–57)

God is rich in mercy.

Because of his great love, even while we were dead in sins, he made us alive by his grace (Ephesians 2:4–5).

When he does this, we show the change by repenting and abandoning our former sins.

Let us pursue holiness.

Let us also show compassion toward other poor sufferers, sincerely desiring, seeking and praying for their cleansing.
