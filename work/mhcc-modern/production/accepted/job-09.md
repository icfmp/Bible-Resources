# Job 9 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Job acknowledges God's justice. | 1–13 |
| He is not able to contend with God. | 14–21 |
| Men not to be judged by outward condition. | 22–24 |
| Job complains of troubles. | 25–35 |

## Job acknowledges God's righteousness (verses 1–13)

Job answered his friends by saying that his denial of hypocrisy did not mean he questioned God's justice.

How could any person claim to be righteous in a dispute with God?

Before him Job admitted countless sins.

If God called him to account for every thought, word and action in his life, he could not justify **one in a thousand**.

He therefore deserved more suffering than he had experienced.

When Job thought of God's wisdom and power, his complaints temporarily disappeared.

We are not qualified to judge all God's ways, because we do not know everything he is doing or intends.

God acts with power no creature can resist.

Those who believe themselves strong enough to rescue others cannot even rescue themselves when God confronts them.

## Job cannot contend with God (verses 14–21)

Nevertheless, Job remained too righteous in his own eyes (**Job 32:1**).

Though he spoke of God's greatness, his words began to treat his dispute with Providence as a matter of **power rather than right**.

The evil fruits of pride and self-righteousness were emerging.

Job was beginning to condemn God in order to justify himself, for which he would later be corrected.

Even then, he understood enough about himself to know he could not endure a trial before God.

If we claim to have no sin, we deceive ourselves and offend God.

We contradict Scripture and sin by making the claim.

Yet Job's assertion that his troubles were entirely without cause failed to give due weight to God's goodness and justice.

## Outward suffering does not prove wickedness (verses 22–24)

Here Job addressed the central disagreement.

His friends believed good people **always prosper in this life** and only wicked people experience severe suffering.

Job answered that wicked people may prosper while righteous people suffer greatly.

He was right about the facts.

Yet his words expressed too much anger, because God does not willingly afflict people.

When dispute or discontent heats our spirits, we need to **guard our lips**.

## Job laments and longs for a mediator (verses 25–35)

How little we need empty entertainments, and how greatly we need to use time well, when it rushes so quickly toward eternity!

Earthly pleasures are vain and can vanish even while we remain alive.

The memory of fulfilling our duty can bring lasting satisfaction.

The memory of having accumulated wealth cannot, once all the wealth has disappeared.

Job spoke as though God would never be appeased or moved toward mercy.

That was the language of his remaining sinful nature.

But **there is a Mediator**, a Daysman, an Umpire between us and God.

He is **God's beloved Son**.

Through the blood of his cross he has purchased peace.

He saves completely everyone who comes to God through him.

When we trust his name, our sins are buried in the depths of the sea.

We are washed from our pollution, made whiter than snow and freed from condemnation.

We are clothed with righteousness and salvation, adorned with the Holy Spirit's graces and presented faultless before God's glory with great joy.

May we learn the difference between **justifying ourselves** and **being justified by God**.

Let every suffering, storm-tossed soul remember Job.

Others have passed through such a dreadful place.

Although they found it hard to believe that God would hear or deliver them, he calmed their storms and brought them to safety.

Resist the devil.

Do not indulge bitter thoughts about God or despairing conclusions about yourself.

Come to the One who invites the weary and burdened and promises never to turn them away.
