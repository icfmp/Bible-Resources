# Luke 16 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The parable of the unjust steward. | 1–12 |
| Christ reproves the hypocrisy of the covetous Pharisees. | 13–18 |
| The rich man and Lazarus. | 19–31 |

## The dishonest manager (verses 1–12)

Everything we possess **belongs to God**.

We have only been entrusted with its use, according to his instructions and for his honor.

The manager in Jesus' parable wasted his master's goods.

We can all be charged with failing to use what God has entrusted to us as well as we should.

We will eventually be called to give an account and leave our stewardship behind.

This reminds us that **death will end the opportunities we now possess**.

The dishonest manager reduced the debts owed by his master's tenants to secure friends for himself.

The master praised **the manager's foresight, not his dishonesty**.

The parable never commends fraud or justifies cheating an employer.

It shows that people devoted to worldly goals often act with greater calculation, energy and perseverance than people who seek spiritual goals.

Worldly people choose an unworthy ultimate object, but they can teach us by the determination with which they pursue it.

How much more diligently should the children of light pursue **eternal good**!

True riches are **spiritual blessings**.

If someone squanders on self-indulgence or hoards worldly wealth entrusted by God, what evidence is there that the person is an heir of God through Christ?

Earthly riches are insecure and deceptive.

Those who are rich in faith, rich toward God and rich in Christ and his promises are **truly wealthy**.

Let us store treasure in heaven and look there for our lasting inheritance.

## God cannot be served alongside wealth (verses 13–18)

Jesus added a solemn warning: **no one can serve God and the world as rival masters**.

Their interests pull in opposite directions.

The greedy Pharisees mocked his teaching.

But their defense of the law distorted its real meaning, as Jesus illustrated by speaking about divorce.

Many people cling fiercely to outward religious forms while loving wealth and opposing the **power of genuine godliness**.

Such people even try to stir others against the truth.

## The rich man and Lazarus (verses 19–31)

Jesus contrasted the condition of a righteous and an unrighteous person in **this life and the next**.

Scripture does not say the rich man gained his wealth by fraud or oppression.

Its warning is more serious: someone can enjoy great wealth, luxury and pleasure and still perish under divine judgment.

The rich man's sin was **using everything for himself**, without concern for Lazarus.

Lazarus was godly though deeply poor and afflicted.

Even some of God's dearest servants experience enormous hardship in this world.

We are not told that the rich man actively hurt him.

But he did **nothing to care for him**.

Death revealed the immense difference between them.

The rich man lifted his eyes in torment.

Henry says it is **unlikely that actual conversations occur between glorified saints and condemned sinners**.

The dialogue instead illustrates the hopeless misery, futile desires and unrelieved suffering of those finally condemned.

Those who now despise God's people may one day long for their kindness.

But there will be no relief from final judgment.

Sinners are warned **now** to remember their spiritual condition, though many deliberately avoid thinking about it.

Unrepentant people may enjoy their best things only during earthly life, while believers may endure their greatest troubles only here.

The blessings of heaven will never leave the redeemed; the consequences of final condemnation will never end.

In the **present life** there is still no impassable gulf between sin and grace.

By God's mercy a person can repent and turn to him.

But death in sin fixes the sinner's condition.

The rich man had **five brothers** and wished to prevent them from joining him.

He had helped encourage their sinful path; their arrival would only increase his misery.

How many people would wish after death to undo the things they wrote, said or did!

Henry explicitly warns against using the rich man's plea to **Abraham** as evidence that Christians should pray to departed saints.

It is the desperate request of a condemned person, and the request was refused.

A visitor from the dead could not deliver a better message than **Scripture** already gives.

The corruption that rejects God's written word would also reject supernatural warnings.

Let us consult **the law and the testimony** (**Isaiah 8:19–20**), the reliable prophetic word (**2 Peter 1:19**).

No warning or argument produces true repentance without **God's regenerating grace** renewing the heart.
