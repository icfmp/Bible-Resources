## Grain and drink offerings; one law for foreigners (verses 1–21)

God gave detailed instructions about the grain and drink offerings accompanying sacrifices.

The opening words were encouraging: **when** Israel came into the land that the Lord would give them.

This assured the people that the promised land would still be given to their descendants.

Because the offerings were considered the food of God's table, bread, oil and wine were to accompany the sacrifices of animals.

These rules fixed the appropriate proportions for each kind of offering.

They applied equally to native Israelites and foreigners living among them.

That equality anticipated the calling of the Gentiles and their acceptance into God's church.

If the Mosaic law made so little distinction between Jew and Gentile in these matters, the gospel breaks down the dividing wall even more completely and reconciles both to God.

## Offerings for sins committed unknowingly (verses 22–29)

Ignorance may lessen a person's guilt in some respects, but it cannot justify people who could have learned God's will and failed to do so.

David prayed to be cleansed from his hidden faults, including sins he had not recognized.

Sins committed through ignorance can be forgiven through Christ, the great Sacrifice.

When he offered himself once for all on the cross, he prayed, “Father, forgive them; they do not know what they are doing.”

That prayer revealed part of the saving purpose of his offering.

It is also a gracious sign of God's favor toward Gentiles that the law explicitly included foreigners among those who could receive atonement for unintended sins.

## Deliberate sin and the Sabbath-breaker (verses 30–36)

People sin presumptuously when they deliberately act against God's known will and glory.

Such sins are especially evil.

The deliberate offender insults the Lord and despises his word.

Presumptuous sinners behave as though they are too important, too good or too wise to accept its rule.

The account of the Sabbath-breaker is a particular example.

He gathered wood on the Sabbath to make a fire, though the people had been instructed to prepare their baking and cooking the previous day (Exodus 16:23).

His action was therefore a deliberate affront to both God's law and the One who gave it.

God is jealous for the honor of his Sabbath.

He will not overlook its contemptuous profanation, whatever people may do.

Henry sees the severe penalty under Israel's law as a warning that God's judgments are consistent with perfect justice and love.

God has a right to a day devoted to himself.

People deny that right when they listen to their pride and unbelief rather than to the Spirit of truth.

Henry then challenges Christians who turn their backs on the blessings of Sabbath worship in order to accumulate wealth or pursue sinful pleasures.

They may gain money or excitement, but they will also receive the dreadful consequences of their disobedience.

Sinful pursuits finally lead to ruin.

## Tassels as reminders of God's commands (verses 37–41)

The Lord instructed Israel to put fringes or tassels on the borders of their clothes.

Their dress, like their diet, would distinguish them from surrounding nations.

They were taught not to conform to the pagan ways of those nations in other matters.

Wherever they went, they visibly acknowledged that they belonged to God and were not ashamed of his law.

The fringes were not intended mainly as decoration.

Their purpose was to stir memory and attention (2 Peter 3:1).

When temptation came, seeing them should remind an Israelite not to break God's commandments.

We should likewise use every good means to keep Scripture's truth and commands in our thoughts.

Such reminders strengthen obedience and prepare the mind to resist temptation.

We are to be holy to our God: cleansed from sin and sincerely devoted to his service.

The repeated reason for every command remains the same: **he is the Lord our God**.
