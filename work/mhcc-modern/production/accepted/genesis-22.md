# Genesis 22 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God commands Abraham to offer Isaac. | 1–2 |
| Abraham obeys in faith. | 3–10 |
| God provides another sacrifice instead of Isaac. | 11–14 |
| God renews the covenant with Abraham. | 15–19 |
| Nahor's family. | 20–24 |

## God tests Abraham (verses 1–2)

We are never beyond the reach of trials.

In Hebrew, the same word can express *tempting*, *testing*, or *proving*.

Every trial tests what is in the heart, showing whether its desires are holy or sinful.

But God tested Abraham without intending to draw him into sin, as Satan does when he tempts.

Strong faith is often tested by great difficulties and called to hard service.

The command to offer Isaac was expressed in words that made the trial more painful.

Every word was like a sword.

**First, consider whom he was to offer.**

“Take your son”—not your cattle or your sheep. How willingly Abraham would have given them all to save Isaac!

“Your son”—not a servant.

“Your only son”—the only son born to Sarah.

“Isaac”—the very son Abraham loved.

**Second, consider where he was to offer him.**

The place was a three-day journey away.

Abraham would have time to think about what he was doing and obey deliberately.

**Third, consider how he was to offer him.**

He was to present Isaac as a burnt offering.

It was not merely a matter of killing his son, his beloved Isaac, but doing so as a sacrifice, with all the solemn ceremonies he normally used when offering a burnt offering.

## Abraham obeys in faith (verses 3–10)

Never was gold tested in a hotter fire.

Who other than Abraham would not have argued with God?

A weaker heart would certainly have raised objections.

But Abraham knew the One with whom he had to deal: the Lord himself.

Faith had taught him to obey rather than argue.

He was certain that whatever God commanded was good and that whatever God promised could not fail.

Anyone who consults merely human feelings and reasoning in a matter of obedience to God will never give up their Isaac for him.

The godly patriarch rose early and began his painful journey.

Then he traveled for three whole days with Isaac still in his sight.

Suffering becomes harder when it continues for a long time.

Abraham told his servants, “We will come back to you.”

This shows that he expected Isaac to return with him, even if God had to raise him from the dead.

Then Isaac asked a deeply moving question.

“My father,” he said.

Those words alone must have touched Abraham's heart more deeply than his knife could have pierced Isaac.

Yet Abraham waited for his son's question.

Then, without intending to do so, Abraham spoke a prophecy: “My son, God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering.”

Through his words, the Holy Spirit seems to point toward the Lamb of God, whom God would provide to take away the sin of the world.

Abraham arranged the wood as though preparing Isaac's funeral pyre.

Now he had to tell his son the astonishing truth: Isaac himself was the lamb appointed for the sacrifice.

No doubt he comforted Isaac with the same hopes of God's faithfulness that had strengthened his own heart.

Isaac had to be bound.

The great Sacrifice who would come in the fullness of time would also be bound.

When Isaac was bound, Abraham took the knife and reached out to strike the fatal blow.

This act of faith and obedience deserves to be witnessed by God, angels, and humanity.

Sometimes God's providence calls us to part with our own Isaac—whatever we love most.

When that happens, we must submit cheerfully to his holy will (1 Samuel 3:18).

## God provides a sacrifice instead of Isaac (verses 11–14)

God had never intended Isaac actually to be sacrificed.

Yet in due time blood more precious than that of animals would be shed for sin: the blood of God's only Son.

Meanwhile, God would not permit human sacrifices to be offered.

Another sacrifice was provided.

We must see here a reference to the promised Messiah, the blessed Seed.

Christ was offered in our place, just as the ram was offered in Isaac's place.

Through Christ's death, our debt was discharged.

Notice also that the temple, where sacrifices were later offered, was built on this same Mount Moriah.

Calvary, where Christ was crucified, was nearby.

Abraham gave the place a new name to encourage believers in every age to trust and obey God with joy.

*Jehovah-jireh*: “The LORD will provide.”

The name probably alluded to Abraham's earlier words: “God will provide himself a lamb.”

The Lord always keeps his eye on his people in times of trouble and distress, ready to give them help when they need it.

## God renews his promise to Abraham (verses 15–19)

In confirming his covenant, God declared his favor toward Abraham more gloriously than ever before.

Those willing to surrender anything for God will find that he makes up their loss with blessings beyond description.

The promise in verse 18 undoubtedly points to the Messiah and the grace of the gospel.

Here we see the loving-kindness of God our Savior toward sinful humanity.

He did not withhold his own Son—his only Son—from us.

And here we see Christ's love: he freely gave himself as the sacrifice for our sins.

Yet he lives!

He calls sinners to come to him and share in the salvation he purchased with his blood.

He calls those he has redeemed to rejoice in him and glorify him.

How can we repay him for all his benefits?

Let his love move us to live not for ourselves, but for the One who died for us and rose again.

Let us admire and worship his grace, giving everything to the service of the Savior who laid down his life for us.

Whatever we love most dearly on earth is our Isaac.

The only way to find true comfort in something earthly is to place it by faith into God's hands.

Yet remember: Abraham was **not justified by his willingness to obey**.

He was justified through the infinitely greater obedience of Jesus Christ.

Faith received that obedience, rested upon it, and rejoiced in it.

And that same faith prepared Abraham and enabled him to show such extraordinary self-denial and obedience.

## Nahor's family (verses 20–24)

The chapter ends with a brief account of Nahor's family, who had settled in Haran.

This genealogy is included because of its connection with God's church.

Both Isaac and Jacob would later take wives from that family, so the list appears before those events are described.

Abraham enjoyed extraordinary privileges, had been brought into God's covenant, and had received the assurance of its promises.

Yet he did not look down upon his relatives.

He was glad to hear that their families were growing and doing well.

## Chapter-level editorial audit

Compared with the [original Concise Genesis 22 text](https://ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhcc/mhcc.ii.xxii.html) in a separate reading pass. `MHCC 0.1.1` remains the pinned primary derivative source.

- **1–2:** testing/temptation same Hebrew term, God unlike Satan, strong faith/severe trials, every swordlike element of the command (son, only son of Sarah, loved Isaac), three-day deliberation and form of burnt offering—all retained.
- **3–10:** not arguing, early departure, three-day pain, Abraham's expectation of resurrection through “we,” moving address “My father,” unintentional prophecy of Lamb of God, Isaac as lamb, shared comfort, binding of Isaac and Christ, 1 Samuel 3:18—all retained.
- **11–14:** God's never intending actual child sacrifice, refusal of human sacrifices, Christ's greater blood and substitution, Moriah and nearby Calvary, name *Jehovah-jireh*, its *probable* earlier verbal allusion and timely help—all retained.
- **15–19:** covenant's greater promises, verse 18 pointing to Messiah, Father not withholding Son, Christ's blood-bought living invitation, devotion, earthly “Isaac” surrender, **justification by Christ's obedience and NOT by Abraham's obedient works**—retained.
- **20–24:** Nahor's genealogy, wives of Isaac and Jacob, the author's reason for recording it, Abraham's continued affection for his less-privileged relatives—all retained.

The five original authorial exposition blocks and outline are retained; the two separately indexed Genesis 22:1 and 22:2 keys share one original exposition unit.
