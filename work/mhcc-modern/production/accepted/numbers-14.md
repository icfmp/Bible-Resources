# Numbers 14 — Modern English

## The people refuse to trust God's promise (verses 1–4)

People who refuse to trust God continually trouble themselves.

The grief produced by worldly unbelief brings death.

Israel complained against Moses and Aaron, but in criticizing them the people were really rebuking the Lord.

They looked back at Egypt with unreasonable discontent.

Uncontrolled fear and anger can make people foolishly throw away even what they ought to treasure most: life itself.

They said they would rather die as guilty people under God's judgment than live as victorious people enjoying his favor.

Finally, they decided to return to Egypt rather than enter Canaan.

People who abandon God's counsel are choosing their own ruin.

Could Israel expect the cloud to guide them back or manna to accompany them?

Even if entering Canaan were as difficult as they feared, returning to Egypt would have been harder.

We often complain about our circumstances and wish to change them.

But there is no place or condition on earth where a determinedly dissatisfied person cannot find something to resent.

The better way to improve our situation is to cultivate a better spirit.

Turning from God leads inevitably toward disaster.

## Joshua and Caleb urge the people to trust (verses 5–10)

Moses and Aaron were astonished that Israel could throw away its greatest blessings.

Joshua and Caleb assured the people that Canaan was good and worth entering.

They did not treat its conquest as an impossible problem.

If people understood how desirable the blessings of faith are, they would not shrink from the duties that come with it.

The Canaanites lived in strongly fortified towns, but their **protection had departed from them**.

The other scouts noticed the Canaanites' military power.

Joshua and Caleb recognized the Canaanites' wickedness and the judgment awaiting them.

No people can remain safe after provoking God to abandon them.

Though Israel lived in tents, God's presence was its defense.

With him, the most powerful enemy need not terrify us.

Sinners bring ruin on themselves through rebellion.

But people who, like Caleb and Joshua, stand faithfully for God may trust his special protection.

He will shelter them from human hostility either during this life or in heaven.

## Moses intercedes after God's warning (verses 11–19)

Moses humbly prayed for Israel.

In this he pictured Jesus Christ, who prayed for those who treated him cruelly.

The pardon Moses sought was the removal of the nation's threatened punishment.

He pleaded earnestly that God's abundant mercy was consistent with forgiving them.

## The unbelieving generation is excluded (verses 20–35)

The Lord answered Moses' prayer by not destroying the entire congregation immediately.

Yet refusal to believe God's promise excluded that generation from enjoying its fulfillment.

Those who despised the pleasant land would not enter it.

God would keep his promise to their children.

The people had said they would rather die in the wilderness.

Their sin became their ruin: God allowed their own words to be fulfilled, and their bodies fell in the desert.

They suffered under the heavy burden of their own wrongdoing.

The expression about God's broken promise indicated both **the cause** and **the consequence** of their loss.

The cause was their unbelief: God does not abandon people until they have first turned from him.

The consequence was their destruction.

But the children younger than twenty—whom their parents had feared would be captured—would be brought safely into Canaan.

In this way God distinguished between guilty adults and their children, judging the former without destroying the latter.

He did not utterly remove his covenant kindness.

## The faithless spies die (verses 36–39)

The ten spies who had brought an evil report about Canaan died suddenly.

They had slandered the land God promised.

People provoke God severely when they misrepresent true religion or make others dislike it.

The complainers now became mourners.

Had they grieved over their sin while they were being warned, the sentence might have been prevented.

But their sorrow was chiefly over the punishment, so it did not help them.

There is a similar grief in hell, where tears cannot end the fire or relieve the suffering.

## Israel attacks without God's blessing (verses 40–45)

Some Israelites now became eager to march into Canaan.

But the right time had passed.

How much better it would be if people sought heaven earnestly while the time for receiving God's grace remains, instead of waiting until it is too late!

An action that was our duty at the proper time may become sinful when done in defiance of God's later command.

People who abandon their duty cannot claim God's protection.

Israel refused to move when the Lord commanded it, then insisted on moving when he forbade it.

This is the hostility of a sinful mind against God.

Earlier the people distrusted God's power.

Now they presumed upon their own strength without him.

Their expedition therefore failed.

The judgment that their bodies would fall in the wilderness had begun.

Nothing founded upon sin can end well.

To enjoy peace with friends and success against enemies, we need the Lord's friendship and must remain in his love.

Let Israel's history warn us against dying through the same unbelief.

Let us go forward trusting God's mercy, power, promise and truth, confident that he will bring our souls to everlasting rest.
