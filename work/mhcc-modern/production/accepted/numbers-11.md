# Numbers 11 — Modern English

## Fire breaks out at Taberah (verses 1–3)

The people complained.

This shows how sin can twist even something good into a reason for rebelling against God.

The law exposes sin but cannot conquer it.

Discontented people will always find something to complain about, however favorable their circumstances may be.

The Lord heard the murmuring, even when Moses did not.

He knows every secret resentment and complaint hidden in the heart.

God was displeased, and he sent a fire of judgment against the people.

They had allowed their anger against him to burn within them.

Now his just anger burned against their bodies.

Yet his judgment came gradually, leaving time for warning and repentance.

God does not delight in punishment.

Even after judgment begins, he is willing to stop when his people turn to him.

## The people crave meat and reject manna (verses 4–9)

Human beings have lost their true place of rest and may remain dissatisfied even in prosperity.

Israel became tired of manna, though it was wholesome and nourishing food.

It cost nothing.

Gathering it took little labor.

Yet the people remembered the fish and other foods they had eaten in Egypt as though those meals had been free.

They forgot the crushing slavery through which they had paid for them.

While they ate manna, they were almost spared the burden of earning bread by the sweat of their faces.

Still they complained about God's provision.

A complaining spirit finds fault even with good gifts.

People who could be happy make themselves miserable through discontent.

They insisted they would not be satisfied unless they had meat.

A desire for bodily pleasure has become dangerous when it rules a person's choices.

We should not encourage any desire that we cannot sincerely turn into prayer.

Something lawful in itself may become sinful when God has not given it to us and we demand it out of uncontrolled appetite.

## Moses complains under the burden of leadership (verses 10–15)

The people had provoked Moses terribly.

But his own response was not as fitting as it ought to have been.

He undervalued the honor God had given him.

He exaggerated the burden of his own work, even though divine wisdom guided him and almighty power could reward or correct the people.

His words showed distrust of God's grace.

If the work had been lighter, Moses could not have fulfilled it by his own strength.

If it had been heavier, he could still have done it through God's help.

Let us ask the Lord not to lead us into temptation.

## Seventy elders are appointed; meat is promised (verses 16–23)

Moses was to choose people already recognized as wise and experienced elders.

God would equip them for the work.

If they were not already capable, he would make them capable.

God also promised meat to the discontented people, removing every excuse for further complaint.

Henry draws two lessons.

**First, bodily pleasures are empty.** They can overwhelm us through excess but cannot truly satisfy us.

Only spiritual pleasures satisfy and endure.

The world and its desires pass away.

**Second, gluttony and drunkenness are degrading sins.** They turn food and drink, which should nourish and strengthen the body, into causes of harm.

Moses questioned how God could provide such enormous quantities of meat.

Even great and genuine believers may find it difficult to trust God when visible circumstances seem hopeless.

God reminded Moses that the Lord is almighty.

He would soon see whether God's word came true.

When God speaks, he can accomplish what he promises.

## The Spirit rests upon the elders (verses 24–30)

God fulfilled his promise to help Moses govern Israel.

He put his Spirit upon the seventy elders.

They spoke to the people about the things of God in a way that made his presence recognizable.

Two elders, **Eldad and Medad**, had not gone to the tabernacle.

Henry understands this as a sign that they felt unworthy and inadequate.

Nevertheless, the Holy Spirit met them in the camp, where they exercised gifts of prayer, preaching and praise.

The Spirit of God is not restricted to the tabernacle.

He moves where he wills, like the wind.

God exalts those who humble themselves.

People most capable of leadership are often those who are least ambitious for it.

Joshua wanted Eldad and Medad restrained.

He was not asking for punishment but feared their ministry might divide the people or rival Moses.

He acted out of concern for the unity of the community.

Moses, however, did not fear such consequences from the Spirit's genuine work.

We should not silence people whom Christ has acknowledged or prevent them from doing good simply because they disagree with us on some matters.

Moses longed for all God's people to become prophets and receive his Spirit.

Those seeking power should remember Moses' testimony: leadership is a burden.

It brings anxiety and responsibility to people who take their duty seriously.

For those who neglect it, the final accounting will be heavier still.

Leaders should welcome useful counsel and assistance rather than despising it.

If all God's people were enabled by Christ's Spirit to minister, there would still be enough work for everyone in calling sinners to repentance and faith, even if outward disagreements remained.

## Quail are given and judgment follows (verses 31–35)

God gave Israel meat, as promised.

People are often much quicker to gather perishable food than to seek the food that lasts to eternal life.

We devote enormous energy to temporary advantages while neglecting our souls.

People ruled by fleshly desires may get exactly what they demand—even when fulfilling those desires ruins them.

Israel's feast proved costly.

God sometimes grants sinners' requests in judgment while denying his own people's desires in love.

What we crave improperly may become a source of grief and suffering if we obtain it.

Many people shorten their lives through excess.

Let us seek joys that truly satisfy, never sicken us through indulgence, and remain forever.
