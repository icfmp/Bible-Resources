# Genesis 13 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Abram returns from Egypt with great wealth. | 1–4 |
| Conflict between Abram's and Lot's herdsmen; Abram gives Lot the choice of land. | 5–9 |
| Lot chooses the region of Sodom. | 10–13 |
| God renews his promise to Abram, who moves to Hebron. | 14–18 |

## Abram returns to Bethel (verses 1–4)

Abram was exceedingly rich.

The Hebrew expression means that he was *very heavy* with wealth. Riches can indeed become a heavy burden.

Those who are determined to become rich load themselves down with what Scripture calls thick clay (Habakkuk 2:6).

Getting wealth brings the burden of anxiety; keeping it brings fear; using it brings temptation; misusing it brings guilt; losing it brings sorrow.

And at the end, we must give an account of what we have done with it.

Yet God's providence sometimes makes godly people wealthy.

God's blessing made Abram rich without bringing sorrow with it (Proverbs 10:22).

It is difficult for a rich person to enter heaven. But in some cases it is possible (Mark 10:23–24).

When outward prosperity is used wisely, it can adorn a godly life and give us more opportunities to do good.

Abram returned to Bethel.

His former altar was gone, so he could not offer sacrifices there. But he still called on the name of the Lord.

You are as likely to find a living person who never breathes as a true child of God who never prays.

## Abram makes peace with Lot (verses 5–9)

Riches provide endless occasions for quarrels. They are among the things people fight about most often.

They also stir up the spirit of conflict itself by making people proud and greedy.

“Mine” and “yours” have caused countless disputes.

Poverty, work, deprivation, and wandering had not separated Abram from Lot. But wealth did.

Bad servants can cause tremendous trouble in families and among neighbors through pride, anger, lies, slander, and gossip.

The quarrel was especially damaging because the Canaanites and Perizzites lived in the land.

When those who profess faith quarrel, they bring disgrace on religion and give God's enemies an excuse to blaspheme.

It is best to preserve peace before it is broken.

But once a quarrel has begun, the next best thing is to put out the fire as quickly as possible.

Abram took the initiative, although he was the older man and held the greater position.

His calm spirit enabled him to control his anger and turn away wrath with a gentle answer.

Those who want peace must never answer insults with more insults.

Abram also showed humility. He was willing to plead for peace even with someone in a lower position than himself.

Whatever others may choose, God's people must be people of peace.

Abram gave a powerful reason for reconciliation: the people of the world may fight over trifles, but we who know better things and hope for a better country should not quarrel.

Christians should be especially careful to avoid disputes.

Many people claim to love peace but do nothing to make it happen. Abram was different.

If God himself stoops to plead with us to be reconciled to him, surely we can plead with one another.

Although God had promised the land to Abram's descendants, Abram offered Lot an equal or even better portion.

Lot did not have the same claim, but Abram would not use God's promise as an excuse to treat a relative harshly.

It is noble to give way for the sake of peace.

## Lot chooses the land near Sodom (verses 10–13)

Abram gave Lot the choice, and Lot immediately accepted it.

Selfishness and strong desire often make people discourteous.

Lot looked at the land's fertility and assumed he would surely prosper in such a fruitful place.

But how did his choice turn out?

When people choose their relationships, work, homes, or places to settle mainly according to bodily desire, the attraction of what they see, or pride, they cannot expect God's presence and blessing.

They are often disappointed even in the thing they most wanted.

One principle should govern every choice: **what is best for our souls is best for us**.

Lot gave little thought to the character of the people who lived nearby.

Sodom's inhabitants were bold and unashamed in their sin.

The city's wickedness included pride, abundance of food, and an excess of idleness (Ezekiel 16:49).

God often allows great plenty to belong to great sinners.

It has frequently been the painful experience of godly people to live among wicked neighbors.

That pain is even greater when, as with Lot, they have brought the situation upon themselves through a poor choice.

## God renews his promise to Abram (verses 14–18)

Those whose spirits are calm, rather than disturbed by angry passions, are best prepared to receive visits of God's grace.

God more than makes up for the outward advantages we give up to preserve peace with our neighbors by giving us spiritual peace.

Even when our relatives separate from us, God does not leave us.

Notice how God now comforted and enriched Abram through his promises.

He promised two things: **a good land and many descendants to enjoy it**.

What faith sees is richer and more beautiful than anything our eyes can see around us.

God told Abram to walk throughout the land.

He was not to imagine that he could settle there permanently. Rather, he would remain a pilgrim, moving toward a better Canaan.

Abram built an altar as a sign of his thankfulness.

When God meets us with gracious promises, he expects us to respond with humble praise.

When outward circumstances are difficult, believers find great benefit in meditating on the glorious inheritance the Lord has prepared for them at the end.
