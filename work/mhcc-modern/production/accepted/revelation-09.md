# Revelation 9 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The fifth trumpet is followed by a representation of another star as falling from heaven and opening the bottomless pit, out of which come swarms of locusts. | 1–12 |
| The sixth trumpet is followed by the loosing of four angels bound in the great river Euphrates. | 13–21 |

## The fifth trumpet: smoke and locusts (verses 1–12)

At the **fifth trumpet**, a star fell from heaven.

Henry interprets the fallen star as someone who **ceased to be a servant of Christ and became a servant of the devil**, releasing destructive spiritual influences against the churches.

The abyss opened and a great cloud of smoke rose from it.

The devil advances his schemes by blinding people's eyes, extinguishing light and knowledge, and spreading **ignorance and error**.

A swarm of **locusts** emerged.

Henry sees them as pictures of Satan's agents who promote **superstition, idolatry, error and cruelty**.

The grass and trees symbolize true believers, whether young in faith or more mature, whom the locusts are not permitted to harm.

Others would experience a hidden poison in their souls, first losing spiritual purity and later losing their peace.

The locusts had no authority over those marked with **God's seal**.

The Lord's powerful and discriminating grace preserves his people from **total and final apostasy**.

The torment would be extremely severe but limited to a comparatively short period.

Even faithful believers may share the outward sufferings experienced by their society, while being protected from the spiritual corruption of error.

Henry sees such heresies as a means of **testing and revealing genuine Christians**, citing **1 Corinthians 11:19**.

He also says early Christian writers connected the image with **an early, widespread invasion of corrupting teachers**.

This is his account of the image's possible historical reference.

## The sixth trumpet: destruction and stubborn impenitence (verses 13–21)

At the **sixth trumpet**, four angels bound at the Euphrates were released.

Henry suggests that **the power of the Turks** is the subject of this vision.

He understands their period of dominance as limited by God and connects their military violence with what he calls a destructive false religion.

This is **Henry's historical and polemical interpretation**, not an identification established by the text of Revelation or a judgment about individual people today.

The people described as opposing Christ did not repent despite these terrible events.

One enemy of the church may become an instrument of divine judgment upon another.

Henry connects this with **idolatry and corruption among professing Christians**, including what remained of the churches of the East.

The historical developments grieved him, but he believed a careful reading of Scripture and history could strengthen Christians' faith and hope as they contemplated Providence.

The final warning concerns people who escape one judgment without repenting.

They continue in **idolatry, injustice and cruelty**, until divine judgment finally overtakes them.
