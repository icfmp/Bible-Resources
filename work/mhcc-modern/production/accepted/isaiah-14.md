# Isaiah 14 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The destruction of Babylon, and the death of its proud monarch. | 1–23 |
| Assurance of the destruction of Assyria. | 24–27 |
| The destruction of the Philistines. | 28–32 |

## Babylon's downfall and the humiliation of its king (verses 1–23)

God governs the whole course of Providence with the good of his people in view.

Settlement in the promised land is a gift of his mercy.

Let the church welcome those whom **God himself receives**.

Wherever believers live, they should recommend true faith through upright, winsome conversation and conduct.

Those who formerly opposed them may eventually be humbled and brought to obedience—a pattern that can also be seen in the gospel's progress.

The Lord himself brings about this blessed change.

His people will find rest from sorrow, burdens and fear of further suffering.

Babylon possessed vast riches. Its king used his control of wealth to dominate other nations.

He had especially oppressed the Jews, increasing the guilt that would eventually bring judgment upon him.

**Tyrants sacrifice their true interests to their passions and desires.**

It is right to aspire to be like **the Most Holy**, because God commands us to be holy as he is holy.

But it is sinful to aspire to be like **the Most High**, demanding his supreme position for ourselves.

Satan used that ambition to tempt our first parents.

The proud monarch would be brought completely low.

Those who refuse to stop sinning may be stopped by God's judgment.

He would die and go down to the grave—the fate that ultimately overtakes even the greatest tyrants.

**Genuine glory, meaning grace, accompanies the soul to heaven**.

Earthly splendor descends with the body into the grave and comes to nothing.

Being denied an honorable burial for righteousness' sake can be borne with joy (**Matthew 5:12**).

But when disgrace is just punishment for sin, it points toward the final shame awaiting the unrepentant.

The fall of a tyrant can bring relief and joy to many people.

God will call to account those who destroy the peace of nations.

The picture of the king being received among the dead points, in Henry's reading, toward **the world of departed spirits**.

Human souls pass from this life into that world. They remain conscious there, although we cannot converse with them.

For someone who dies unholy after enjoying worldly glory and pleasure, death and judgment are dreadful indeed.

The offspring of persistent evildoers have no lasting honorable inheritance.

Babylon would become ruined and deserted.

Henry sees a further illustration of **the destruction of New Testament Babylon** (**Revelation 18:2**).

When people refuse to be cleansed through the **broom of reformation**, they face being swept away through the **broom of destruction**.

## Assyria's power will be broken (verses 24–27)

Those who make themselves a burden and a yoke upon God's people should consider the judgment awaiting them.

Those who are called according to God's purpose may take comfort: **everything God has determined will certainly stand**.

The Lord of hosts had resolved to break Assyria's yoke.

His hand was raised to carry out that judgment, and no human power could turn it back.

Through such providential events, God demonstrates how deeply he hates sin.

## The Philistines are warned (verses 28–32)

Isaiah also announced the destruction of Philistia through **famine and war**.

Henry says **Hezekiah** would become a more serious threat to them than **Uzziah** had been.

Their expectation of joy would end in mourning as their land suffered ruin.

The same warning applies to proud and rebellious sinners.

Yet **the Lord founded Zion as a refuge for poor sinners** who flee the judgment to come and trust in his mercy through Jesus Christ.

Let us tell others of the protection and comfort we have found, and urge them to seek that salvation too.
