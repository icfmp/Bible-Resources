# Micah 6 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God's controversy with Israel. | 1–5 |
| The duties God requires. | 6–8 |
| The wickedness of Israel. | 9–16 |

## God calls Israel to account (verses 1–5)

God called the people to explain why they were tired of **his worship** and inclined toward **idolatry**.

**Sin creates the dispute between God and humanity**.

The Lord reasons with us to teach us **how to examine our own conduct honestly**.

He called Israel to remember the many favors shown both to them and to their ancestors.

They should compare those gifts with **their own ungrateful and shameful behavior**.

## What does God require? (verses 6–8)

Henry says these verses **seem to reflect the substance of Balak's consultation with Balaam** about how to obtain Israel's God's favor.

A deep awareness of guilt and judgment makes people ask urgently **how to find pardon and peace**.

That concern may be an early sign of hope.

But we must learn that acceptance with God rests upon **the atoning work of Jesus Christ**.

We must also leave the sins through which we offend him.

What could satisfy God's justice?

In whose name can we come, since **we have no righteousness of our own to plead**?

In what righteousness can we appear before him?

The sacrifices the people proposed were impressive and expensive.

Their eagerness showed some understanding of guilt, but **they did not know the right means of pardon**.

People deeply convinced of their sin and danger would surrender everything they owned if that would purchase forgiveness.

But their proposals could not succeed.

The animal sacrifices received their meaning from **their reference to Christ**.

It was impossible for **the blood of bulls and goats** to remove sin.

Every plan for peace with God outside **the gospel** fails.

No gift offered by sinners can satisfy divine justice or compensate for dishonoring God through sin.

Nor can offerings ever replace **holiness of heart and a reformed life**.

People may surrender almost anything **except their sins**.

Yet no offering makes them acceptable to God if they refuse to abandon their sins.

God commands moral duties because **they are good for human beings**.

Keeping his commandments brings reward **in the keeping itself**, as well as afterward.

God has made his requirements **clear and understandable**.

What he requires is not a payment for pardon, but **love for him**, expressed through **doing justice, loving mercy and walking humbly with him**.

Nothing in that is unreasonable.

If we are to walk happily with God, every thought must be brought into obedience to him.

We must do this **as repentant sinners depending on the Redeemer and his atonement**.

Blessed be God, who is ready to give grace **to the humble person who waits for him**.

## Israel's injustice cannot be excused (verses 9–16)

God had made the duty to do justice clear.

The same passage now showed plainly how Israel had **acted unjustly**.

Hear the Lord's warning **before the rod of judgment arrives**.

And when correction does come, listen to its guidance.

God's voice should be heard **in his acts of discipline**.

People who are dishonest in business cannot be considered pure simply because they make a show of religious devotion.

What comes through **fraud and oppression** cannot be kept or enjoyed with genuine peace.

The possessions we cling to most tightly are often lost first.

Sin is **a root of bitterness**, easy to plant but difficult to remove.

It was once an honor for Israel to be called God's people.

But when they abandoned him, the name became a **reproach rather than an honor**.
