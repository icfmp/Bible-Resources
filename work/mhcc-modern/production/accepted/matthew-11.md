# Matthew 11 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Christ's preaching. | 1 |
| Christ's answer to John's disciples. | 2–6 |
| Christ's testimony to John the Baptist. | 7–15 |
| The perverseness of the Jews. | 16–24 |
| The gospel revealed to the simple. The heavy-laden invited. | 25–30 |

## Jesus continues preaching (verse 1)

Our divine Redeemer never grew tired of his work of love.

We should likewise refuse to grow weary of doing good, knowing that in due time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up.

## Jesus answers John the Baptist's disciples (verses 2–6)

There are **two possible explanations** for John's message.

Some believe he sent his disciples because **he himself desired reassurance**. Genuine faith can still be mixed with unbelief, and temptation may temporarily lead even good people to question important truths.

Yet Henry hopes John's faith did not fail in this matter and that he wanted only to have it strengthened and confirmed.

Others believe John sent them **for the disciples' own benefit**, so that Jesus would assure them directly.

Christ pointed them toward what they had **heard and seen**.

His merciful works, particularly toward poor people, identified him as the One bringing the tender mercies of God into the world.

When what people hear and see is compared carefully with **Scripture**, it points toward the way of salvation.

Prejudice is difficult to overcome, but allowing it to rule is dangerous.

Faith that continues despite prejudice and opposition will ultimately bring greater praise, honor and glory.

## Jesus honors John the Baptist (verses 7–15)

Jesus praised John not simply to honor him but to **help the listeners**.

Everyone who hears God's word will have to give an account of what they did with it.

The end of a sermon is not the end of our responsibility; in an important sense, it is when the real work **begins**.

John practiced serious self-denial and was detached from luxury, outward pomp and sensual pleasure.

Our conduct and appearance should be consistent with our calling and situation.

John was great and good, but **not perfect**.

In that sense he fell short of believers already glorified in heaven.

Even the least of them knows, loves and praises God more fully than the greatest saint in this world.

But Henry judges that **“the kingdom of heaven” in this passage refers more especially to the kingdom of grace**, the gospel age in its power and purity.

We should be thankful for living amid the advantages of its light and love.

John's ministry stirred many people, who became his disciples.

People previously thought unlikely to have any interest in God's kingdom now strove earnestly to enter it, seeming almost like unexpected intruders.

This illustrates the **urgency and zeal** required of us all.

Self must be denied. Our hearts, habits of mind and fundamental dispositions must change.

Those who truly desire God's great salvation will receive it on the terms he sets, refusing to let go until they obtain his blessing.

Divine things concern everyone.

God requires that people use the abilities he gives them properly.

Many remain ignorant because they refuse to learn.

## Jesus exposes a stubborn generation (verses 16–24)

Jesus reproved the self-important scribes and Pharisees.

Their behavior resembled children who refuse to join their companions' games and complain no matter how others try to please them.

Worldly people often produce petty criticisms of everyone, however good and holy the person may be.

Their objections reveal malice more than wisdom.

John's austere life displeased them, and Jesus' fellowship with people displeased them too.

Christ, completely holy and separate from sin, was accused of joining in sinners' wrongdoing.

Even spotless innocence cannot always protect a person from slander.

Jesus knew that many Jewish hearers had become **more hardened against his miracles and teaching** than people in **Tyre and Sidon** would have been.

Their judgment would therefore be more serious.

God possesses almighty power but **never punishes anyone beyond what is deserved**, nor withholds truth from people who genuinely long to know it.

## Jesus invites the weary to find rest (verses 25–30)

Children should be grateful.

When we approach God as **Father**, we must also remember that he is **Lord of heaven and earth**.

We should come with reverence before the sovereign Ruler of all and with confidence that he can protect us from evil and give us what is good.

Jesus made the extraordinary declaration that the Father had entrusted **all power, authority and judgment** into his hands.

Every revelation of the Father's will and love since Adam sinned has come to us **through Christ**.

The Savior invites everyone who labors and is heavily burdened to come to him.

In different senses, all human beings are burdened.

Some wear themselves out pursuing wealth, position and honor.

Others exhaust themselves chasing pleasure.

People enslaved to Satan and their own desires endure the harshest bondage.

Others labor uselessly trying to establish **their own righteousness** before God.

Convinced sinners feel guilt and dread. Tempted and suffering believers also carry real burdens.

Christ invites them all to come to him for **rest for their souls**.

He himself issues this invitation.

We come when, aware of guilt and misery, believing his love and power to help, we seek him earnestly through prayer.

It is both our duty and our highest interest to come.

The gospel's summons is generous: **Whoever desires may come**.

All who come this way receive rest as **Christ's free gift**, finding peace and comfort in the heart.

But we must take **his yoke** and willingly submit to his authority.

We must learn from him both how to obey and how to receive comfort.

He accepts willing service even when the service is imperfect.

His commands are holy, just and good.

They do require self-denial and can bring difficulty, yet his grace provides inward joy and peace even in this life.

His yoke is, as Henry puts it, **lined with love**.

The help Christ supplies is so powerful, his encouragements so suitable and his consolations so strong that obedience becomes pleasant.

**The path of duty is the path of rest.**

We may confidently stake our souls on the truths he teaches.

Why then would weary sinners seek rest elsewhere?

Let us come to Christ **daily** for deliverance from wrath and guilt, from sin and Satan, and from our worries, fears and sorrows.

Forced, unwilling obedience is a heavy burden rather than a light yoke.

It is useless to approach Jesus with our lips while keeping our hearts far from him.

Come to him and receive rest for your souls.

## Editorial review — Matthew 11

- **1:** Jesus never weary of **loving ministry**, perseverance in doing good — retained.
- **2–6:** **two views** of John's question carefully distinguished, mixed faith/unbelief possible but **Henry hopes John sought confirmation only**, evidence assessed through Scripture, prejudice dangerous — retained.
- **7–15:** sermons require **subsequent obedience**, John's self-denial, heavenly glorified saints vs **kingdom of grace as preferred explanation**, energetic response to salvation, deliberate ignorance — retained.
- **16–24:** critics like unreasonable **children's games**, John and Jesus both criticized, **innocent Christ slandered**, **Tyre/Sidon** more responsive, judgment perfectly just — retained.
- **25–30:** **Father who is Lord**, revelation entrusted to Son, several different burdens **worldly ambition, sensuality, sin, self-righteousness, conviction, trials**, invitation to Christ, **rest alongside submission**, yoke **lined with love** not merely easy outward life, formal unwilling service rejected — retained.
