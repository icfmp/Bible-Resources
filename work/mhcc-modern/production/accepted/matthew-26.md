## The leaders plot against Jesus (verses 1–5)

Jesus had often announced his suffering as an event still in the future.

Now he spoke of it as **immediately approaching**.

At the same time, the religious council plotted to put him to death secretly.

But God overruled their intentions.

Jesus, **the true Passover Lamb**, was to be sacrificed at that very season.

His death and resurrection were to become public events, not a secret execution hidden from the world.

## Jesus is anointed at Bethany (verses 6–13)

Pouring costly perfume upon Christ's head expressed the highest honor.

Where the heart genuinely loves him, nothing seems too precious to offer him.

The more his servants and their acts of devotion are criticized, the more clearly he may reveal his acceptance of them.

Mary's loving act was so remarkable that it would be remembered wherever the gospel was preached.

Jesus' promise concerning **the continuing memorial of her faith and love** has been fulfilled.

## Judas agrees to betray Jesus (verses 14–16)

Only twelve men were called apostles, yet **one was inwardly like a devil**.

We must therefore not expect any earthly religious community to be completely free from hypocrisy.

The greater a person's religious reputation, the more damage that person can cause when the heart is not right before God.

Judas knew Jesus' doctrine and life intimately.

Despite betraying him, **he could not accuse Jesus of any real wrongdoing**, even though such an accusation might have helped excuse his treachery.

What did Judas lack?

He had shared Christ's hospitality and lived alongside his Master.

**Not lack of money but love of money** lay at the root of his sin.

After arranging the betrayal, he still had time to repent and withdraw.

But lesser dishonest acts can gradually harden the conscience until worse crimes are committed without hesitation.

## Jesus celebrates the Passover (verses 17–25)

Jesus directed the disciples to the place where the Passover should be prepared.

He knows the often-hidden people who are willing to welcome and support his cause.

He graciously visits those ready to receive him.

The disciples obeyed his instructions.

Those who desire Christ's presence in the gospel's Passover must also submit to his commands.

Christians should remain alert to their own weakness, especially in times of testing.

We cannot know how strongly we may be tempted or how far God may allow us to experience our own weakness.

We should therefore avoid pride and cultivate reverent self-examination.

Before the Lord's Supper it is particularly fitting to search our hearts and pray earnestly.

Christ our Passover has been sacrificed for us.

We should therefore celebrate the feast with renewed **repentance, faith in his blood and dedication to his service**.

## Jesus institutes the Lord's Supper (verses 26–30)

The Lord's Supper is the Christian counterpart to the Passover.

It commemorates a deliverance far greater than Israel's rescue from Egypt.

**Take and eat** means receiving Christ as he is offered, welcoming his atoning work and submitting to his grace and rule.

Even an excellent meal provides no nourishment if merely admired; it must be eaten.

So the teaching and benefits of Christ must be **received personally by faith**.

Henry explicitly interprets **“This is my body” spiritually**: the bread **signifies and represents Christ's body**.

We share in the sun's warmth not by placing the sun itself in our hands but by receiving its rays.

Likewise, we share in Christ by receiving his grace and the benefits of his broken body.

The wine **signifies and represents his blood**.

Jesus gave thanks, teaching us to acknowledge God throughout the ordinance.

He gave the cup to the disciples with the command **to drink from it, all of them**.

The forgiveness of sin is the great benefit conveyed to **true believers** in the Lord's Supper, forming the foundation for every other blessing.

Jesus then anticipated the future joy of renewed fellowship in **his Father's kingdom**.

Henry understands drinking anew with them as picturing the happiness and glory believers will share with Christ forever.

That consolation will always be new.

When we look upon the outward signs of Christ's body broken and his blood shed for the forgiveness of sins, let us remember the terrible cost.

The supper cost Jesus as much as if he had **literally given his flesh to be eaten and his blood to be drunk**.

## Jesus warns of the disciples' failure (verses 31–35)

Peter's excessive confidence was the beginning of his fall.

We all tend to think too highly of our own firmness.

Those who feel most secure are often **least watchful** and therefore fall most quickly and terribly.

Satan actively exploits such carelessness.

God sometimes allows self-confident people to discover their weakness so that they may become humble.

## Jesus suffers in Gethsemane (verses 36–46)

Humanity rebelled against God's will **in a garden of pleasure**.

Christ, who came to atone for humanity's sin, submitted to God's will **in a garden of suffering**.

Jesus took into the place of his agony the disciples who had witnessed his glory at the Transfiguration.

Those who have seen his glory through faith are best prepared to suffer with him.

Scripture describes his overwhelming anguish, sorrow, astonishment and terror.

He began suffering this particular depth of sorrow and continued until he cried, **“It is finished.”**

He asked whether the cup could pass from him, yet declared perfect willingness to submit to everything necessary for our redemption.

Following his example, believers must accept even the bitterest cup their Father assigns.

Nature may shrink back, but faith should submit.

Our deeper concern should be **that suffering sanctifies us and that our hearts remain satisfied in God**, rather than simply that every trial be removed.

It is well for us that salvation rests with the One who **neither slumbers nor sleeps**.

Everyone is tempted.

We should especially fear **entering into temptation** and being overcome by it.

**Watch and pray**, depending on the Lord to uphold us.

Jesus knew clearly what suffering awaited him.

Until this point he had usually spoken of it calmly.

As **our Surety**, he undertook responsibility for our sins.

He was made sin for us and suffered **the righteous One for the unrighteous**.

Henry emphasizes that Scripture attributes Christ's deepest suffering to **God's own judgment upon sin**.

Jesus understood perfectly the terrible evil of sin, the vast guilt he was bearing and the holiness of divine justice.

No human mind can fully conceive the suffering resulting from that knowledge.

Henry further suggests, **as a possibility**, that Satan tormented Jesus through terrifying temptations and suggestions.

Such thoughts would have been especially dreadful to his perfect holiness.

If the burden of **imputed guilt** caused such sorrow in the One who upholds all things by his power, how dreadful is the fate of those who must answer for unforgiven sin themselves!

How can anyone neglect so great a salvation?

## Jesus is arrested and abandoned (verses 47–56)

Few enemies are more odious than those who claim discipleship and betray Christ with a kiss.

God does not need **our services**, and certainly not **our sins**, to fulfill his purposes.

Christ's submission to arrest and death was voluntary.

Though crucified in apparent weakness, **he chose to submit**.

If he had not been willing to suffer, his enemies could not have conquered him.

The disciples had abandoned everything to follow Jesus.

Now they abandoned him out of fear, though they had no better refuge to seek.

How foolish to flee from **the Fountain of Life** for fear of death!

## Jesus stands before Caiaphas (verses 57–68)

Jesus was taken into Jerusalem.

Peter's decision to follow **at a distance** foreshadowed his denial.

It is dangerous to wish to be a disciple without being willing to be known as one.

Rather than obsess over the outcome of an ordeal, we should prepare to do our duty whatever the result.

**The outcome belongs to God; duty belongs to us.**

False witnesses arose, fulfilling Scripture.

Christ was accused so that his people might avoid final condemnation.

If we are slandered, we should not expect better treatment than our Master received.

When Jesus was made sin for us, he remained silent, leaving **his blood to speak on behalf of the guilty**.

During his ministry he had not always stated his messianic identity in so direct a form, although his teaching and miracles testified to it.

Now he openly confessed that he was **the Christ, the Son of God**.

Remaining silent could have appeared to be an attempt to avoid suffering.

He spoke to provide an example for believers who must confess him despite danger.

Mockery and humiliation fell upon him, as described in **Isaiah 50**.

Let us confess his name and endure reproach, trusting that he will confess us before the Father's throne.

## Peter denies Jesus and repents (verses 69–75)

Scripture records Peter's terrible failure faithfully and without concealment.

Dangerous company often leads to sin, especially when people expose themselves needlessly to temptation.

Hiding our connection with Christ when we are called to acknowledge him amounts to **denying him**.

Peter's denial was serious, but **he fell through sudden fear**, unlike Judas, who had deliberately planned betrayal.

The crowing rooster awakened Peter's conscience.

Our conscience should similarly remind us of sins we have overlooked or forgotten.

God permitted this failure to correct Peter's excessive confidence and make him more modest, humble, compassionate and useful to others.

The record has taught believers ever since.

People who mock it or use Peter's fall as an excuse to sin do so at their own peril.

We cannot know how we would behave under severe testing if God left us to our own power.

**Those who think they stand should take care lest they fall.**

Distrust your own heart and trust the Lord.

Peter wept bitterly.

True grief over sin should be deep.

But it also produces change: after his repentance Peter did **not** deny Jesus again.

Instead he repeatedly confessed Christ despite danger.

Genuine repentance is shown by **a new practice of the very duty previously neglected**.

This is evidence not merely of bitter tears but of sincere sorrow.
