# 2 Samuel 24 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| David numbers the people. | 1–9 |
| He chooses the pestilence. | 10–15 |
| The staying the pestilence. | 16, 17 |
| David's sacrifice, The plague removed. | 18–25 |

## David orders a census (verses 1–9)

Because Israel had sinned, God allowed David to act wrongly.

The people then shared in the punishment that followed.

This teaches something important about God's government of the world.

**The sin was the pride in David's heart**.

By counting the nation, he hoped to appear more powerful and intimidating.

He trusted human strength more than he should have done, although he had written so often about trusting God alone.

The Lord does not judge sins as we do.

An act that appears to us harmless, or at worst a small mistake, may be deeply sinful before him.

God searches the thoughts and intentions of the heart.

Sometimes even ungodly people recognize wrong attitudes or behavior in believers when those believers remain unaware of them.

Yet God rarely allows those he loves to enjoy for long the things they selfishly covet.

## David chooses the plague (verses 10–15)

It is good for us when, after sinning, our consciences convict us.

When we confess our sins, we may pray with faith for forgiveness and for God's mercy to remove the guilt we abandon through sincere repentance.

It is just for God to take away what we boast about, or turn it into something bitter, so that our pride becomes a source of correction.

The punishment involved the people as well as David.

Although David's sin opened the way for judgment, the people's own sins also contributed.

David chose a judgment that would come **directly from God**.

He knew God's mercies were very great.

He preferred this to falling into the hands of enemies who would rejoice at Israel's suffering and become further hardened in their idolatry.

The plague would place David and his family in as much danger as the poorest Israelite.

It would also last for a shorter time than the other offered judgments, however severe its effects.

The rapid destruction showed how easily God can bring down the proud and how much we owe each day to his patience.

## God stops the destroying angel (verses 16 and 17)

Henry suggests that Jerusalem may have been especially guilty of sin and pride, leading the destroying angel to stretch out his hand toward that city.

But God **relented**.

As elsewhere, Henry explains that this means a change **in God's manner of dealing with people**, not a change in his eternal mind.

At the place where Abraham had been prevented from killing his son, the destroying angel was likewise stopped from destroying Jerusalem.

It is because of **the great Sacrifice, Jesus Christ**, that lives otherwise forfeited are protected from final destruction.

David showed a true shepherd's heart by offering to bear the judgment himself in order to save his subjects.

## David offers sacrifices; the plague ends (verses 18–25)

God's encouragement to offer him spiritual sacrifices is a sign that he is bringing us into fellowship with himself.

David purchased the ground on which he would build an altar.

God hates offerings acquired by injustice.

People who care chiefly about making their religion easy and inexpensive, involving little effort or money, do not understand true devotion.

What else are our possessions for, if not to honor God?

How could they be put to better use?

David offered burnt offerings, expressing honor for God's justice, and fellowship offerings, expressing honor for his mercy.

**Christ is both our Altar and our Sacrifice**.

Only through him can we expect to escape God's righteous judgment and receive his favor.

Death is destroying people all around us through many different causes, often suddenly.

It is foolish not to expect our own death and prepare to meet God.
