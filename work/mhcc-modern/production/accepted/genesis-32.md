# Genesis 32 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Jacob's vision at Mahanaim, His fear of Esau. | 1–8 |
| Jacob's earnest prayer for deliverance, He prepares a present for Esau. | 9–23 |
| He wrestles with the Angel. | 24–32 |

## God's angels meet Jacob, and he fears Esau (verses 1–8)

God's angels appeared to Jacob to encourage him and assure him of divine protection.

When God intends to lead his people through great trials, he often prepares them with great comforts.

Jacob, to whom the promise belonged, had spent years in difficult service, while Esau had become a prince.

Jacob sent a respectful message to his brother, showing that he was not insisting on the rights of the firstborn.

Giving way can calm even serious offenses (Ecclesiastes 10:4).

We should not refuse to speak respectfully even to people who are unjustly angry with us.

Then Jacob heard that Esau was approaching with men prepared for conflict, and he was deeply afraid.

A sharp awareness of danger, and the fear it awakens, can exist alongside humble confidence in God's power and his promises.

## Jacob prays and prepares a gift (verses 9–23)

Times of fear should become times of prayer.

Whatever frightens us should drive us to our knees before God.

Jacob had recently seen the angels who guarded him. But in his trouble, he turned to God, not to the angels.

He knew that the angels were servants of God, just as he was (Revelation 22:9).

Jacob's prayer is an excellent model of true prayer.

He gratefully remembered the undeserved kindness God had shown him.

He humbly confessed that he was unworthy of those blessings.

He plainly told God about his fears and his distress.

He placed the entire matter in the Lord's hands and rested all his hopes on him.

The best words we can bring to God in prayer are the words God himself has spoken to us.

In this way, Jacob made the Lord's name his strong tower, where he could find safety.

Fear did not drive Jacob into despair.

Nor did his prayer make him so presumptuous that he neglected the practical means God had given him.

God sometimes answers prayer by showing us how to arrange our affairs wisely.

Jacob sent Esau a gift in the hope of calming his anger.

We must not give up hope of reconciliation, even with those who seem most angry with us.

## Jacob wrestles with the Angel (verses 24–32)

Long before daybreak, Jacob was alone, laying his fears more fully before God in prayer.

As he prayed, someone who appeared to be a man wrestled with him.

When the Spirit helps us in our weakness, our deepest and most earnest desires may be more than words can express.

We mean more than we can say. Prayer then truly becomes wrestling with God.

However severely we are tested or discouraged, we may persevere and prevail.

When we prevail with God in prayer, we will also prevail over the enemies who struggle against us.

Wrestling demands strength and unrelenting effort. It is a fitting picture of true faith and prayer.

Jacob held his ground.

The struggle lasted a long time, but it neither shook his faith nor silenced his prayer.

He was determined to receive a blessing. He would rather have every bone put out of joint than leave without one.

Those who seek Christ's blessing must resolve not to give up.

Fervent prayer is effective prayer.

The Angel gave Jacob a lasting mark of honor by changing his name.

*Jacob* means “supplanter.” From then on, he would be remembered not for clever schemes or cunning, but for genuine courage.

He would be called *Israel*, “a prince with God”—a greater title than any borne by the powerful rulers of the earth.

A true prince is someone who has power with God.

The people most worthy of honor are those who are strong in prayer.

Having prevailed with God, Jacob would also prevail with people. He would win Esau's favor.

Jacob gave the place a new name: *Peniel*, meaning “the face of God.”

He called it that because he had seen God's appearance there and received God's favor.

When God honors us, our proper response is to marvel at his grace.

Matthew Henry identifies the Angel who wrestled with Jacob as the second Person of the Holy Trinity.

This is the One who would later be revealed as God in the flesh, dwelling among us in human nature and called *Immanuel* (Hosea 12:4–5).

Jacob left the encounter limping because of his injured thigh.

That continuing weakness may have kept him from becoming proud of the extraordinary revelations he had received.

The sun rose as Jacob went on his way.

And for every soul that has enjoyed fellowship with God, it is truly a sunrise.

## Editorial verification — Genesis 32

The modern manuscript was read against the complete [CrossWire MHCC chapter rendering at STEP](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version%3DMHCC@reference%3DGen.32) in a distinct post-drafting comparison, with [the separately posted Concise text at Bible Study Tools](https://www.biblestudytools.com/commentaries/matthew-henry-concise/genesis/32.html) as a secondary witness. The published `MHCC 0.1.1` has no recorded editorial wording correction for Genesis 32. This is a chapter-level prose audit, **not** attested full source-native OSIS coverage.

- **1–8:** the angels' encouragement, great comfort before great trials, Jacob's hard service versus Esau's princely status, respectful message not claiming his birthright, **Ecclesiastes 10:4**, obligation to treat hostile people respectfully, and fear coexisting with trust — all preserved.
- **9–23:** fear leading to God in prayer, angels as fellow servants (**Revelation 22:9**), all five parts of Jacob's model prayer (thanksgiving, unworthiness, fears, surrender, hope), God's promises as prayer's strongest words and his name as a tower, neither despair nor presumption, practical means and the gift as a step toward reconciliation — all preserved.
- **24–32:** solitary prayer, manlike wrestler, the Spirit helping unutterable prayers, perseverance through suffering, wrestling as the image of vigorous faith, insistence on Christ's blessing, renaming from supplanter to Israel/prince with God, honor in prayer, Esau's favor, Peniel/face of God, Henry's explicit **Trinitarian and incarnational identification of the Angel**, **Hosea 12:4–5**, the injured thigh as possible protection against pride, and the sunrise as an image of communion with God — all preserved.

Three original exposition divisions and the three outline rows are retained without resegmentation. The comparison was a separate same-assistant editorial pass, not an external Codex or human review.
