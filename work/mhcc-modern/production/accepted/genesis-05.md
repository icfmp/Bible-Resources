# Genesis 5 — Modern English

## Adam and Seth (verses 1–5)

Adam was made in God's image. But after he fell, he fathered a son in his own likeness—sinful and defiled, weak, miserable, and mortal like himself.

His son was like him not only as a human being with body and soul, but also as a sinner.

This was the opposite of the divine likeness in which Adam had been created. Having lost that likeness, he could not pass it on to his descendants.

Adam lived for 930 years. Then he died, just as God had sentenced him: “You shall return to dust.”

He did not die on the very day he ate the forbidden fruit. But on that day he became mortal, and from then on he began to die.

The rest of his life was a reprieve—a life already forfeited and under sentence of death, slowly wasting away.

Human life is a gradual dying.

## The patriarchs from Seth to Enoch (verses 6–20)

Of every one of these patriarchs except Enoch, we read the words, “And he died.”

It is good for us to pay attention to the deaths of others.

These men lived extraordinarily long lives. None died before reaching nearly eight hundred years, and some lived much longer.

That is a very long time for an immortal soul to remain confined within a house of clay.

Life in this world surely did not feel as burdensome to them as it often does now; otherwise they would have grown weary of it.

Nor was the future life revealed to them as clearly as it is to us under the gospel. Otherwise they would have been eager to depart for it.

Every patriarch who lived before the flood, except Noah, was born while Adam was still alive.

They could therefore have learned directly from him about creation, the fall, the promise, and God's commands concerning worship and godly living.

In this way, God preserved the knowledge of his will in his church.

## Enoch walks with God (verses 21–24)

Enoch was the seventh generation from Adam.

To be godly is to walk with God. That means being reconciled to him, for two cannot walk together unless they are in agreement (Amos 3:3).

Walking with God includes every part of a godly, righteous, and self-controlled life.

It means keeping God continually before us and acting as people who always live under his gaze.

In everything, we seek to please him and avoid offending him. We follow him as dearly loved children.

Instead of simply saying that Enoch *lived*, the Holy Spirit says that he *walked with God*.

This was his constant concern and work.

While other people lived for themselves and for the world, Enoch lived for God. That was the joy of his life.

God took Enoch to a better world.

Because he had not lived like other people, he did not leave the world through death as they did.

He was no longer found, because God had taken him away (Hebrews 11:5).

Enoch had lived only 365 years—little more than half a lifetime compared with the ages of people then.

God often takes earliest those he loves best. The time they lose on earth is more than made up to them in heaven, to their unspeakable benefit.

Notice the way his departure is described: he was no more, because God took him.

He was no longer in this world. He had been changed, as believers still alive at Christ's return will be changed.

Those who begin walking with God when they are young may hope to walk with him for many years, finding comfort and doing good.

A Christian's steady walk in holiness over many years, until God takes that person home, is one of the best recommendations for a faith that many oppose or misuse.

And walking with God fits perfectly well with the responsibilities, comforts, and duties of ordinary life.

## Methuselah, Lamech, and Noah (verses 25–32)

Matthew Henry explains *Methuselah* as meaning something like “when he dies, there is a dart” or “a sending out,” which he connects with the flood that came in the year of Methuselah's death.

Methuselah lived 969 years, longer than any other person on earth.

Yet even the person who lives longest must die at last.

*Noah* means “rest.” His parents gave him that name in the hope that he would be a great blessing to his generation.

Notice the complaint of Noah's father about the misery of human life since sin entered the world and brought its curse.

Our lives are filled with work, and our time with continual toil.

Because God cursed the ground, even those who work with the greatest care and effort may struggle to earn a bare living from it.

Lamech hoped that Noah's birth would bring relief: “This one will comfort us.”

That expresses the hope parents naturally place in their children—that they will bring them comfort and help. Yet children often turn out differently.

The name also points to a greater expectation.

Do we belong to Christ? Do we have heaven ahead of us?

We need greater comfort in our toil and sorrow than even our closest family or our most promising children can provide.

May we seek and find that comfort in Christ.

> Because God cursed the ground, even those who work with the greatest care and effort may struggle to earn a bare living from it.
