# Acts 15 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |

| The council at Jerusalem. | 7–21 |

## Teachers dispute Christian liberty (verses 1–6)

Some visitors from Judea taught the Gentile believers in Antioch that they could not be saved unless they obeyed **the whole ceremonial law of Moses**.

In this way they sought to destroy **Christian liberty**.

We are strangely inclined to suppose that everyone who acts differently from us must be wrong.

This teaching burdened the Gentile believers and discouraged them.

Wise and good people try to avoid quarrels and controversies whenever they can.

**But when false teachers oppose central gospel truths or introduce harmful teaching, we must be willing to confront them.**

## The Jerusalem council discerns God's work (verses 7–21)

Peter's words about God **purifying hearts by faith** show that **justification by faith and sanctification through the Holy Spirit cannot be separated**.

Both are gifts from God.

We should thank God for the privilege of hearing the gospel and ask for faith that the great Searcher of hearts will approve, confirmed by the Holy Spirit's seal.

In that way our hearts and consciences are purified from sin's guilt, and we are freed from burdens people try to impose upon Christ's disciples.

Paul and Barnabas described concrete evidence that **God himself accepted the gospel preached to Gentiles who had not submitted to Moses' ceremonial law**.

To demand that law afterward would be to reverse what God had accomplished.

James therefore argued that Gentile converts should not be burdened with Jewish religious rites.

They should **avoid food sacrificed to idols** as evidence of their rejection of idolatry.

They should also **avoid sexual immorality**, which Gentile culture did not condemn seriously enough and which formed part of some pagan religious practices.

James further advised abstaining from **things strangled and from blood**.

This had been forbidden under the Mosaic law.

Now it was important to respect the association of blood with the sacrifices **still being offered** and avoid needlessly grieving Jewish Christians or prejudicing Jews who had not yet believed.

**But the historical reason for that temporary restriction has long ceased**, Henry says, **so believers remain free in this matter as in similar matters**.

Converts should be warned against even the appearance of former sins and practices likely to tempt them.

Christian liberty should be exercised with **moderation and good judgment**.

## The council's letter reassures the Gentiles (verses 22–35)

The apostles and church leaders could declare that **the Holy Spirit guided their decision**.

It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to them not to impose more requirements than those already described—requirements important either in themselves or because of the particular circumstances.

Gentile converts were comforted that they were not compelled to keep the outward ceremonial regulations that **could neither cleanse nor quiet the conscience**.

The teachers who had disturbed them were corrected.

Church peace was restored and an approaching division was averted.

The people therefore had reason to thank God.

Many ministers served in Antioch.

When others labor faithfully in teaching God's word, **that does not excuse us from work**.

Their zeal and usefulness should stir us up instead of making us idle.

## Paul and Barnabas separate (verses 36–41)

Even excellent ministers can quarrel.

This private disagreement between **Paul and Barnabas** nevertheless ended in a way that did not prevent further gospel service.

Barnabas wanted his **nephew John Mark** to accompany them.

We should be alert to partiality when promoting our own relatives.

Paul judged Mark unfit for the service and honor of the mission because he had earlier departed from them **without their knowledge or consent** (see **Acts 13:13**).

Neither would yield, so they separated.

**The best believers are still human** and subject to the same sinful passions as others.

**Perhaps each side was partly at fault**, as so often happens in disputes.

Only Christ's example is perfect.

It should not surprise us that wise and good people disagree.

While we remain imperfect, we will not reach complete unity of mind.

That awaits heaven.

Yet pride and anger remaining even in sincere believers cause enormous harm in the church and the wider world.

Many at Antioch, unfamiliar with the extent of Paul and Barnabas's former devotion, would hear of their quarrel and departure.

Our own conduct can likewise damage the cause we genuinely wish to serve.

Pray constantly that unholy tempers will not lead us to harm Christ's work.

In epistles written after this event, **Paul spoke with honor and affection about both Barnabas and Mark**.

Loving Savior, may everyone professing your name be reconciled through love received from you—a love not easily provoked and ready to forgive and bury offenses.
