# Judges 14 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Samson desires a wife of the Philistines. | 1–4 |
| Samson kills a lion. | 5–9 |
| Samson's riddle. | 10–20 |

## Samson wants to marry a Philistine woman (verses 1–4)

As an ordinary marriage decision, Samson's desire was weak and foolish.

He was not merely an Israelite; he was also a **Nazirite** set apart for the Lord.

Why should he seek marriage with a worshiper of **Dagon**?

The account gives no reason to think the woman was wise, virtuous or likely to make a good companion.

Samson simply saw something attractive in her.

Anyone who chooses a spouse solely by what the eyes see and by personal fancy has only himself to blame if the marriage proves spiritually harmful.

Still, Samson did well to speak with his parents before going further.

Henry says children ought not to marry or even begin pursuing marriage without parental counsel and consent.

Samson's parents were right to warn him against being unequally joined to someone who did not share his faith.

It seems that God allowed Samson to follow his own strong desire while intending to bring good from what happened.

His parents eventually consented because he remained determined.

**But this account is not given to us as an example to imitate.**

## Samson kills a lion (verses 5–9)

By empowering Samson to kill the lion, God showed him what he could do through the strength given by the Lord's Spirit.

This should have encouraged him not to fear even the greatest difficulties.

Samson was alone in the vineyards after wandering away.

Young people often fail to recognize the danger they face when wandering away from wise, godly parents.

The devil is like a roaring lion seeking someone to devour.

People also fail to notice how many dangerous lions lurk in vineyards—especially where red wine and its temptations are found.

Our Lord Jesus has conquered Satan, the roaring lion.

Believers, like Samson, can therefore find **honey in the defeated lion**: strength and spiritual satisfaction enough for themselves and for their friends.

## Samson's riddle and the betrayal of his trust (verses 10–20)

Literally, Samson's riddle simply meant that he had obtained honey for food and pleasure from the body of a fierce lion.

But Henry sees it as pointing toward **Christ's victory over Satan**.

Through Christ's humiliation, suffering and death came victory and exaltation, glory for the Father and great spiritual blessings for his people.

Even death itself, that devouring enemy, has been stripped of its sting and terror.

Instead of preventing blessedness, it now helps believers pass into eternal joy.

Thus food comes from the eater and sweetness from the strong.

Samson's companions forced his wife to obtain the answer to the riddle.

A spouse or friend who is controlled by worldly loyalties can become an enemy within the camp, always ready to betray the believer.

A close relationship cannot remain secure or happy when confidential matters cannot be shared without danger of betrayal.

Satan could not do so much harm through temptation unless he could use the corruption of our own hearts.

His greatest advantage comes from his cooperation with our deceitful desires.

This experience helped separate Samson from his new relationships.

It would be good if every disappointment and act of unkindness we experienced from the world led us, through faith and prayer, to return to our heavenly Father's house and find rest there.

Human friendship can be unreliable.

However warmly someone may profess friendship, a person devoted to the world may quickly grow tired of a faithful believer.
