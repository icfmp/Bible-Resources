# Lamentations 3 — Modern English

## Original heading

**The faithful lament their calamities, and hope in God's mercies.**

## The prophet's grief and temptation (verses 1–20)

The prophet describes the darkest and most discouraging part of his experience before explaining where he found help and relief.

During his trial, the Lord seemed terrifying to him.

His affliction felt like misery itself, because **sin makes suffering's cup bitter**.

The struggle between **unbelief and faith** may become very severe.

Yet even the weakest believer is mistaken when imagining that **strength and hope from the Lord have entirely disappeared**.

## The Lord's mercies are new every morning (verses 21–36)

Having described his distress and temptations, the prophet now shows how he rose above them.

However bad our condition is, **God's mercy is the reason it is not worse**.

We should notice not only what works against us, but also the gifts and promises that support us.

**God's compassion never fails.**

Every morning provides fresh evidence of it.

Earthly possessions and inheritances perish, but **the Lord himself is a portion forever**.

It is our duty, and also our comfort, to hope and **wait quietly for his salvation**.

Afflictions may accomplish much good.

Many have found that bearing the yoke in youth makes them serious, humble and less attached to the world, when otherwise they might have been proud and unruly.

When suffering produces patience, patience can deepen experience, and that experience strengthens **a hope that does not put us to shame**.

Remembering both sin's evil and our own sinfulness shows us that we are not consumed only because of the Lord's mercies.

Perhaps we cannot yet say with steady confidence, **“The Lord is my portion.”**

But can we not at least pray, **“I desire him as my portion and salvation; I hope in his word”**?

Happy are those who learn to receive their affliction as being under God's sovereign hand.

## Examine your own ways and return to God (verses 37–41)

As long as life remains, hope remains.

Rather than only complaining that things are bad, we should encourage ourselves that they may become better.

Our sins deserve far more suffering than we actually receive.

We should **bring our complaint to God**, not **complain against God**.

During disaster we are quick to examine other people's ways and blame them.

But our duty is to **search and test our own ways**, and turn away from sin toward God.

Our hearts must accompany our prayers.

If inward conviction does not correspond to outward words, we **mock God and deceive ourselves**.

## The prophet weeps and waits for God (verses 42–54)

The more the prophet contemplated the devastation, the more deeply he grieved.

Yet one word of comfort remained.

While the people continued **weeping**, they also continued **waiting**.

They looked to the Lord alone for relief and rescue.

## Faith finds courage in God's answer (verses 55–66)

Faith finally emerges victorious.

The prophet ends with a measure of comfort.

**Prayer is the breath of a person made new by God.**

In asking for mercy, the renewed soul draws in grace like air.

In praise, that soul breathes it back.

Prayer reveals spiritual life and helps sustain it.

God calmed the people's fears and quieted their hearts.

He said, **“Do not fear.”**

That was the language of his grace, borne witness by his Spirit to their spirits.

And what are our sufferings compared with **the sufferings of the Redeemer**?

The Lord will deliver his people from their troubles and renew his church after persecution.

He will save believers with **everlasting salvation**, while his enemies face eternal judgment.
