# Genesis 15 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God encourages Abram. | 1 |
| God's promise; Abraham is justified by faith. | 2–6 |
| God promises Abram the land of Canaan. | 7–11 |
| The promise is confirmed in a vision. | 12–16 |
| The promise is confirmed by a sign. | 17–21 |

## God assures Abram of his protection (verse 1)

God assured Abram that he would remain safe and happy.

“I am your shield,” he said.

In other words, God himself was near him, actively caring for him and protecting him.

God is a shield for his people—ready whenever they need him and surrounding them on every side.

If we truly understand that he is and will remain our shield, it should silence our distressing and tormenting fears.

## The promised son and justification through faith (verses 2–6)

We must never complain *about* God, but we are allowed to bring our complaints *to* him and tell him all that troubles us.

It relieves a burdened heart to open its concerns to a faithful and compassionate friend.

Abram's grief was that he had no child and no reason to expect one.

The absence of a son distressed him so deeply that it overshadowed every other comfort.

If Abram was thinking only of earthly comforts, his complaint deserved correction.

But if his longing concerned the promised Seed, his desire was very good.

Until we know that we belong to Christ, we should not be satisfied.

What good will anything else do me if I remain without Christ?

If we persevere in prayer while humbly submitting to God's will, we will not seek him in vain.

God gave Abram an explicit promise of a son.

Christians can trust God for the ordinary concerns of life.

But the faith by which they are justified always looks to the person and work of Jesus Christ.

Abram believed God as the One who promised Christ.

We believe God as the One who has raised Christ from the dead (Romans 4:24).

Through faith in Christ's blood, we receive the forgiveness of sins.

## God confirms the inheritance of Canaan (verses 7–11)

God assured Abram that the land of Canaan would belong to him as an inheritance.

Unlike human beings, who often promise more than they can deliver, God never promises anything beyond his power to accomplish.

Abram obeyed God's instructions.

He divided the animals, following the custom used for confirming covenants (Jeremiah 34:18–19).

After making the preparations God required, he waited for whatever sign the Lord might give.

We should also watch carefully over the spiritual sacrifices we bring to God.

When empty thoughts descend on our worship like birds upon the divided animals, we must drive them away.

Let us seek to give God our attention without distraction.

## The vision of Israel's future (verses 12–16)

Abram fell into a deep sleep, and with it came a dreadful darkness.

What a sudden change!

Even God's children, who belong to the light, do not always walk in the light.

God then foretold several things.

**First**, Abram's descendants would suffer for a long time.

They would live as strangers. In a deeper sense, the heirs of heaven are strangers on earth.

They would become servants. But the Canaanites serve under a curse, while the Hebrews would serve under a blessing.

They would also suffer oppression. Those whom God loves and blesses are often cruelly treated by wicked people.

**Second**, God would judge their enemies.

He may allow persecutors and oppressors to trample upon his people for a long time, but in the end he will certainly call them to account.

**Third**, God would bring Abram's descendants out of Egypt.

**Fourth**, he would settle them happily in Canaan.

God's words also show that guilt grows until it reaches its appointed measure. Some people fill up that measure slowly.

Knowing the future would rarely make us happier.

Even the most favored families and the happiest lives contain so many afflictions that God shows mercy by hiding from us what will happen to us and those we love.

## The smoking furnace and the burning lamp (verses 17–21)

The smoking furnace and the burning lamp probably represented Israel's severe trials and its joyful deliverance, along with God's gracious help in the meantime.

It is also likely that the furnace and lamp passing between the divided animals burned and consumed them.

If so, this completed the sacrifice and showed that God accepted it.

The sign therefore points to the truth that God's covenants with human beings are made through sacrifice (Psalm 50:5).

We too can know that God accepts our sacrifices when he kindles holy and devoted affections in our souls.

The boundaries of the promised land were stated.

God also named the nations and tribes that would be driven out so that Abram's descendants could live there.

Throughout this chapter, we see Abram's faith struggling against unbelief and finally overcoming it.

Believers, do not be surprised when you pass through seasons of distress and darkness.

God does not want you to remain crushed by fear.

Do not be afraid. All that he was to Abram, he will be to you.
