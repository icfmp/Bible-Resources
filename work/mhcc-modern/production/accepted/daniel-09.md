# Daniel 9 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Daniel considers the time of the captivity. | 1–3 |
| His confession of sin, and prayer. | 4–19 |
| The revelation concerning the coming of the Messiah. | 20–27 |

## Daniel studies Jeremiah's prophecy (verses 1–3)

From the prophetic books, especially **Jeremiah**, Daniel learned that Jerusalem's desolation would last **seventy years**, a period now approaching its end.

God's promises are intended **to encourage prayer**, not to make prayer unnecessary.

The closer we see their fulfillment drawing, the more earnestly we should plead them before God.

## Daniel confesses sin and prays (verses 4–19)

Our prayers should include **confession**: of the sins we have committed, of our **faith in God** and dependence upon him, of sorrow for sin and of our resolve to turn from it.

Such words must express our genuine convictions, not merely follow a form.

Daniel prayed humbly, earnestly and reverently.

He gave God glory as One **to be feared and also trusted**.

We should consider both **God's greatness and goodness**, his **majesty and mercy**.

Daniel confessed his people's sin as the cause of the suffering they had experienced for many years.

Everyone who seeks mercy must confess sin.

Daniel humbled himself and acknowledged **the righteousness of God's judgment**.

True repentance agrees that God is just.

Afflictions are sent to make people turn away from wrongdoing and learn God's truth.

Daniel also appealed in faith to God's mercy.

The Lord has always been **ready to forgive**.

Remembering that righteousness belongs to God humbles us; remembering that **mercies belong to him** encourages us.

His mercy is abundant, providing not merely a single pardon but **repeated forgiveness**.

Daniel pointed to the disgrace his people had suffered and **the ruins of God's sanctuary**.

Sin disgraces any people, especially people who profess God's name.

Faithful believers grieve over the desolation of his worship.

Daniel asked for the restoration of the Jewish captives:

**Lord, hear and act!** Not merely hear and answer in words, but **act on our behalf**, accomplishing what no one else can. **Do not delay**.

His prayer included several reasons for asking God to intervene, especially **for the sake of the Lord Christ**, through whom God shows mercy to sinners when they repent.

In our own prayers we should plead **Christ's righteousness alone**, not our merit.

Daniel's earnest, humble faith should guide the way we pray.

## The prophecy of the Messiah's coming (verses 20–27)

God sent Daniel an answer immediately.

It was a particularly memorable answer.

We are not now entitled to expect answers through angels as Daniel received them.

Nevertheless, when we pray earnestly for what God has promised, **we can receive the promise itself as an answer by faith**, because the One who promised is faithful.

Daniel was shown a redemption greater and more glorious than the return from Babylon: **the work God would accomplish through Christ for his church**.

Anyone wishing to know **Christ and his grace** must devote serious attention to prayer.

The **evening sacrifice** pictured the great offering Christ would make at **the evening of the world**, in Henry's typological reading.

Through that sacrifice Daniel's prayer was accepted, and he received this revelation of redeeming love.

Henry calls **verses 24–27 one of the most remarkable prophecies of Christ, his coming and his salvation**.

He interprets **the seventy weeks** as **seventy sets of seven years, or 490 years**.

Around the end of that period, Christ would offer a sacrifice providing **complete atonement for sin** and bringing in **everlasting righteousness**, the ground of full justification for every believer.

Henry argues polemically that Jewish expectation of a **different Messiah** after the period he believes fixed by this prophecy reflects **obstinate unbelief**.

He also understands the crucifixion of Jesus as bringing Israel's guilt to a climax, followed by national calamities.

These are **Henry's theological and chronological readings**, not a license to condemn Jewish people collectively or to treat his timetable as an editorial correction to Scripture.

Every blessing given to sinners comes through **Christ's one atoning sacrifice**.

He, the righteous, suffered for the unrighteous **to bring us to God**.

This sacrifice opens the way to **the throne of grace** and the entrance to heaven.

The prophecy confirms the covenant with many and brings prophecy's purpose to its fulfillment.

As we rejoice in salvation, let us remember **what it cost the Redeemer**.

How can anyone escape who neglects such a great salvation?
