## Moses' song begins (verses 1 and 2)

Moses called heaven and earth to witness the truth and importance of what he was about to declare.

His teaching was the gospel: God's message about Jesus Christ, grace and mercy through him, and salvation and life received through him.

## God's perfection and Israel's character (verses 3–6)

God is called **a Rock** here.

Henry notes that this is the first time Scripture uses that name for him.

The image expresses God's unchangeable power, faithfulness and love, revealed in Christ and the gospel.

We may build our hope upon that sure foundation and find shelter in him against enemies, burning heat and storms.

God's work is perfect.

This includes the saving work in which every divine perfection is displayed.

His dealings with creatures are directed by wisdom that cannot make mistakes and by flawless justice.

No one will suffer injustice at his hand.

The song also brings a serious charge against Israel.

Even God's children have faults and sinful stains while they remain in this imperfect life.

Anyone claiming complete freedom from sin is deceiving himself.

But Henry distinguishes the failings of God's children from a settled, notorious and unrepentant practice of sin, which he regards as a mark of the devil's children.

Israel acted foolishly in abandoning divine mercy for worthless idols.

## God's extraordinary care for Israel (verses 7–14)

Moses recalled particular examples of the Lord's goodness toward Israel.

The eagle caring for her young was a beautiful picture of Christ's love.

Christ placed himself between God's justice and our guilty souls, bearing our sins in his body upon the tree.

Through gospel preaching and the Holy Spirit's power, he awakens sinners and draws them away from Satan's bondage.

The imagery in verses 13 and 14 also pictures believers overcoming their spiritual enemies—sin, Satan and the world—through Christ.

In him they are safe and victorious.

When their hearts are set above earthly affairs, they enjoy spiritual peace and joy.

Henry looks forward to such blessings for spiritual Israel in the latter days.

## Israel abandons its Savior (verses 15–18)

Moses described two forms of Israel's wickedness, both involving apostasy.

The people were called **Jeshurun**.

Henry notes alternative explanations of the name: some interpret it as **upright people**, others as **seeing people**.

Israel soon lost the reputation for both righteousness and knowledge.

The people indulged bodily appetite as though their only task was to satisfy the flesh.

Proud and self-indulgent people effectively make gods of themselves and their appetites.

They turn away from the Lord and treat him as unimportant.

Henry insists that sinners have only one way of being accepted and sanctified before God.

False religion may commend other routes and call this openness, but that does not make those routes valid.

How foolish it is to forsake the Rock of salvation for the rock of ruin!

## God's judgments against sin (verses 19–25)

The previous lines described Israel's rebellion.

These lines describe the judgments God's justice would bring.

We deceive ourselves if we think people may reject him while he remains indifferent.

Sin is hateful before the holy God.

We should see the harm it causes and reject the folly of laughing at it.

## God withholds the vengeance Israel deserved (verses 26–38)

Israel's idolatry and rebellion deserved destruction.

Yet the Lord spared the nation.

Henry regards the preservation of the Jewish people as a continuing witness to Scripture's truth and as part of God's wise and holy purposes.

The prophecies, he says, give some idea of those purposes.

God will not allow his glorious throne to be dishonored.

It is wise to consider the end of one's life.

Such consideration often helps sinners return to him.

The passage speaks particularly about God's purpose for Israel in the latter days, but the warning applies more widely.

What happiness will sinners lose, and what misery will they encounter, if they continue rebelling against God?

“What will be the end of it?” (Jeremiah 5:31).

God will bring down his church's enemies at the time he chooses.

People who imagine themselves completely secure may suddenly be destroyed.

And the moment of greatest distress for God's people may be the moment when he appears to rescue them.

Anyone trusting in a refuge other than God will find that false refuge fail when it is needed most.

Henry understands the Jewish rejection of the Messiah as a continuation of Israel's earlier idolatry, apostasy and rebellion.

He anticipates a future humbling and repentance, followed by faith in the Mediator they have rejected and renewed deliverance and prosperity.

These are Henry's theological interpretations of the historical nation and prophecy, preserved as his words rather than asserted as new editorial judgments.

## The Lord delivers his people (verses 39–43)

The end of the song contains three messages.

**First, glory belongs to God.** No one can escape his power.

**Second, it brings terror to his enemies.** God's judgment comes against those who hate him.

**Third, it brings comfort to his own people.** The song ends with joy.

Whatever judgments fall on sinners, those who belong to God will be cared for.

## Moses urges Israel to remember the song (verses 44–47)

Moses delivered the song solemnly.

He charged Israel to remember all the good words God had spoken through him.

These matters are not trivial.

They concern life and death.

To heed them is to find everlasting life; to disregard them is to face ruin.

How good it would be if everyone were convinced that faith is the very life of the soul!

## Moses is commanded to die on Nebo (verses 48–52)

Moses had completed his work.

Why should he desire to stay longer in this world?

God reminded him of the sin that had prevented him from entering Canaan.

Even the best believers should approach death with repentance for the sins and weaknesses they still remember.

But they may die peacefully whenever God calls them.

A sure hope of eternal life beyond death, founded on faith, allows them to do so even though they remember their faults.
