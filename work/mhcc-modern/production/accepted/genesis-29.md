# Genesis 29 — Modern English

## Jacob reaches the well near Haran (verses 1–8)

After enjoying sweet communion with God at Bethel, Jacob continued his journey with a cheerful heart.

Providence brought him to the field where his uncle's flocks came to drink.

The shepherds' care for their sheep can remind us of the tender love of our Lord Jesus, the great Shepherd of the church.

He is the good Shepherd who knows his sheep and is known by them.

A stone covered the mouth of the well to protect it.

Water was scarce, and this well was not available for anyone to use whenever they wished.

But having separate interests should never stop us from helping one another.

When all the shepherds arrived with their flocks, they drew water together like loving neighbors.

Kind words have great power (Proverbs 31:26).

Jacob was courteous toward these strangers, and they responded courteously to him.

## Jacob meets Rachel and is welcomed by Laban (verses 9–14)

Notice Rachel's humility and diligence.

No one should be ashamed of honest, useful work.

Nor should such work stand in the way of a person's future advancement.

When Jacob discovered that Rachel was his relative, he was eager to help her.

Laban, though not especially pleasant in temperament, welcomed Jacob.

He was satisfied with Jacob's account of himself.

We should avoid being foolishly ready to believe everything we hear.

But we must also avoid becoming suspicious of others without good reason.

## Jacob serves for Rachel and is deceived by Laban (verses 15–30)

Jacob was not idle during the month he spent in Laban's household as a guest.

Wherever we find ourselves, we should try to engage in some useful work.

Laban wanted Jacob to remain with him.

But a younger or less influential relative must not be taken advantage of. Honest work deserves proper reward.

Jacob told Laban that he loved Rachel.

He had no worldly property with which to provide for a marriage, so he promised to work for seven years.

Love makes long and difficult service seem short and easy.

That is why Scripture speaks of the *labor of love* (Hebrews 6:10).

If we learn to value the happiness of heaven properly, the sufferings of this life will seem like very little.

Even a lifetime of labor will pass like a few days for those who love God and long for Christ's appearing.

Jacob had deceived his father. Now his father-in-law deceived him in much the same way.

Laban acted unrighteously, but the Lord remained righteous in allowing Jacob to experience this reversal (Judges 1:7).

Even godly people who take a dishonest step are sometimes repaid for it during their lives on earth.

Many people who do not face Jacob's particular disappointment in marriage later discover, to their grief, that they have misunderstood their spouse's character.

Marriage should therefore be approached with thoughtful counsel and careful consideration on both sides.

There is reason to believe that Laban's explanation for his deception was not truthful.

His way of putting matters right only made them worse.

Jacob became caught up in the troubles of having more than one wife.

He could not refuse Rachel, to whom he had already committed himself, and still less could he refuse Leah.

At that time there was not yet an explicit command against taking more than one wife.

Henry regards the patriarchs' polygamy as a sin committed in ignorance.

But it cannot be used to justify the same practice now, when God's will has been made clear through the law (Leviticus 18:18) and still more fully through our Savior: one man and one woman are to be joined in marriage (1 Corinthians 7:2).

## Leah's sons (verses 31–35)

The names Leah gave her sons expressed her concern both for God and for her husband.

*Reuben* means “See, a son!” Leah hoped, “Now my husband will love me.”

*Levi* means “joined.” She hoped, “Now my husband will become attached to me.”

Mutual affection is both a duty and a comfort in marriage.

Husbands and wives should seek to make themselves worthy of one another's affection (1 Corinthians 7:33–34).

Leah gratefully acknowledged God's kindness in hearing her.

Whenever something supports or comforts us in hardship, or helps to deliver us from it, we must acknowledge God as its source.

Her fourth son was called *Judah*, meaning “praise.”

Leah said, “Now I will praise the LORD.”

From Judah came Christ according to his human ancestry.

Whatever causes us joy should also cause us to give thanks.

New blessings should stir us to thank God more deeply for earlier blessings.

“Now I will praise the LORD”—more and better than before.

All our praise must center on Christ.

He is both the One for whom we praise God and the Mediator through whom our praise is offered.

He descended in the flesh from the man whose name means “Praise,” and he himself is our praise.

Is Christ being formed within my heart?

Then let me say: “Now I will praise the LORD.”
