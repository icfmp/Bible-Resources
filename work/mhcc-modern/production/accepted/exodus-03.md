## God appears in the burning bush (verses 1–6)

Moses' life may be divided into three periods of forty years.

He spent the first forty as a prince in Pharaoh's court, the second as a shepherd in Midian, and the third as a king in Jeshurun.

How changeable human life is!

When God first appeared to Moses, he found him tending sheep.

For someone with Moses' abilities and education, that might seem a humble occupation.

But Moses was content with it.

In this way, he learned meekness and contentment, qualities for which Scripture remembers him more than for all his learning.

Satan likes to find us idle. God is pleased when he finds us faithfully at work.

Solitude can also be a good companion to communion with God.

To Moses' astonishment, he saw a bush burning, though nothing had set it alight.

It burned without being consumed.

This was a picture of the church enslaved in Egypt.

It also reminds us of the church through every age: even under the fiercest persecution, God's presence preserves it from destruction.

In Scripture, fire can picture God's holiness and justice.

It can also represent the sufferings and trials through which God tests and purifies his people.

It even pictures the baptism of the Holy Spirit, through which sinful desires are consumed and the soul is transformed into God's likeness.

God called Moses graciously, and Moses answered promptly.

Those who seek fellowship with God must meet him in the worship he has appointed, wherever he chooses to reveal his glory—even in a bush.

Removing his shoes was Moses' sign of reverence and submission.

We should draw near to God with serious preparation and deep respect, avoiding everything careless, irreverent, or unsuitable for his service.

God did not say, “I *was* the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob,” but “I *am*.”

The patriarchs are still alive, though their bodies have been in the grave for many years.

No passage of time can separate the souls of the righteous from their Maker.

God was teaching Moses about the life to come and strengthening his confidence in the future state.

Jesus himself explained the words in this way, using them to prove the resurrection of the dead (Luke 20:37).

Moses hid his face, ashamed and afraid to look upon God.

The more we see of God's grace and covenant love, the more reason we have to worship with reverence and godly fear.

## God sends Moses to deliver Israel (verses 7–10)

God sees his people's suffering.

He knows even their hidden sorrows.

He hears the cries of those who are oppressed.

He sees their oppressors, however powerful; none is above him.

God promises a speedy deliverance, using means beyond the ordinary course of Providence.

And those whom God rescues by his grace from spiritual Egypt he will bring into the heavenly Canaan.

## God reveals his name (verses 11–15)

Years earlier, Moses had believed himself able to deliver Israel and had rushed into the work before the right time.

Now, when he was the most suitable person on earth for the task, he was deeply aware of his weakness.

He knew God and himself better than before.

Earlier, self-confidence had been mixed with strong faith and zeal.

Now, even sinful distrust of God was slipping in under the appearance of humility.

Such weaknesses can mar even the finest graces and best actions of the most remarkable believers.

But God answered every objection with one promise: “I will certainly be with you.”

That was enough.

God now made himself known by two names.

The first declares **who he is in himself**: “I AM THAT I AM.”

This explains the name *Jehovah*.

It declares, first, that God is self-existent. His being comes from himself.

Second, he is eternal and unchangeable—the same yesterday, today, and forever.

Third, he is beyond complete human understanding.

We cannot discover all that he is by searching, and this name restrains our bold and prying questions about him.

Fourth, he is faithful and true to all his promises.

His word is as unchanging as his nature.

Israel was to know that “I AM” had sent Moses.

God alone exists by himself. Everything else receives its existence from him and depends completely on him.

The second name declares **who he is to his people**: “The Lord, the God of your fathers, has sent me to you.”

Moses had to revive among Israel the faith of its ancestors, which had almost disappeared.

Then the people could expect the promises made to their fathers to be fulfilled soon.

## God promises to free Israel (verses 16–22)

Moses would succeed in speaking to Israel's elders.

God himself moves human hearts and opens their ears.

He could tell Moses beforehand, “They will listen to you,” because he would make them willing when his power was shown.

Pharaoh, however, would refuse to yield to requests, persuasion, and humble appeals.

Nor would he submit merely because of a mighty hand stretched out in signs and wonders.

Yet those who will not bow before the power of God's word will surely be broken by the power of his hand.

At their departure, Pharaoh's people would supply Israel with riches.

Pharaoh's tyranny and Israel's slavery picture the miserable condition of sinners.

They continue laboring under a painful yoke until God sends redemption.

God accompanies the gospel's invitations with the teaching of his Spirit.

Through that work, people become willing to seek and strive for deliverance.

Satan loses his power to keep them in bondage.

They come out with all they are and all they possess and devote everything to God's glory and the service of his church.
