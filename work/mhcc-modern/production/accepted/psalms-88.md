# Psalm 88 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The psalmist pours out his soul to God in lamentation. | 1–9 |
| He wrestles by faith, in his prayer to God for comfort. | 10–18 |

## A believer's deeply troubled soul (verses 1–9)

The psalm's opening words are **its only words of comfort and support**.

Even godly people can suffer terribly. They may think very dark thoughts about their suffering and reach bleak conclusions about their future, because of **melancholy and weakened faith**.

The psalmist's greatest distress was his sense of **God's displeasure**.

Even those whom God loves may sometimes imagine themselves to be objects of his wrath.

No outward affliction is as painful as that fear.

The psalmist was probably describing his own troubles, but Henry also directs us to **Christ**.

We look to Jesus, **wounded and bruised for our sins**.

The divine wrath poured into his cup brought its greatest bitterness and weighed him down into darkness and the depths.

## Continuing in prayer while comfort is delayed (verses 10–18)

Departed souls may declare God's faithfulness, justice and lovingkindness.

But dead bodies can neither enjoy his favors with comfort nor praise him in response.

The psalmist resolved to **continue praying**, especially because deliverance had not come quickly.

When our prayers are not answered soon, we must not abandon them.

The more severe our troubles, the more earnest and serious our prayers should be.

Nothing grieves God's child more than losing the sense of **his presence**; nothing is more frightening than the thought of being cast away from him.

When the sun is covered by clouds, the earth grows dark. If the sun left the earth entirely, it would become a dungeon.

Likewise, even people destined to experience God's favor may for a time feel his terrors.

The psalmist suffered that fear deeply.

When Providence or death separates us from friends, we rightly consider that loss an affliction.

This was the desperate condition of a godly person.

Yet Henry sees the psalm's appeals as particularly fitting to **Jesus Christ**.

We should not imagine that Jesus suffered for us only in **Gethsemane and on Calvary**.

His whole earthly life involved work and sorrow.

He endured unparalleled affliction from his youth onward and was prepared throughout life for the death he would eventually taste.

No other person could share the redemptive sufferings through which others would be saved.

When the hour came, **everyone deserted him and fled**.

Blessed Jesus, we often forsake you. **Do not forsake us; do not take your Holy Spirit from us**.
