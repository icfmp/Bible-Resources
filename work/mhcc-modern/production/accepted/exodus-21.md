## Laws concerning servants (verses 1–11)

The laws in this chapter help explain the fifth and sixth commandments.

They reflect a society and customs very different from ours, and Henry says they are **not binding on us as civil laws**.

Nevertheless, they explain the enduring moral law and the basic principles of justice.

A person held in servitude pictured humanity's bondage to sin, Satan and the condemnation of the law.

People fell into this bondage when they robbed God of his glory by breaking his commands.

In the servant's release, we see a picture of the freedom Christ gives his people.

Christ, the Son of God, sets them free from slavery so that they become truly free.

That liberty is given freely, without money or payment, entirely through grace.

## Laws protecting life and restraining violence (verses 12–21)

God gives and sustains life through his providence.

Through his law he also protects it.

A deliberate murderer was to be taken even from God's altar for judgment.

But God provided cities of refuge for people who caused another person's death without intending any harm.

They had not committed deliberate murder, but had caused an accidental death while doing something lawful.

Children should hear the solemn judgment God's word pronounces upon ingratitude and disobedience toward parents.

If they have cursed a parent—even secretly in their hearts—or struck a parent, they should remember that God will hold them accountable unless they repent and flee to the Savior for refuge.

Parents, meanwhile, should raise their children carefully.

They should give them a good example, particularly in controlling their own tempers and praying for them.

They must take care not to provoke their children to anger.

In times of poverty, Israelites sometimes sold their own labor or that of their children.

Magistrates could sell some offenders as punishment, and creditors were sometimes allowed to sell people who could not pay what they owed.

But **kidnapping people for the purpose of enslaving them** is classed in the New Testament among the gravest crimes.

These laws also required compensation when someone was injured, even if the injury did not lead to death.

The gospel instructs masters to restrain their threats (Ephesians 6:9).

They should remember Job's question: What will I do when God rises to judge me? (Job 31:13–14).

## Further principles of justice (verses 22–36)

The cases in this passage give rules of justice for the society of Israel.

Their principles still help us judge similar situations.

They teach us to take great care not to wrong another person, whether directly or indirectly.

When we have caused a loss, we should be ready to make it good.

Indeed, we should desire that no one suffer loss because of what we have done.
