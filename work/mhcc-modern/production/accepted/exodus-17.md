# Exodus 17 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The Israelites murmur for water at Rephidim, God sendeth it out of the rock. | 1–7 |
| Amalek overcome, The prayers of Moses. | 8–16 |

## Water from the rock at Rephidim (verses 1–7)

The children of Israel traveled according to the Lord's command, guided by the pillar of cloud and fire.

Yet they arrived at a place where they had no water to drink.

We may be following our duty and still meet trouble. God's providence brings us into difficulties to test our faith and to glorify himself when he delivers us.

The people began to ask whether God was truly with them.

Scripture calls this **testing God**.

They distrusted him despite the remarkable proofs they had already received of his power and goodness.

Moses answered them gently.

It is foolish to respond to anger with more anger. That only makes a bad situation worse.

God graciously came to their help.

How wonderful is his patience with sinners who repeatedly provoke him!

To reveal his power as well as his compassion, and to make the provision an unmistakable miracle of mercy, he brought water out of a rock.

God can open springs for us where we would never expect them.

Those who keep to his path through the wilderness may trust him to provide what they need.

The miracle also directs us to Christ's grace.

The apostle says that the Rock **was Christ** (1 Corinthians 10:4). It was a picture of him.

God's curse could justly have fallen upon our guilty souls, but the Son of God was struck for us.

Let us ask and receive.

The water from the rock flowed in a constant, abundant supply.

However numerous the believers may be, Christ's Spirit has enough for them all.

Streams from the rock refreshed the desert and accompanied Israel on the way to Canaan.

Likewise, the grace flowing from Christ through his appointed ordinances refreshes our souls in the barren wilderness of this world until we reach glory.

The place received a new name.

But the name did not commemorate God's mercy in providing water. It remembered the people's sin in complaining.

*Massah* means **testing**, because they tested God.

*Meribah* means **quarreling**, because they argued with Moses.

Sin leaves a stain on a person's name.

## Amalek attacks Israel; Moses prays (verses 8–16)

Israel fought Amalek in necessary self-defense.

God equips his people and calls them to different kinds of service for the good of his church.

Joshua fought while Moses prayed.

Both served Israel.

Moses raised his staff as a banner to encourage the soldiers and as an appeal to God for help.

Eventually his arms grew tired.

Even the strongest arm will fail when held up for a long time.

Only God's hand can remain stretched out without tiring.

We do not read that Joshua's hands became heavy during the battle, but Moses' hands became heavy during prayer.

The more spiritual an act of service is, the more likely we are to become weary and lose heart in it.

God wanted Israel to learn that Moses—the very man they had been quarreling with—was doing more for their safety through his lifted hands than they were doing through their swords.

The outcome shifted as Moses raised or lowered his hands.

The church's cause likewise prospers according to the strength of its friends' faith and the fervor of their prayers.

Even Moses, God's servant, welcomed help.

We should neither be embarrassed to ask for assistance nor reluctant to offer it.

With others supporting his arms, Moses kept them steady until sunset.

The people could take courage from the sight of Joshua before them in battle and Moses above them on the hill.

Christ fulfills both roles for us.

He is our Joshua, the Captain of our salvation who fights our battles, and our Moses, who lives forever to intercede for us so that our faith may not fail.

Weapons formed against God's Israel cannot prosper for long. They will finally be broken.

God instructed Moses to write down what had happened.

The record was to preserve the memory of Amalek's bitter hatred and cruel attacks.

But it must also preserve the memory of God's deliverance.

It foretold that Amalek would eventually be utterly defeated and destroyed.

Henry sees that destruction as a picture of the final destruction of the enemies of Christ and his kingdom.
