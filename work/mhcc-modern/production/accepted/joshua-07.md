# Joshua 7 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The Israelites smitten at Ai. | 1–5 |
| Joshua's humiliation and prayer. | 6–9 |
| God instructs Joshua what to do. | 10–5 |
| Achan is detected, He is destroyed. | 16–26 |

## Israel is defeated at Ai (verses 1–5)

Achan secretly took some of Jericho's possessions that God had forbidden Israel to keep.

Love for the world is a deeply rooted bitterness, difficult to remove from the heart.

We must guard against sin, lest others be troubled or defiled through us (Hebrews 12:15).

We should also guard against joining others in wrongdoing, lest we share their guilt.

Our responsibility to care for one another includes helping prevent sin, because one person's wrongdoing may injure many others.

Israel's easy victory at Jericho led the people to despise their next enemy and to expect God to win every battle without their using proper means.

People can similarly twist the doctrine of divine grace and God's promises into excuses for laziness and self-indulgence.

We must work out our salvation, even though God himself is at work within us.

Israel's defeat became a costly victory for the Canaanites.

Through it, Israel was awakened, corrected and reconciled to God, while its enemies became more hardened in the path leading toward their own ruin.

## Joshua humbles himself and prays (verses 6–9)

Joshua was more concerned for the honor of God's name than even for Israel's fate.

That concern was an expression of the Spirit of adoption.

He feared that Israel's defeat would cast doubt upon God's wisdom, power, goodness and faithfulness.

He pleaded with the Lord.

We can present no better argument in prayer than: **“Lord, what will you do for your great name?”**

If God is glorified in everything, we may welcome whatever he chooses.

## God reveals the hidden sin (verses 10–15)

God told Joshua what needed to be removed from the camp before Israel could prosper again.

Danger and distress should call us to reform our lives.

We must look into our hearts, households and habits for anything God condemns: a hidden desire, unjust gain or an improper withholding of something owed to God or others.

We cannot expect to prosper spiritually while we preserve what the Lord commands us to abandon.

When sin is exposed, we must acknowledge God's righteous judgment.

He distinguishes with perfect knowledge between the innocent and the guilty.

Even when righteous people belong to the same tribe, family or household as the wicked, God will not finally judge them as though they were the same.

## Achan's crime is discovered and judged (verses 16–26)

How foolish it is for sinners to imagine that their wrongdoing can remain secret.

God has many ways of bringing hidden deeds to light.

When suffering comes, we should humbly ask him, as Job did, to show us why he is contending with us.

Achan's sin began with what he **saw**, just as Eve saw the forbidden fruit.

He allowed his heart to follow his eyes.

Those who want to avoid sinful actions must resist sinful desires, especially the craving for wealth.

With the eyes of faith, Achan would have recognized that the possessions were under God's ban and feared to take them.

Looking only with bodily eyes, he saw attractive objects and coveted them.

After taking them, he hid them.

They became a burden he could not openly use.

Temptations look very different from a distance than they do after we have yielded to them.

What seems delightful while we commit a sin becomes bitter when we remember it afterward.

Sin deceives.

People who rob God rob themselves of peace.

And their wrongdoing harms others around them.

Achan did not die alone. His sons and daughters died with him.

Henry considers it **probable**, not certain, that they knew of the hidden possessions and had helped conceal them.

The consequences of one person's sin may be fatal even to family members.

One sinner destroys much good.

What, then, will the final judgment be like?

Let us flee to Jesus Christ, the sinner's Friend, for refuge.

Achan's confession reveals the progress of sin from desire, to action, to concealment—a pattern present in many offenses against God's law and against the sacrifice of Jesus Christ.
