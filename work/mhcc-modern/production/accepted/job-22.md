# Job 22 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Eliphaz shows that a man's goodness profits not God. | 1–4 |
| Job accused of oppression. | 5–14 |
| The world before the flood. | 15–20 |
| Eliphaz exhorts Job to repentance. | 21–30 |

## Eliphaz says God gains nothing from human goodness (verses 1–4)

Eliphaz misunderstood Job's complaints as an accusation that God was unjust to afflict him.

Job had not actually meant that.

Eliphaz's teachings here were **wrongly applied to Job**, yet their main principle was true.

God is not indebted to us when he gives us good things.

Human godliness does not enrich him or provide something he lacks.

The benefit of religion to human beings is infinitely greater than any loss we sustain through obedience.

God is sovereign and does not have to justify his choices to created beings.

Nevertheless, his character is entirely wise, righteous, faithful, good and merciful.

He delights in holiness that reflects his own character and in **the fruit of his Spirit**.

He accepts the humble believer's grateful service but rejects the proud claims of people who think their goodness earns his favor.

## Eliphaz accuses Job of oppressing others (verses 5–14)

Eliphaz leveled serious accusations against Job.

He had **no real grounds** for them except his belief that God must have punished Job as he always punishes wicked people.

Eliphaz therefore charged Job with using his former wealth and influence to harm and oppress others.

Henry presents these statements as **baseless accusations**, not as facts about Job.

## Eliphaz invokes the people before the flood (verses 15–20)

Eliphaz asked Job to consider the ancient paths of wicked people and the destruction toward which they led.

That history is a warning we should study so we do not follow the same path.

But when others suffer and we are spared, we must not boast over them or congratulate ourselves, as Eliphaz did.

We ought instead to **thank God for mercy and take the events as warnings to ourselves**.

## Eliphaz tells Job to repent (verses 21–30)

Eliphaz's counsel wrongly implied that Job had never truly known God and that conversion would necessarily restore his worldly prosperity.

Much of the counsel was good in itself.

But its application was based on the false supposition that Job was a stranger and enemy to God.

We must avoid slandering our brothers and sisters.

If we suffer unjust accusations, remember Job's treatment—and even more, **how Jesus was insulted**.

These examples can strengthen our patience.

We should still examine whether any part of the criticism has merit.

Let us live carefully and avoid even the appearance of wrongdoing.
