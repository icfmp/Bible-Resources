# Psalm 77 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The psalmist's troubles and temptation. | 1–10 |
| He encourages himself by the remembrance of God's help of his people. | 11–20 |

## Distress, prayer and dangerous conclusions (verses 1–10)

Days of trouble should be **days of prayer**.

When God seems to withdraw his presence, we must continue seeking him.

The psalmist did not look for a distraction in work or amusement. He sought **God himself, his favor and his grace**.

People troubled in spirit should pray, not merely brood over their distress.

The psalmist concentrated so much on suffering that his usual means of consolation increased his sorrow.

When he remembered God, his thoughts turned only to **divine justice and anger**.

His spirit felt overwhelmed.

Yet we should not let grief over comforts lost make us ungrateful for comforts that remain.

He tried to recall the promises and past mercies that had sustained him during earlier difficulties.

The words express the condition of a grieving soul that feels deserted and walks in darkness—a condition even believers may experience (**Isaiah 50:10**).

Few things wound a godly soul more deeply than the thought that God is angry with it.

During dark periods, Christians may be tempted to draw **false conclusions about their own spiritual state and the future of God's church**.

We must resist these thoughts.

Let **Scripture answer the questions unbelief raises**.

A troubled fountain may eventually clear again.

Remembering former times of comfort can encourage fresh hope and bring relief.

Such fears arise from weakness in faith.

Despair and distrust are too often weaknesses of believers during affliction.

When unbelief stirs within us, we should recognize it sorrowfully and suppress it.

## Remembering God's works (verses 11–20)

The remembrance of God's works is a powerful remedy against distrust of his goodness and promises.

He is God and **does not change**.

His way is in the sanctuary.

We can be certain that everything he does is holy.

Nevertheless, his purposes are often like **deep waters that cannot be sounded**, or **the course of a ship that leaves no trace**.

God brought Israel out of Egypt.

This deliverance pictured the greater redemption accomplished in the fullness of time through Christ—**both by the price he paid and by the power he exercises**.

If we have entertained doubts about God's kindness, turn promptly to meditate on the One who **did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all**.

How can we doubt that with Christ he will graciously give everything necessary for our salvation?
