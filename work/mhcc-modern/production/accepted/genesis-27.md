# Genesis 27 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Isaac sends Esau to hunt for food. | 1–5 |
| Rebekah teaches Jacob how to obtain the blessing. | 6–17 |
| Jacob pretends to be Esau and receives the blessing. | 18–29 |
| Isaac trembles; Esau pleads for a blessing. | 30–40 |
| Esau threatens Jacob; Rebekah sends Jacob away. | 41–46 |

## Isaac intends to bless Esau (verses 1–5)

The promises of the Messiah and the land of Canaan had passed down to Isaac.

Isaac was now about 135 years old, and his sons about 75.

Yet he did not give proper attention to God's word about his two sons: the older would serve the younger.

Instead, he decided to give Esau, his older son, all the honor and authority included in the promise.

We are often tempted to make decisions according to our own reasoning rather than God's revelation.

When we do, we frequently lose our way.

## Rebekah persuades Jacob to deceive Isaac (verses 6–17)

Rebekah knew that God intended the blessing for Jacob, and she expected him to receive it.

But she sinned against Isaac by deceiving him.

She sinned against Jacob by tempting him to do wrong.

She also placed a stumbling block before Esau, giving him an excuse to hate both Jacob and the true religion.

Everyone involved deserved blame.

This was one of those dishonest methods people adopt when trying to bring about God's promises, as though a good outcome could justify sinful means.

Many have done wrong while imagining they were helping Christ's cause.

God's answer to such reasoning is the same as his command to Abraham: “I am God Almighty; walk before me and be blameless.”

Rebekah's words were especially reckless: “Let the curse fall on me, my son.”

Christ has borne the curse of the law for all who accept the yoke of the gospel's commands.

But it is far too daring for any mere creature to say, “Let the curse fall on me.”

## Jacob receives the blessing through deception (verses 18–29)

Jacob succeeded in obtaining the blessing, though not without difficulty.

The blessing was expressed in broad and general terms.

It did not explicitly mention the special mercies promised in God's covenant with Abraham.

Perhaps that was because Isaac believed he was blessing Esau.

He surely knew that Esau had despised the best things of God.

And Isaac's affection for Esau, which led him to disregard God's will, must have weakened his own faith in those promises.

We should therefore not be surprised that his blessing lacked much of the spiritual richness it might otherwise have contained.

Its limited scope reflected his own state of mind.

## Esau's grief and Isaac's renewed blessing (verses 30–40)

When Esau learned that Jacob had received the blessing, he cried out with deep and bitter grief.

A day is coming when those who now despise covenant blessings and sell their spiritual inheritance for something worthless will beg for those blessings in vain.

When Isaac realized he had been deceived, he trembled violently.

Those who follow their own affections rather than God's revealed will bring confusion and distress upon themselves.

But Isaac soon recovered his composure.

He confirmed the blessing he had given Jacob: “I have blessed him, and he shall be blessed.”

Those who give up wisdom and grace, faith and a good conscience, in exchange for worldly honor, wealth, or pleasure have already judged themselves unworthy of the greater blessing.

They may pretend to be deeply concerned about it, but their final judgment will reveal their true choice.

Esau received an ordinary, earthly blessing. It was the kind of blessing he had desired.

Many people are deceived into ruin by a vague wish to be happy, without choosing the right goal or following the right path to reach it.

Multitudes go to hell with their mouths full of good wishes.

The great difference is that Esau's blessing said nothing that pointed to Christ.

Without Christ, the richness of the earth and the spoils of war will bring little lasting benefit.

So Isaac, by faith, blessed both sons according to the futures appointed to them.

## Esau threatens Jacob, who leaves home (verses 41–46)

Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing he had received.

In doing so, he followed the way of Cain.

Cain murdered his brother because Abel received the favor with God that Cain had made himself unworthy to receive.

Esau intended to prevent Jacob and his descendants from receiving authority by killing him.

People may rage against God's purposes, but they cannot change them.

Rebekah warned Jacob of the danger and told him to leave for his own safety.

Even with the most promising children, we should not presume too much on their wisdom or self-control.

We must take care to keep them away from danger and opportunities for evil.

As we read this chapter, we should remember that even the best people are examples to follow only so far as their conduct agrees with God's law.

We must never do evil so that good may come.

God overruled the wrong actions recorded here to accomplish his own purposes.

Yet his judgment upon those actions can be seen in the painful consequences suffered by everyone involved.

Jacob's special privilege was to carry spiritual blessings to all nations.

Christ, the Savior of the world, would be born within one particular family.

God chose Jacob's family rather than Esau's, according to his own wise pleasure.

He knows best what is right and has the unquestionable right to give his favors as he chooses (Romans 9:12–15).
