# Genesis 26 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Famine leads Isaac to Gerar. | 1–5 |
| Isaac denies that Rebekah is his wife and is rebuked by Abimelech. | 6–11 |
| Isaac becomes wealthy and the Philistines envy him. | 12–17 |
| Isaac digs wells and receives God's blessing. | 18–25 |
| Abimelech makes a covenant with Isaac. | 26–33 |
| Esau's marriages grieve his parents. | 34–35 |

## Isaac remains faithful during famine (verses 1–5)

Isaac had been raised to trust God's promise that Canaan would belong to him and his descendants.

Now famine had come upon the land, yet Isaac continued to hold fast to the covenant.

Difficult circumstances cannot diminish the true worth of God's promises to a believer.

If God has promised to be with us and we are where he wants us to be, only our own unbelief and distrust can rob us of our comfort.

Abraham's obedience to God's command was evidence of the faith through which he, a sinner, had been justified before God.

It was also the fruit of the love through which genuine faith works.

God declared his approval of Abraham's obedience to encourage others, especially Isaac, to follow his example.

## Isaac deceives Abimelech (verses 6–11)

There is nothing about Isaac's denial that Rebekah was his wife that deserves imitation—or even excuse.

Isaac faced the same temptation that had overcome his father, and on two occasions.

That made his conduct even more blameworthy.

The falls of those who lived before us are like rocks on which ships have run aground.

Scripture records them as warning buoys to keep later sailors from the same danger.

This Abimelech was not the ruler who lived in Abraham's time, though both rulers behaved rightly.

When professing believers fall into sin, they bring shame upon themselves before people who do not profess true faith.

## Isaac becomes prosperous, and the Philistines envy him (verses 12–17)

God blessed Isaac with a great harvest.

This should encourage poor tenants who work another person's land honestly and diligently.

The Philistines envied Isaac.

Here we see the emptiness of worldly riches: the more people possess, the more they may be envied, criticized, and harmed.

We also see how corrupt human nature can be.

What a terrible attitude it is to be miserable because someone else has received something good!

The Philistines drove Isaac out of their land.

Heavenly wisdom teaches us to give up even what we have a right to, when doing so avoids quarrels.

If people wrongfully drive us from one place, the Lord can provide room for us somewhere else.

## Isaac digs wells and worships at Beersheba (verses 18–25)

Isaac faced repeated opposition as he dug wells.

Two were named for conflict and hatred.

This reveals the nature of worldly possessions: they become causes of quarrels.

Even peaceful people who refuse to start disputes cannot always prevent others from disputing with them.

What a blessing it is to have plenty of water, especially when we can enjoy it without fighting for it!

The more common a blessing becomes, the more reason we have to notice it and give thanks.

At last Isaac dug a well that no one disputed.

Those who strive to live quietly often succeed in finding peace.

When other people prove false or unkind, God remains faithful and gracious.

Indeed, he often shows that faithfulness most clearly when we are disappointed by others.

On the very night Isaac arrived at Beersheba, tired and troubled, God brought comfort to his soul.

Those who know God is with them can move from one place to another with confidence.

## Abimelech makes peace with Isaac (verses 26–33)

When people's ways please the Lord, he can make even their enemies live at peace with them (Proverbs 16:7).

The hearts of kings are in God's hands.

He can turn them toward his people whenever he chooses.

There is nothing wrong with being careful when dealing with those who have treated us unfairly.

But Isaac did not insist on rehearsing every wrong they had done him.

He willingly made peace and entered into friendship with them.

True religion teaches us to be good neighbors and, as far as we can, to live at peace with everyone.

God's providence favored Isaac and blessed his work.

## Esau's marriages grieve his parents (verses 34–35)

Esau acted foolishly in taking two wives at the same time.

He acted even more foolishly by marrying Canaanite women.

They were strangers to Abraham's covenant blessing and belonged to the people under Noah's curse.

His marriages grieved his parents because he had neither asked for nor received their counsel and consent.

They also grieved them because he married outside the worship of the true God.

Children who deliberately bring such distress upon godly parents have little reason to expect God's blessing.
