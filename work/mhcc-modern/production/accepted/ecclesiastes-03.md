# Ecclesiastes 3 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

## The changing seasons of human life (verses 1–10)

Anyone seeking lasting happiness in an ever-changing world will be disappointed.

It is our duty and wisdom to accept the circumstances God appoints and live faithfully in them.

His whole plan for governing the world will eventually prove **wise, just and good**.

We should therefore seize favorable opportunities for every good purpose and task.

The time when we must die is coming quickly.

Meanwhile human life is full of labor and sorrow.

Yet God allows us to experience this work so that **we always have something useful to do**.

No one was sent into the world merely to be idle.

## God's purposes do not change (verses 11–15)

Things truly are as God has made them, **not necessarily as they appear to us**.

Worldly concerns so fill our hearts and minds that we often lack the time or desire to notice God's hand in events.

The world not only takes possession of our affections; it also leads us to form unfair thoughts about the beauty of God's works.

We are mistaken if we think we were born only for ourselves.

**Our task is to do good during a brief and uncertain life.**

Because our time for such service is limited, we must use it carefully.

Being content with God's Providence means trusting that **all things work together for good for those who love him**.

God acts so that people will reverence him.

As the world has been, so it is now and so it will be.

Every change and temptation we meet belongs to the kinds of experiences shared by humanity.

We are not enduring some entirely unprecedented form of testing.

## Worldly power cannot escape final judgment (verses 16–22)

Without reverence for the Lord, human authority is empty.

When judges cast aside fear of God, they misuse their power.

But **another Judge stands at the door**.

God has appointed a time to correct injustices, even if we do not yet see that correction.

Solomon appears to wish that people would recognize this: by choosing the present world as their ultimate portion, they bring their lives down toward the level of animals, while still enduring the anxieties of this life and awaiting an account of their conduct.

Both humans and animals return to the dust from which they were taken.

How little reason we have to boast about our bodies or physical accomplishments!

But the **human rational soul** and an animal's life are fundamentally different, even if few people think properly about the difference.

Henry explains that **the human spirit goes upward to judgment**, where its eternal state is fixed in unchanging happiness or misery.

The life of an animal, by contrast, perishes with the animal's death.

How pitiable are those whose greatest wish is simply to die as animals die!

Our crucial question is how **eternal existence** may become **eternal happiness**.

That is the great purpose of revelation.

**Jesus is revealed as the Son of God and the Hope of sinners.**
