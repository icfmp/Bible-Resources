# Genesis 21 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Isaac is born; Sarah rejoices. | 1–8 |
| Ishmael mocks Isaac. | 9–13 |
| Hagar and Ishmael are sent away and receive help from an angel. | 14–21 |
| Abimelech makes a covenant with Abraham. | 22–34 |

## Isaac's birth and Sarah's joy (verses 1–8)

Few people in Old Testament times were born with such great hopes resting upon them as Isaac.

In this he was a picture of Christ, the Seed whom the holy God had promised so long before and whom godly people had awaited for so many generations.

Isaac was born according to God's promise, at the very time God had appointed.

Whatever mercy God has promised will certainly come at the time he has chosen—and that time is best.

*Isaac* means “laughter.”

There was good reason for that name (Genesis 17:17; 18:13).

When the sun of comfort rises over our souls, it is good to remember how welcome the first light of dawn once was.

When Sarah first heard God's promise, she laughed with doubt and distrust.

When God gives us blessings we had almost given up hoping for, we ought to remember with sorrow and shame how we once doubted his power and his promise.

Now God's mercy filled Sarah with joy and wonder.

The blessings God gives his covenant people go far beyond what they or anyone else could have imagined.

Who could have expected him to do so much for those who deserve so little—and indeed deserve his judgment?

Who would have imagined that God would send his Son to die for us, and his Spirit to make us holy, and his angels to care for us?

Who could have imagined the pardon of such great sins, the acceptance of such insignificant service, and the admission of such unworthy creatures into his covenant?

Scripture gives a brief account of Isaac's infancy.

We should recognize God's special care and tenderness in the feeding and nursing of children and in their preservation through the dangers of early childhood (Psalm 22:9–10; Hosea 11:1–2).

## Ishmael mocks Isaac (verses 9–13)

This family incident teaches us not to rely on outward privileges or on anything we have done ourselves.

Instead, let us seek the blessings of the new covenant through faith in its divine Guarantor.

Ishmael's mocking was persecution.

He showed wicked contempt for God's covenant and promise and acted with malice toward Isaac.

God notices what children say and do, even while they play.

He will hold them accountable when they do wrong, even if their parents overlook it.

Mockery is a serious sin that greatly offends God.

The children of the promise should therefore expect to be mocked.

Abraham was deeply troubled.

He grieved over Ishmael's misconduct and over Sarah's demand for such a severe punishment.

But God showed Abraham that Isaac must become the ancestor of the promised Seed.

Ishmael was therefore to be sent away, lest he corrupt Isaac's conduct or try to take away his rights.

Abraham's covenant descendants were to be a people set apart, not mixed with those outside the covenant.

Sarah did not understand the full meaning of what she was asking.

Yet God directed her words toward his own wise purpose.

## Hagar and Ishmael receive help in the wilderness (verses 14–21)

If Hagar and Ishmael had behaved rightly in Abraham's household, they might have remained there.

But they had misused their privileges and were justly punished.

When we abuse privileges, we lose our claim to them.

People who do not appreciate their blessings while they have them may come to understand their value only when those blessings are taken away.

Hagar and Ishmael soon found themselves in distress in the wilderness.

Scripture does not say that all their supplies had run out, or that Abraham had sent them away without money.

But their water was gone.

They had lost their way, and in the heat Ishmael was quickly overcome with exhaustion and thirst.

Knowing that God is ready to help us in trouble must not make us less active in helping ourselves.

On the contrary, it should encourage us to do everything we can.

God repeated the promise concerning Ishmael to give Hagar a reason to rise and care for him.

We should work diligently for the good of children and young people, remembering that we cannot know what great work God may intend them to accomplish.

The angel showed Hagar where she could find water.

Many people have good reason to be comforted, yet continue mourning because they cannot see that reason.

There is a well of living water close at hand in the covenant of grace.

But they do not notice it until the same God who opened their eyes to see their wound opens them again to see the remedy.

Paran was a wild country, fitting for a wild man like Ishmael.

Those born merely according to the flesh are content with the wilderness of this world.

But the children of the promise long for the heavenly Canaan and cannot finally rest until they reach it.

Yet God was with Ishmael, and his outward prosperity came from that divine care.

## Abraham and Abimelech make a covenant (verses 22–34)

Abimelech was convinced that God would keep his promises to Abraham.

It is wise to form good relationships with those whom God has blessed.

We also ought to repay kindness to those who have shown kindness to us.

Wells are scarce and precious in eastern countries.

Abraham made sure his right to the well was acknowledged, so that future quarrels could be avoided.

We cannot demand more of an honest person than a willingness to make things right as soon as a wrong is known.

Abraham now lived among good neighbors, so he remained there for a long time.

He not only practiced his religion privately but professed it openly.

He called upon the Lord as the everlasting God, probably in the grove he had planted as a place of prayer.

He maintained public worship, so that his neighbors could join him.

Godly people should do everything they can to help others become godly too.

Wherever we live as strangers, we must neither neglect nor be ashamed of worshipping the LORD.
