# Numbers 5 — Modern English

## Cleansing the camp and making restitution (verses 1–10)

Israel's camp had to be kept clean.

The church's purity deserves just as much attention as its peace and order.

Those who were ceremonially defiled had to be separated from the camp.

Wisdom from above is pure first, and then peaceable.

The more openly a home or family professes faith, the more carefully it should put away wickedness.

When one person cheats or exploits another, the offense is also committed against the Lord, who commands us to act justly.

What should someone do when an awakened conscience recalls dishonesty committed many years earlier?

The person must confess the sin both to God and to the neighbor who was harmed.

This can be humiliating, especially if it requires admitting a past lie. But it remains necessary.

Both the wrong against God and the financial loss suffered by the neighbor must be addressed.

Restitution alone is not enough without genuine faith and repentance.

And as long as someone knowingly keeps what was obtained through injustice, the guilt remains.

Neither religious sacrifices, prayers nor tears can erase an offense that the sinner is deliberately continuing to commit.

This principle is taught both by sound reasoning and by God's word.

It exposes hypocrisy, while guiding a tender conscience toward faithful action and the inward peace that comes through faith in Christ.

## The trial of jealousy (verses 11–31)

This law encouraged Israelite wives to avoid conduct that gave reasonable grounds for suspicion.

It also restrained husbands from acting cruelly because of suspicion alone.

The guilty were not to escape judgment, and innocent wives were not to be condemned unjustly.

When there was no evidence sufficient to decide the case, the woman was called to appeal solemnly to the God who knows every heart.

If she was guilty, she could not sincerely accept the oath and drink the prescribed water unless she refused to believe God or defiantly disregarded his justice.

The water was called **bitter** because it brought a curse upon the guilty.

Sin, too, is bitter.

Anyone who pursues forbidden pleasures should remember the misery that follows them.

Henry draws three lessons.

**First**, God knows every secret sin.

He sometimes exposes hidden wrongdoing even during this life.

A final day is coming when God will judge human secrets through Jesus Christ (Romans 2:16).

**Second**, God will judge sexual immorality and adultery.

We no longer have this ordeal involving bitter water, but we have God's word, which should warn us no less seriously.

Sinful lust ends in bitterness.

**Third**, God will make the innocence of the innocent known.

The same act of providence can help some people while harming others, according to God's righteous purposes.
