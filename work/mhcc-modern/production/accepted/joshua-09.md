# Joshua 9 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The kings combine against Israel. | 1, 2 |
| The Gibeonites apply for peace. | 3–13 |
| They obtain peace, but are soon detected. | 14–21 |
| The Gibeonites are to be bondmen. | 22–27 |

## Canaan's kings unite against Israel (verses 1 and 2)

Until this point, the Canaanites had mainly fought to defend themselves. Now their rulers planned to attack Israel together.

Although those nations had often been enemies of one another, they put aside their quarrels to oppose God's people.

How much better it would be if God's people learned to put the good of the whole community above private interests.

They should settle their disputes and unite in resisting the enemies of God's kingdom.

## The Gibeonites seek peace (verses 3–13)

Most neighboring nations heard of God's works and responded by making war.

The Gibeonites heard the same news and sought peace.

The gospel likewise has different effects on different people.

For some it is the fragrance of life; for others, the fragrance of death (2 Corinthians 2:16).

The same sun softens wax and hardens clay.

The Gibeonites' deception was **not justified**.

We must never do evil in the hope that good will come from it.

Had they truly presented themselves as people submitting to Israel's God, Henry thinks Joshua might have been directed by God to spare them.

But after telling their first lie about coming from a far country, they needed more lies to support it.

One falsehood leads to another, and then another. The road of deceit runs steadily downhill.

Yet their **faith and prudence** in seeking mercy deserve praise.

By submitting to Israel, they submitted to the God of Israel and were turning away from their old idolatry.

We can do no better than throw ourselves upon the mercy of the perfectly good God.

Repentance is the way to meet and escape judgment.

Let us follow the Gibeonites by seeking peace with God, humbled over sin.

Let us become servants of Jesus, our blessed Joshua, and live.

## Joshua and the leaders keep their oath (verses 14–21)

The Israelites examined the Gibeonites' provisions and quickly decided their story must be true.

They did not ask God for direction through his word and prayer.

When we hurry without consulting him, our haste often leads to trouble.

The lie was soon discovered.

But there was an important distinction.

An oath requiring someone to commit sin is not binding, because no promise can make an evil act right.

Yet sparing Canaanites who sincerely submitted and abandoned idolatry was **not** unlawful.

Joshua's promise therefore remained binding, despite the fraud.

A citizen of Zion keeps a sworn promise even at personal cost (Psalm 15:4).

Joshua and the leaders did not try to escape their oath by asking Eleazar to release them from it or by claiming that promises to deceptive people did not matter.

Their example teaches us to keep promises conscientiously and honor the agreements we have made.

## The Gibeonites become servants (verses 22–27)

The Gibeonites did not attempt to justify their lie.

They explained that they had been afraid of losing their lives.

Their fear was not simply of Israel's military strength; it was of God's power, which they knew was acting against them.

Joshua assigned them permanent service connected with God's house.

Even humble work becomes honorable when it is done in the Lord's service.

Let us likewise submit to Jesus, our true Joshua, saying: “We are in your hand. Do with us whatever is good and right, but save our souls.”

If he appoints us to carry a cross and serve him, the work will not be shameful or cause us lasting grief.

The humblest service in his house comes with the promise of dwelling with him.

We do not approach Christ with only a remote possibility of acceptance.

He invites sinners to come near and assures us that he will never turn away the one who comes to him.

Even the hard and humbling demands that test the sincerity of our faith will ultimately benefit us.
