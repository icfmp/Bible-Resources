# 2 Kings 14 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Amaziah's good reign. | 1–7 |
| Amaziah provokes Jehoash king of Israel, and is overcome. | 8–14 |
| He is slain by conspirators. | 15–22 |
| Wicked reign of Jeroboam II. | 23–29 |

## Amaziah begins well (verses 1–7)

Amaziah began his reign well but did not continue as he began.

It is not enough merely to repeat the good actions of faithful ancestors because those actions have become customary.

We must act from the same inward **faith and devotion**, with the same sincerity and determination that led them to do what was right.

## Amaziah challenges Jehoash and loses (verses 8–14)

Soon after the kingdoms divided, Judah suffered greatly from Israel's hostility.

After Asa's time, Judah suffered still more from friendship and alliances with Israel.

Now hostility returned.

A humble person might almost smile to hear two proud and contemptuous rulers compete in belittling each other.

Unholy success produces pride.

Pride leads to conflict.

And proud people find the pride of others unbearable.

These tendencies cause trouble and sin in private life.

When they govern rulers, however, entire nations suffer.

Jehoash warned Amaziah about the folly of his challenge: **“Your heart has made you proud.”**

The root of every sin is in the heart.

Outward events, the circumstances Providence brings, or the opportunities we meet do not themselves make people proud, self-confident or discontented.

Such reactions come from **the heart**.

## Amaziah is killed (verses 15–22)

Amaziah outlived the king who had defeated him by **fifteen years**.

He was later killed by his own subjects.

Azariah, also called Uzziah, appears to have been very young when his father died.

Henry understands his regnal years as counted from that event, although he thinks Azariah was not fully established as king until **eleven years later**.

This is Henry's chronological explanation, not an alteration of the biblical dates.

## Jeroboam II's wicked reign (verses 23–29)

God raised up the prophet **Jonah** and through him announced merciful purposes toward Israel.

The continued presence of faithful ministers is a sign that God has not completely abandoned his people.

Henry gives **two reasons** why God granted Israel victories.

**First**, the people's suffering was so severe that they became objects of his compassion.

**Second**, the decree for their destruction had not yet been issued.

Israel had known many prophets.

But, according to Henry, none had left **written prophetic books** until this period.

**Hosea** began prophesying during Jeroboam's reign.

**Amos** was active around the same time; **Micah** came soon afterward, followed by **Isaiah** in the days of Ahaz and Hezekiah.

Even during the church's darkest and most corrupt periods, God raises up people who shine brightly.

Through their preaching and example they guide their own generation.

And through the writings of some of them, they continue to give light to later generations.
