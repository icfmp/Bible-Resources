# Psalm 69 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| David complains of great distress. | 1–12 |
| And begs for succour. | 13–21 |
| He declares the judgments of God. | 22–29 |
| He concludes with joy and praise. | 30–36 |

## The sufferings of David and Christ (verses 1–12)

We should regularly contemplate **the Person who suffered** in this psalm, and ask not merely what he suffered but **why**.

Meditation on Christ's suffering should humble us for our sins, awaken us to their danger and fill us with gratitude and love that move us to live for the One who died to save us.

When afflicted, entrust the soul to God. This keeps suffering from making us bitter or driving us toward despair.

David was wrongfully hated, but these words apply **more fully to Jesus Christ**.

Because injustice is common, we should not be surprised when people become our enemies without cause.

Our concern should be that we never do wrong ourselves. Then we can better bear wrong committed against us.

Through his blood Christ made **satisfaction for our sins**, restoring what he had not taken away and paying a debt that was ours.

Even when innocent of human accusations, we must acknowledge that before God our own sins deserve judgment.

Our sins spring from folly and are all committed in the Lord's sight.

David suffered rejection from friends and family.

Christ also faced unbelief from his brothers and desertion by his disciples.

He made satisfaction not only through the honors he rendered to God, but also by submitting to extraordinary public dishonor.

If zeal for God's truth, commandments or worship brings mockery, we should not lose courage.

## Prayer when trouble overwhelms us (verses 13–21)

Whatever deep waters of affliction, temptation or oppression seem to engulf us, we should persevere in prayer for the Lord's salvation.

Even in severe outward trouble, signs of God's favor can keep the soul from sinking.

If we continue thinking rightly of God during hardships, we can trust him to deal wisely and kindly with us.

When we suffer reproach for Christ's name, it comforts us that **he knows our shame**.

A damaged reputation pains someone who values a good name.

Yet being considered worthy to suffer for Jesus should prevent that pain from overwhelming us.

The psalm foretold aspects of Christ's suffering so accurately that its fulfillment confirms Scripture's divine truth and **Jesus as the true Messiah**.

The **vinegar and gall** given to him were a faint image of the far more bitter cup he drank so that we could receive the cup of salvation.

We should expect little from human comforters and much from **the God of all consolation**.

## Divine judgment and the offer of mercy (verses 22–29)

Henry understands these words as **prophecies of judgment upon Christ's persecutors**, not as an instruction for private revenge.

**Verses 22 and 23** are applied by Paul to judgment on unbelieving Jews in **Romans 11:9–10**.

When bodily pleasures and ordinary supports of life become the **food and fuel of sin**, even the table becomes a snare.

Those people refused to see the light because they loved darkness.

Their punishment was that they could no longer see clearly: God left them to the desires and hardening of their own hearts.

Everyone who rejects the great salvation freely offered by God must take seriously the warning of divine judgment.

Yet even those who have greatly multiplied their sins **may still find mercy through the Mediator's righteousness**.

God excludes no one from that righteousness who does not, through unbelief, exclude themselves.

Those too proud or stubborn to accept it choose their own ruin.

A person poor and sorrowful with God's blessing is better off than someone rich and cheerful while under his judgment.

Henry also applies this contrast to **Christ**.

When he lived on earth, he was a Man of Sorrows with nowhere to rest his head. Yet God exalted him.

Let guilty and afflicted sinners call upon God: **his salvation lifts them up**.

## Joy, praise and the future of Zion (verses 30–36)

The psalm began with sorrow and complaint but ends in **holy praise**.

Humble thanksgiving pleases God more than the most impressive and expensive outward sacrifice.

The humble will seek him and rejoice; those who seek him **through Christ** will live and receive consolation.

God will accomplish great things for his gospel church.

Let everyone who loves his people rejoice in that prospect.

Future generations will serve him on earth, and his servants will inherit **his heavenly kingdom**.

Those who love his name will dwell before him forever.

God did not spare his own Son but gave him for us all. With him he will freely give us everything needed for salvation.

Lord, great Restorer of ruined places, arise and **turn ungodliness away from your people**.
