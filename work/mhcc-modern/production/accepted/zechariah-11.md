# Zechariah 11 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| Destruction to come upon the Jews. | 1–3 |
| The Lord's dealing with the Jews. | 4–14 |
| The emblem and curse of a foolish shepherd. | 15–17 |

## The shepherds and their people are warned (verses 1–3)

Henry understands these figurative words as predicting **the destruction of Jerusalem and of the Jewish church and nation**, later plainly foretold by Jesus as the time approached.

If even mighty cedars fall, how can lesser fir trees imagine they will stand?

When wise and godly people fall into sin, or influential and wealthy people fall into trouble, their downfall warns those with fewer advantages.

A nation is in a sad condition when people who ought to be its **shepherds** behave instead like **young lions**.

The pride of the Jordan was the thick vegetation along its banks.

When floods covered the banks, lions emerged roaring.

In Henry's application, Jerusalem's destruction should therefore warn **other churches**.

## The Good Shepherd and the two staffs (verses 4–14)

Henry explains these words as announcing **Christ's coming in judgment to a Jewish nation and religious community that had become deeply corrupt**.

People whose minds are darkened may do wrong while justifying themselves.

But God does not pronounce innocent those who approve their own wrongdoing.

How can we ask God to bless **dishonest methods of gaining wealth**, or thank him for the profits of such conduct?

The community had experienced serious religious decline but failed to recognize it.

The **Good Shepherd** would care for his flock, giving special attention to the poor.

The prophet appears to have used two staffs.

The first, **Beauty**, represented the Jewish nation's privileges within its national covenant.

The second, **Bands**, represented the unity that had held the flock together.

Yet many preferred false teachers.

Worldly-mindedness and friendship with sin place people in hostility to God.

The prophet requested his wages and was paid **thirty pieces of silver**.

By divine direction, he threw them to the potter, rejecting the sum as a contemptible price.

Henry interprets this as foreshadowing **Judas's bargain to betray Christ and the later use of the money**.

Nothing ruins a people more surely than the collapse of their **mutual bonds of fellowship**.

As sin increases, love grows cold, and disagreements turn into public conflict.

People who have provoked God against themselves should not be surprised if they begin to fight one another.

**Deliberate contempt for Christ** brings ruin.

If professing Christians properly valued him, they would not quarrel so fiercely over trivial matters.

## The foolish shepherd abandons the flock (verses 15–17)

Having shown what happens when the Good Shepherd's care is rejected, the vision shows the further misery of people abused by **foolish shepherds**.

Henry relates the portrait to the **scribes and Pharisees as Jesus described them**.

Such leaders do nothing to uphold the weak or encourage those who are discouraged.

They look only for personal comfort while treating the flock cruelly.

An **idol shepherd** may dress and appear like a true shepherd, receive submission and enjoy generous support.

Yet through neglect or wicked example, the shepherd lets the sheep perish.

Henry says this pattern fits leaders in **many churches and nations**, while interpreting the prophecy as having a particularly severe application to **some Jewish teachers**.

Those who deceive others toward destruction will themselves receive severe condemnation.
