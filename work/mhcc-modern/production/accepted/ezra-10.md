## Ezra encourages reform (verses 1–5)

**Shecaniah** acknowledged the nation's guilt.

The situation was sad but not hopeless.

The sickness was threatening but not incurable.

As the people began to mourn, a spirit of repentance appeared to have been poured out.

There was hope that the Lord would forgive and show mercy.

The sin that troubles us in the **right way** will not destroy us.

Even in dark times, we should notice what gives us hope as well as what warns us.

Grace provides grounds for hope even when we recognize great guilt before God.

The matter was clear.

As far as possible, what had been done wrongly needed to be undone.

Genuine repentance requires putting sin away with the settled intention of never returning to it.

Goods unjustly obtained must be restored.

**Rise and act courageously.**

In this situation, weeping was good, but **reform was better**.

Henry distinguishes the ancient law from the gospel era.

He teaches that believers should not enter marriages unequally yoked with unbelievers.

But he also expressly says that **such marriages are not now nullified** as they were under Israel's former separation laws, because the gospel has ended the separation between Jews and Gentiles.

The record must therefore not be taken as an instruction to dissolve existing marriages today.

## Ezra assembles the people (verses 6–14)

There is hope when people recognize not merely that abandoning sin would be good but that it is **necessary**.

God's mercy is so rich and his redemption so abundant that even the worst sinner who hears the gospel and willingly accepts its freely offered salvation can hope.

When people mourn over wrongdoing and tremble at God's word, we may hope they will abandon their sins.

Anyone seeking to move others toward godly sorrow or love for God must first be moved personally.

Ezra and the people made careful plans for the reform.

Decisions made in haste seldom produce lasting results.

## The reform is carried out (verses 15–44)

Even the best reformers can only make their best efforts.

When **the Redeemer comes to Zion**, he will effectively turn ungodliness away from Jacob.

When people truly repent and abandon sin, God forgives.

But **Christ's blood**, shed as our sin offering, is the only atonement that removes guilt.

An outward appearance of repentance or better behavior brings no lasting benefit if someone rejects Christ.

Self-reliance shows that the heart is still proud and unrepentant.

Every name written in **the book of life** belongs to a **repentant sinner**, not someone claiming self-righteousness and denying any need to repent.
