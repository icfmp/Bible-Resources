# Genesis 35 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| God commands Jacob to go to Beth-el, He puts away idols from his family. | 1–5 |
| Jacob builds an altar, Death of Deborah, God blesses Jacob. | 6–15 |
| Death of Rachel. | 16–20 |
| Reuben's crime, The death of Isaac. | 21–29 |

## Jacob returns to Bethel and removes the idols (verses 1–5)

Jacob had forgotten Bethel.

But God reminds those he loves of duties they have neglected. He does this in one way or another, through conscience or through his providence.

When we make a vow to God, we should not delay keeping it.

Yet it is better to fulfill it late than never.

Jacob told his household to prepare not only for the journey, but also for worship.

Those who lead households should use their authority to maintain the worship of God within their families (Joshua 24:15).

The family had to put away its foreign gods.

Even in homes that appear religious and have an altar to God, much may be wrong.

There may be more idols than anyone expects.

The people were told to make themselves clean and change their clothes.

These outward acts were signs of the cleansing and renewal that must take place within the heart.

What good are clean or new clothes if the heart itself is neither clean nor new?

If Jacob had demanded the removal of those idols earlier, his family would have given them up earlier.

Sometimes efforts at reform succeed far better than we thought possible.

Jacob buried the images.

We must separate ourselves completely from our sins, just as we are separated from those who have died and been buried out of sight.

Jacob then moved from Shechem to Bethel.

The Canaanites were deeply angry at Jacob's sons for their brutal treatment of the Shechemites.

But God's power restrained them, and they did not seize the opportunity to take revenge.

The path of duty is the path of safety.

When we are carrying out God's work, we are under his special protection.

God is with us while we are with him. And if he is for us, who can stand against us?

God governs the world through hidden fears in people's minds far more often than we realize.

## God renews his covenant with Jacob (verses 6–15)

The comfort believers receive through holy worship does not come chiefly from *Bethel*, the house of God.

It comes from *El-Bethel*, the God of that house.

Religious ordinances are empty if we do not meet God through them.

At Bethel, Jacob buried Deborah, Rebekah's nurse.

Her death was deeply mourned.

Long-serving members of a household who have been faithful and helpful deserve respect.

God appeared to Jacob and renewed his covenant with him.

“I am God Almighty”: God is completely sufficient, able to fulfill his promise at the proper time and to support and provide for Jacob in the meantime.

God promised Jacob two things.

He would become the father of a great nation, and he would possess a good land.

Both promises also had a spiritual meaning.

Jacob understood something of that meaning, although not as clearly or fully as we now can.

Christ is the promised Seed, and heaven is the promised land.

The promised Seed is the foundation of all God's favors, and the heavenly inheritance is their crowning completion.

## Rachel's death and Benjamin's birth (verses 16–20)

Rachel once cried out passionately, “Give me children, or I will die!”

Now, after bearing children, she died.

The death of the body is the soul's departure into the world of spirits.

When will we learn that God alone truly knows what is best for his people?

In all earthly concerns, the safest course for a Christian is to say sincerely, “It is the Lord; let him do what seems good to him.”

Our safety and comfort lie in submitting our wills to his.

As Rachel was dying, she named her newborn son *Ben-Oni*, “son of my sorrow.”

Many sons become a source of grief to the mothers who bore them.

Mothers already suffer enough sorrow on account of their children.

Those children should therefore grow up seeking to bring their mothers joy and, as far as possible, make up for some of that grief.

But Jacob did not want every mention of the boy's name to renew the sorrow of Rachel's death.

So he renamed him *Benjamin*, “son of my right hand.”

The name expressed how dear Benjamin was to him: he would be the support of Jacob's old age, like a staff held in his right hand.

## Reuben's sin and Isaac's death (verses 21–29)

The depth of the suffering caused by Reuben's sin is made clear in the brief words, “And Israel heard of it.”

Nothing more is said, but nothing more needs to be said.

Reuben probably thought that his father would never find out.

Yet those who count on keeping their sins secret are usually disappointed.

Scripture records Isaac's age and death, even though he did not die until after Joseph had been sold into Egypt.

Isaac lived for about forty years after he had made arrangements in expectation of his death (Genesis 27:2).

Putting our hearts and households in order early will not make us die even an hour sooner.

But it will help us die much better.

The account specifically mentions that Esau and Jacob were together in harmony at their father's funeral.

This shows how wonderfully God had changed Esau's disposition.

How terrible it is to see relatives quarreling over a little worldly property beside the graves of those they loved!

They do this even though they themselves are not far from the grave.

## Editorial verification — Genesis 35

The four distinct sections were compared after drafting against [STEP's complete CrossWire MHCC text for Genesis 35](https://www.stepbible.org/?q=version%3DMHCC@reference%3DGen.35). The published `MHCC 0.1.1` has no recorded wording correction in this chapter.

- **1–5:** neglected Bethel vow, reminders by conscience/providence, delayed fulfillment, household worship and **Joshua 24:15**, hidden idols despite outward religion, changed clothes as a symbol of inner renewal, possible earlier reform, burial/separation-from-sin image, Canaanites' anger over the **brutality** at Shechem, divine restraint and protection, fears secretly governing people — all retained.
- **6–15:** the **Bethel/El-Bethel** distinction and empty ordinances without God, Deborah's burial and faithful service, renewed covenant, Almighty's sufficiency for both present needs and future promises, nation **and** land, Jacob's only partial spiritual understanding, **Christ as promised Seed** and heaven as the promised land, foundation and crowning image — all retained.
- **16–20:** the irony of Rachel's earlier demand for children, physical death and the soul, God's superior wisdom and submission to his will, Ben-Oni's sorrow, maternal grief and children's duty to bring joy, Benjamin's right-hand/staff imagery and care in choosing his name — all retained.
- **21–29:** the weight of “Israel heard it,” failed expectation of secrecy, Isaac's actual death after Joseph's sale, the approximately **forty-year** interval since his death-related preparations and **Genesis 27:2**, timely spiritual/household preparation without hastening death, Esau and Jacob's reconciliation visible at the funeral, and the warning about inheritance quarrels at graves — all retained.

The four original range groupings and chapter-outline rows remain unchanged, and the author's doctrinal and historical claims have not been silently recast.
