## The war with Midian (verses 1–6)

People who carry out private revenge without a commission from God must one day answer to him.

The same is true of those who make war and devastate kingdoms because of ambition, greed or personal resentment.

But Henry distinguishes such unjust violence from an extraordinary judgment **expressly authorized by God**.

Instead of an earthquake, epidemic or famine, God may appoint people to carry out a sentence against a nation.

Henry maintains that ancient Israel could demonstrate such a divine commission in this case, whereas **no one today can claim one like it**.

Israel's wars of judgment were commanded directly by God and were accompanied by miraculous victories.

Henry understands these judgments as punishment of nations whose sin had reached its full measure. He argues that objections arise from underestimating the evil of sin, which God hates.

This is his explanation of the historical account—not permission for private vengeance or contemporary religious warfare.

## Balaam dies with Midian (verses 7–12)

Israel killed Midian's kings and Balaam.

God's providence had brought Balaam to the place where the judgment fell upon him.

If he had sincerely believed the words he had spoken about Israel's happiness, he would not have continued to associate with its enemies.

The wicked schemes by which Midian tempted Israel were Balaam's own projects.

It was just, Henry says, that he perish with those who had carried them out (Hosea 4:5).

The Israelites took women and children captive, burned the towns and strongholds, and returned to camp.

## Those held responsible for leading Israel into sin (verses 13–18)

The ordinary sword of war should spare women and children.

But Henry distinguishes this from what he considers the **execution of a divine judicial sentence**, which must distinguish between the guilty and the innocent rather than merely between adults and children.

He says that in this particular judgment the Midianite women who had led Israel into sin bore the greatest guilt.

The female children were spared and would be raised among the Israelites, where they would not lead them into idolatry.

The account illustrates the hateful nature of sin and the grave responsibility involved in drawing others into it.

Henry applies it to the Christian's struggle: we should avoid opportunities for evil and refuse to spare our own sinful desires.

He also distinguishes the ancient custom of keeping war captives as slaves from the intention to use them for sexual immorality. This reflects his description of a historical practice, not a new endorsement of slavery.

He observes that in national disasters brought by war, disease or famine, children can suffer as a result of their parents' wrongdoing.

Yet he adds that children who die before committing actual sin are provided for by God's mercy in Christ with respect to their eternal happiness.

## Purification after battle (verses 19–24)

Even though the war had been commanded by God and the fighters had committed no moral fault in obeying that command, they were required to undergo ceremonial purification.

They remained outside the camp for seven days.

This kept a strong sense of revulsion toward bloodshed alive in their minds.

The captured goods were also purified because they had previously been used by the Midianites.

## The distribution of the spoils (verses 25–47)

Whatever we possess, the Lord has a rightful claim upon a portion of it.

The people's share was assessed at **one part in fifty**, while the soldiers' share was assessed at **one part in five hundred**.

Henry draws a practical lesson from that difference.

People with fewer opportunities to honor God through direct personal service should be especially ready to give from their possessions.

## The officers' thanksgiving offerings (verses 48–54)

Israel's victory was remarkable: a small force overcame a much larger people.

Still more remarkable, no Israelite soldier was killed or missing.

The officers brought the gold captured in battle as an offering to the Lord.

In doing so, they acknowledged that even while serving God, they had no claim to a reward based on personal merit.

They needed forgiveness for anything sinful that had occurred.

They also wanted to express gratitude that their lives had been preserved, although they might justly have been taken away.
