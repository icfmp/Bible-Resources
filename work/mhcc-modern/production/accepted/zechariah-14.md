# Zechariah 14 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |

## Jerusalem's suffering and the coming of Christ (verses 1–7)

During his earthly life **Jesus often stood on the Mount of Olives**.

He ascended to heaven from that place, and afterward distress and desolation overtook the Jewish nation.

Henry presents this as **one figurative interpretation** of the vision.

But he also reports that **many understand the prophecy to concern events still unfulfilled**, including troubles beyond what we can now fully imagine.

He does not settle this dispute by declaring one reading certain.

Every believer can nevertheless look forward to **Christ's coming in power** with joy, since the Lord is their God.

For a long time, the church may be weakened and deformed by sin.

Truth and error, happiness and sorrow, may be mixed together.

The same mixture affects each believer: **grace and remaining corruption coexist**.

But when circumstances are darkest and hope seems most remote, God can bring light.

Deliverance may come just when his people have stopped expecting it.

## Living waters and divine judgments (verses 8–15)

Some understand the **living waters flowing from Jerusalem** as an image of the gospel spreading from that city.

Neither the gospel and its appointed means of grace nor the grace the Holy Spirit works through them can be exhausted.

Persecution's heat, temptation's storms and other painful experiences cannot stop their lasting benefit.

The passage appears also to announce severe judgments against those who oppose the settlement of **the Jews in their own land**.

**Henry insists that events alone can establish how far this language should be interpreted literally.**

Human rage and violence are only faint images of the hostility found among those lost in their sins.

Even animals can suffer because human beings sin and bring ruin on the world around them.

Through such judgments, God reveals his displeasure against wickedness.

## The holiness of ordinary life in the latter days (verses 16–21)

Henry considers it impossible for all the nations literally to travel to Jerusalem every year to celebrate one festival.

He therefore says this language **must be understood figuratively**.

The **Feast of Tabernacles** represents gospel worship.

Every day of a Christian's life is to be lived as a holy celebration.

**The Lord's Day**, in particular, should receive special devotion.

Those who neglect God's appointed means of grace cannot expect to receive the benefits of fellowship with him.

This neglect becomes its own punishment: by refusing the duty, they lose the privilege.

The passage promises a time of unusual peace and purity for the church.

Ordinary work and holy worship will be governed by **the same principles of faith, love and obedience**.

Holiness will spread more widely because the **Holy Spirit will be poured out more abundantly** than before.

Even common things will become holy through their use.

Every action and enjoyment of a believer should be directed toward God's glory.

Our whole life should become **one continuous sacrifice of devotion**, untouched by selfish motives.

How far the church now is from such purity!

But Henry anticipates days when God will reform and enlarge it.

Still, **perfect holiness and perfect happiness are found in heaven alone**.
