# Matthew 18 — Modern English

## Becoming humble like children (verses 1–6)

Jesus spoke often about his coming sufferings and only briefly about his glory.

Yet the disciples focused on glory and neglected the warnings about suffering.

Many people enjoy discussing religious privileges and honors but avoid thinking about duty and trouble.

Jesus placed a young child before them.

He solemnly taught that **unless they were converted and became like children, they could not enter his kingdom**.

Very young children do not normally seek authority or concern themselves with social distinctions.

They are generally free of deliberate malice, ready to be taught and willing to depend upon their parents.

Children soon begin displaying less admirable qualities, often encouraged by what others teach them.

Nevertheless, these traits of early childhood provide a fitting picture of **true Christian lowliness**.

We need our minds to be renewed every day so that we become simple, humble and willing to occupy the lowest place.

Let us examine ourselves carefully and frequently.

## Avoiding sin and caring for the little ones (verses 7–14)

Given Satan's cunning and malice and humanity's sinful weakness, occasions of temptation and offense will arise.

God permits them for wise and holy purposes, including revealing who is sincere.

Jesus has warned us about seducers, tempters, persecutors and harmful examples.

We should remain alert.

As far as we lawfully can, we must give up anything we cannot keep without becoming entangled in sin.

**Outward occasions of sin should be avoided**.

Living according to sinful desires leads to death.

But putting the body's sinful practices to death **through the Spirit** leads to life.

Christ came to save souls.

He will judge severely those who obstruct the spiritual progress of people moving toward heaven.

Should we then refuse to care for people whom the Son of God came to seek and save?

An affectionate father cares for all his children but has a particular tenderness toward the smallest.

So should our care extend to the vulnerable and spiritually immature.

## Correcting a brother or sister (verses 15–20)

When one professing Christian wrongs another, the injured person should not immediately spread the complaint among other people, especially when it rests on gossip.

Instead, go **privately** to the person responsible.

Describe the matter kindly and explain the offense.

With a genuine Christian, this would often bring reconciliation without further dispute.

The principles of Jesus' teaching can be followed in many different circumstances, yet Christians neglect them far too often.

How few try the very method Christ explicitly gave his disciples!

Throughout such dealings, pray for guidance.

Treasure God's promises.

When believers gather in **Christ's name**, whatever their number or location, they should recognize **his presence among them**.

## Forgiving from the heart (verses 21–35)

Our entire life depends upon God's mercy and forgiveness, yet we are often reluctant to forgive people who offend us.

Jesus' parable reveals how greatly sin offends the Lord and how unreasonable his servants can be.

It has **three principal movements**.

**First, the master's extraordinary mercy.**

The servant's debt pictures our sins, which we could never repay.

Being sold into slavery represents the judgment sin deserves.

A person under strong conviction of sin may foolishly imagine that enough personal effort could satisfy God's justice.

But **we cannot make payment for our own guilt**.

**Second, the servant's unreasonable cruelty toward his fellow servant.**

Although his master had shown him enormous mercy, he treated another person with harshness.

This does not mean wrongdoing against a neighbor is insignificant.

Such wrongdoing also offends God.

Yet we must not exaggerate offenses committed against ourselves or dwell upon revenge.

Bring complaints about wicked actions and human suffering to God and leave them with him.

**Third, the master condemns the servant's cruelty.**

The greatness of human sin displays the extraordinary riches of divine forgiveness.

A true understanding of being forgiven should incline us to forgive others.

**Henry explicitly rejects the idea that God genuinely pardons someone and afterward restores that forgiven person's guilt in order to condemn them.**

The servant's final judgment instead warns against the **false confidence of people who claim their sins are forgiven while their subsequent conduct shows they have not received the gospel's sanctifying grace**.

Real forgiveness of others must come **from the heart**.

Even that is not all: we should actively seek the welfare of those who have harmed us.

People who persist in merciless behavior despite bearing the name Christian will rightly face condemnation.

The humbled sinner looks entirely to **free, overflowing mercy obtained through Christ's death as our ransom**.

Let us ask God for renewing grace so that we forgive others as we hope to be forgiven.
