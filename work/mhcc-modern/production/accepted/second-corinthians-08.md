# 2 Corinthians 8 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The apostle reminds them of charitable contributions for the poor saints. | 1–6 |
| Enforces this by their gifts, and by the love and grace of Christ. | 7–9 |
| By the willingness they had shown to this good work. | 10–15 |
| He recommends Titus to them. | 16–24 |

## God's grace produces generous service (verses 1–6)

We must recognize the **grace of God as the source** of every genuinely good quality in us and every good deed we accomplish.

It is a great gift from God to be made useful to others and eager to participate in good work.

Paul praised the generosity of the Macedonian believers.

They did not have to be pressured to contribute. They actually **pleaded with Paul to accept their gift**.

Whatever we spend in God's service, we are merely returning to him something that already belongs to him.

But our contributions will not be accepted by God or truly benefit us unless we **first give ourselves to the Lord**.

Acknowledging divine grace as the source of good works both honors their true Giver and teaches us where to find the strength to perform them.

**Spiritual joy** expands the heart and moves it to loving service.

How different this is from people who refuse to join any good undertaking unless others press them repeatedly!

## Christ's generosity is our greatest example (verses 7–9)

**Faith is the root** of a life that pleases God (**Hebrews 11:6**).

Those who abound in faith should also grow in other graces and good works, especially those motivated by love.

People who talk the most are not always the most active in doing good.

But the Corinthians had already shown diligence in understanding and speaking about the faith, as well as in putting it into practice.

Paul urged them to add another grace to these virtues: **generous care for poor believers**.

The greatest reasons for fulfilling our Christian duties come from **the grace and love of Jesus Christ**.

Christ was rich because he was God, equal with the Father in power and glory.

Yet he not only became human for our sake; he also became **poor**.

He humbled himself to the point of offering his life upon the cross as a sacrifice to ransom our souls.

From what glory to what poverty our blessed Lord descended for us!

And through his poverty, what riches he has opened to us!

Our greatest happiness is to belong entirely to him and be available for his service.

## Finish the generous work you began (verses 10–15)

Good intentions resemble **buds and blossoms**: they are pleasant and give promise of fruit.

But if they produce no actual good deeds, their promise comes to nothing.

A good beginning is valuable, yet its benefit is lost without perseverance.

When people sincerely intend to do good and act **according to the resources and abilities they possess**, God does not reject them for what they cannot do.

But this does not excuse anyone who imagines that **good intentions or merely claiming willingness** is enough for salvation or for performing duty.

God's providence distributes earthly possessions unequally.

Some have much; others have little.

Those with abundance are therefore able to help people who lack necessities.

God intends that by supplying one another's needs, there should be **a kind of equality**.

Henry expressly rejects a forced **leveling that would destroy private ownership**; such a system, he argues, would remove the opportunity for voluntary generosity.

Everyone should feel responsible to help those in need.

Paul illustrates the point through the gathering and distribution of **manna in the wilderness** (**Exodus 16:18**).

In the end, even the wealthiest person needs only the ordinary necessities of life—food and clothing—while many who possess little still receive those necessities.

## Titus and trustworthy companions administer the collection (verses 16–24)

Paul recommended the brothers entrusted with receiving and carrying the gift so that the Corinthians would know whom they could trust.

Christians have a duty to act **prudently** and remove unnecessary grounds for suspicion.

It is essential to behave honestly **before God**.

But we should also take appropriate care to appear honest **before other people**, so that our integrity is visible and our usefulness is not hindered.

A clear public reputation and a pure conscience are both valuable for effective service.

The men commended by Paul glorified Christ through their work.

They were honored by him as faithful servants entrusted with his service.

When others place confidence in us, we should regard that confidence as a further reason to act well.
