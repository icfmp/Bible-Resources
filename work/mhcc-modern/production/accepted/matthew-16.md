# Matthew 16 — Modern English

## Original chapter outline

| Original outline | Verses |
| --- | --- |
| The Pharisees and Sadducees ask a sign. | 1–4 |
| Jesus cautions against the doctrine of the Pharisees. | 5–12 |
| Peter's testimony that Jesus was the Christ. | 13–20 |
| Christ foretells his sufferings, and rebukes Peter. | 21–23 |
| The necessity of self-denial. | 24–28 |

## The religious opponents demand a sign (verses 1–4)

The Pharisees and Sadducees differed in their beliefs and way of life.

Yet they united in opposing Jesus.

They wanted a miracle of **their own choosing**.

They disregarded the signs Christ had already given through his mercy toward the sick and suffering.

Instead they demanded some impressive display that would satisfy proud curiosity.

It is great hypocrisy to dismiss signs **God himself has appointed** while insisting that he give signs **we have invented**.

## Jesus warns against corrupt teaching (verses 5–12)

Christ used a picture drawn from ordinary life to teach a spiritual truth.

The disciples misunderstood his words as a warning about literal bread.

Jesus was displeased that they imagined he was as anxious about food as they were and that they understood so little of his usual manner of teaching.

At last they recognized what he meant.

Christ teaches through **the Spirit of wisdom working in the heart**, enabling us to understand **the revelation contained in the word**.

## Peter confesses Jesus as the Christ (verses 13–20)

Speaking for himself and the other disciples, Peter declared that Jesus is **the promised Messiah, the Son of the living God**.

They believed he was more than a mere human being.

Jesus called Peter blessed because **God had taught him** what many of his unbelieving countrymen did not know.

Christ had given him the name Peter, recalling stability or firmness in confessing the truth.

Henry observes that the Greek word translated **rock** is not identical to the name Peter, though the two are related in meaning.

Henry explicitly rejects the interpretation that **Peter's person is the foundation rock of the church**.

Rather, **Christ himself is the Rock**, the tested foundation.

Peter's confession is **the rock in respect of doctrine**.

If Jesus is not truly the Christ, those claiming to belong to him are deceived and cannot be his church.

Jesus then declared the authority Peter would exercise.

Since Peter spoke for his fellow apostles, **the authority also concerned them**.

The apostles could misjudge people's inward characters and could sin in their personal conduct.

But they were kept from error in faithfully announcing **the way of salvation and acceptance**, the rule of Christian obedience, the believer's character and experience, and the final judgment of unbelievers and hypocrites.

In those matters their declarations were right and confirmed in heaven.

Henry strongly rejects any human claim to possess independent power **to forgive sins or refuse forgiveness**.

**God alone forgives sin.**

In common Jewish speech, **binding and loosing** could mean forbidding and allowing, or declaring what conduct is lawful or unlawful.

## Jesus predicts suffering and corrects Peter (verses 21–23)

Christ revealed his purposes to the disciples **gradually**.

Only after they confessed him clearly as the Son of God did he begin teaching them directly about his approaching sufferings.

This corrected their mistaken expectation of a kingdom characterized by worldly splendor and political power.

Those who follow Christ should not expect great worldly honors.

Peter wanted Jesus to fear suffering as Peter feared it.

We are wrong to measure Christ's love and endurance by our own limitations.

Jesus responded to Peter with unusually strong indignation.

Anyone who draws us away from something good or persuades us to fear doing **too much for God** speaks in the language of Satan.

Every temptation to sin must be resisted firmly, not entertained through negotiation.

Those who refuse suffering for Christ's sake are valuing human interests above God's interests.

## The cross and the worth of the soul (verses 24–28)

A genuine disciple follows Christ **in duty now and into glory hereafter**.

Such a person walks in his path, is led by his Spirit and follows where Jesus goes.

**Deny yourself.** This is a difficult lesson, but our Lord both taught and practiced it in redeeming us.

**Take up your cross.** The cross represents the troubles God permits us to bear.

We often think another person's burden would be easier than our own.

But the task and trial appointed for us is the one we are called to bear faithfully.

We must not create suffering through our own recklessness or foolishness and then congratulate ourselves for carrying a cross.

Instead, when real suffering stands in the way of duty, we must accept it and follow Jesus.

Whoever wants the honor of being called a disciple must also perform **a disciple's work and duties**.

We readily understand that worldly possessions have little value compared with **bodily life**.

How much more do they fade in value compared with **the eternal soul**!

Thousands surrender their souls for trivial gains, momentary indulgence, or even simple laziness and neglect.

Whatever motivates someone to abandon Christ is, in effect, **the price for which Satan buys that soul**.

Yet **one human soul is worth more than the entire world**.

Jesus knows its worth because **he redeemed souls**.

He also knows the worth of the world because **he created it**.

A dying sinner cannot buy even one more hour in which to seek pardon.

Let us value our souls rightly and turn to Christ, **their only Savior**.
