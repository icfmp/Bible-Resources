# Job — Book introduction (Modern English candidate)

The book takes its name from **Job**, whose prosperity, suffering and restoration it records.

Henry places Job's lifetime soon after **Abraham**, or possibly before him.

He regards it as most likely that **Job himself wrote the book**, and calls it **the most ancient book in existence**.

These are the commentator's historical judgments; the text does not establish them as independently verified conclusions.

The lessons drawn from Job's patience and trials remain as necessary now as ever.

We live under the same divine Providence and the care of the same heavenly Father, who corrects his children to lead them toward righteousness.

Job showed considerable courage and patience, but even these weakened under severe suffering.

His faith, however, remained directed toward **his coming Redeemer**. That faith gave him endurance while every other support was tested and consumed, particularly the pride of **self-righteousness**.

Another great teaching displayed throughout the book is **divine Providence**.

The narrative shows that the Lord watched over Job with the affection of a wise and loving father.
